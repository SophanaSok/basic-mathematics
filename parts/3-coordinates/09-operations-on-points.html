Part III — Coordinate Geometry · Chapter 9

Operations on Points

Once points are pairs of numbers, we can do arithmetic on them. Two operations — adding two points, and multiplying a point by a number — turn out to have exact geometric meanings, and between them they carry most of the rest of this part.

By the end of this chapter you can compute $tP$, $P + Q$ and $Q - P$ in coordinates and say what each does geometrically, use the parallelogram law, recognise reflections and dilations as special cases of $tP$, distinguish a point from the located vector that joins two points, and recognise when two arrows represent the same displacement.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with coordinates (Chapter 8), with the standard mappings of the plane (Chapter 6), and with scaling (Chapter 7). This chapter does those same motions in arithmetic.

9.1 Dilations and reflections

The simplest operation is to multiply both coordinates of a point by the same number. For a point $P = (x,y)$ and a real number $t$ we define

$$tP = (tx,\; ty).$$

Chapter 6 called this a dilation, and described it as a motion. Now it is one line of arithmetic. Reading the definition in the two directions that matter:

What $tP$ does
  • $tP$ lies on the line through the origin and $P$ — for every $t$.
  • Its distance from the origin is $|t|$ times that of $P$. (Check: $\sqrt{(tx)^2 + (ty)^2} = |t|\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$.)
  • If $t > 0$, $tP$ is on the same side of the origin as $P$; if $t < 0$, on the opposite side.
  • $1P = P$, and $0P = O$, the origin.

Special values name familiar motions. $t = -1$ gives $-P = (-x,-y)$, the reflection through the origin — the $180°$ rotation of Chapter 6. And reflections in the axes, though not of the form $tP$, are just as easy in coordinates: $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ in the $x$-axis, and $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)$ in the $y$-axis.

Example 1. Scaling a triangle

A triangle has vertices $A = (1,2)$, $B = (4,1)$, $C = (2,5)$. Scale it by $t = 3$ about the origin and find the new side length $A'B'$.

  1. $A' = 3A = (3,6)$, $B' = 3B = (12,3)$, $C' = 3C = (6,15)$.
  2. Original $AB = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (1-2)^2} = \sqrt{9+1} = \sqrt{10}$.
  3. New: $A'B' = \sqrt{(12-3)^2 + (3-6)^2} = \sqrt{81+9} = \sqrt{90} = 3\sqrt{10}$.
  4. Exactly $3$ times the original, as the scaling law of Chapter 7 promised. The area, by the same law, is $9$ times as large. $\blacksquare$

9.2 Addition and the parallelogram law

The second operation adds two points coordinate by coordinate. For $P = (x_1,y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2,y_2)$,

$$P + Q = (x_1 + x_2,\; y_1 + y_2).$$

This is defined by fiat, and the definition looks unmotivated until you draw it. Then the picture is unmistakable.

The parallelogram law

If $O$, $P$, $Q$ are not on one line, then $P + Q$ is the fourth vertex of the parallelogram with vertices $O$, $P$, $Q$ — the one diagonally opposite the origin.

Addition of points inherits its properties directly from addition of numbers, since it is nothing but two copies of it. So $P + Q = Q + P$, and $(P+Q) + R = P + (Q+R)$, and $P + O = P$, and every $P$ has $-P$ with $P + (-P) = O$. Compare the list in Chapter 1: the same structure has reappeared, one dimension higher.

Why the parallelogram law is true

Let $P = (x_1,y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2,y_2)$. To get from $O$ to $P$ you move $x_1$ across and $y_1$ up. To get from $Q$ to $P+Q$ you move $(x_1+x_2) - x_2 = x_1$ across and $(y_1+y_2) - y_2 = y_1$ up — the same journey. So the side $Q\to P+Q$ is a copy of the side $O \to P$, shifted; the two are parallel and equal in length. The same argument applies to the other pair of sides, and a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel is a parallelogram. $\blacksquare$

Example 2. Completing a parallelogram

Three vertices of a parallelogram are $O = (0,0)$, $P = (5,1)$, $Q = (2,4)$. Where is the fourth?

  1. If the missing vertex $D$ is the one opposite $O$, the parallelogram law gives it directly: $D = P + Q = (7,5)$.
  2. But "the fourth vertex" is ambiguous — any one of the three given points could be the one opposite the gap, and each choice gives a different parallelogram.
  3. If $D$ is opposite $P$, then $O$ and $Q$ are the two vertices adjacent to both, and the diagonals share a midpoint: $\tfrac12(P + D) = \tfrac12(O + Q)$, so $D = O + Q - P = (2,4) - (5,1) = (-3,3)$.
  4. If $D$ is opposite $Q$, the same reasoning gives $D = O + P - Q = (3,-3)$.
  5. So there are three parallelograms: the fourth vertex is $(7,5)$, $(-3,3)$, or $(3,-3)$. $\blacksquare$

A question with three answers is not a trick; it is the honest answer. Naming the vertex you want — "opposite the origin" — removes the ambiguity.

9.3 Subtraction and located vectors

Subtraction is defined as you would expect, $Q - P = Q + (-P) = (x_2-x_1,\; y_2-y_1)$, but its meaning is worth a section of its own, because it is not the same kind of object as the points that made it.

Draw the arrow from $P$ to $Q$. To travel it you move $x_2 - x_1$ horizontally and $y_2 - y_1$ vertically. Those are exactly the coordinates of $Q - P$. So:

$Q - P$ is a displacement

$Q - P$ records how to get from $P$ to $Q$ — a direction and a distance — not a place. The arrow from $P$ to $Q$ is called the located vector $\overrightarrow{PQ}$, and two located vectors are said to be equivalent when they have the same $Q - P$.

Equivalent located vectors are parallel, point the same way, and have the same length: they are the same instruction carried out starting from different places. In particular, $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ is always equivalent to the arrow from $O$ to $Q - P$, which is why every displacement can be drawn from the origin if you prefer.

Point or displacement?

$(3,4)$ can mean "the place three right and four up from the origin" or "the instruction: go three right and four up". The coordinates are identical; the roles are not. Adding two places is a strange thing to do — what is Paris plus Rome? — while adding a displacement to a place is entirely natural, and is what $P + (Q-P) = Q$ says. Keep track of which you mean and a great deal of confusion never arises.

Example 3. Same displacement, different places

Let $P = (1,2)$, $Q = (4,6)$, $R = (-2,0)$, $S = (1,4)$. Are $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ and $\overrightarrow{RS}$ equivalent?

  1. $Q - P = (4-1,\; 6-2) = (3,4)$.
  2. $S - R = (1-(-2),\; 4-0) = (3,4)$.
  3. They agree, so the two arrows are equivalent: parallel, the same direction, and both of length $\sqrt{9+16} = 5$. $\blacksquare$

Note what this proves without further work: $PQSR$ has a pair of opposite sides that are parallel and equal, so it is a parallelogram. Checking equivalence of two displacements is the quickest parallelogram test there is.

Example 4. The midpoint, properly derived

Find the midpoint $M$ of the segment from $P$ to $Q$.

  1. Start at $P$ and travel half of the displacement to $Q$: $$M = P + \tfrac12 (Q - P).$$
  2. Expand using the arithmetic rules: $M = P + \tfrac12 Q - \tfrac12 P = \tfrac12 P + \tfrac12 Q$.
  3. In coordinates, $M = \left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\; \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ — the average of the coordinates.
  4. Sanity check: $M - P = \tfrac12(Q-P)$ and $Q - M = \tfrac12(Q-P)$, the same displacement, so $M$ really is halfway. $\blacksquare$

The step $P + \tfrac12 Q - \tfrac12 P = \tfrac12 P + \tfrac12 Q$ used commutativity and distributivity for points — which hold because they hold coordinate by coordinate for numbers. Chapter 1 is still doing the work.

Practice

Write points and displacements as pairs, like (3,-2).

Compute $(3,-2) + (4,3)$.

Answer: $(7,1)$

Coordinate by coordinate: $3 + 4 = 7$ and $-2 + 3 = 1$. Geometrically this is the fourth corner of the parallelogram whose other corners are the origin and the two given points.

With $P = (5,-2)$, compute $-2P$.

Answer: $(-10,4)$

$-2P = (-2\cdot5,\; -2\cdot(-2)) = (-10,4)$. It lies on the line through $O$ and $P$, on the far side of the origin, and twice as far from it.

With $P = (6,-1)$ and $Q = (1,5)$, compute $Q - P$.

Answer: $(-5,6)$

$Q - P = (1-6,\; 5-(-1)) = (-5,6)$: to get from $P$ to $Q$ you go five left and six up.

Subtracting the other way gives $P - Q = (5,-6)$ — the same journey in reverse, which is why the signs flip.

What is the midpoint of the segment from $(-3,7)$ to $(7,-1)$?

Answer: $(2,3)$

$\left(\frac{-3+7}{2}, \frac{7+(-1)}{2}\right) = (2,3)$.

Equivalently $M = P + \tfrac12(Q-P)$: here $Q - P = (10,-8)$, half of it is $(5,-4)$, and $(-3,7) + (5,-4) = (2,3)$ ✓.

Let $A=(0,0)$, $B=(2,5)$, $C=(6,1)$, $D=(8,6)$. Is $\overrightarrow{AB}$ equivalent to $\overrightarrow{CD}$?

  • Yes — both displacements are $(2,5)$.
  • No — they start at different points, so they cannot be equivalent.
  • No — the displacements are $(2,5)$ and $(2,-5)$.
  • Only if the four points form a square.

Answer: yes — both displacements are $(2,5)$

$B - A = (2,5)$ and $D - C = (8-6,\; 6-1) = (2,5)$. Equivalence is about the displacement, not the starting point — that is the entire point of the definition. So $ABDC$ is a parallelogram.

Starting at $(3,2)$, apply the displacement $(-7,-3)$. Where do you land?

Answer: $(-4,-1)$

$(3,2) + (-7,-3) = (3-7,\; 2-3) = (-4,-1)$. Adding a displacement to a point is translation — the first isometry of Chapter 6, now written as arithmetic.

How long is the located vector from $(2,-4)$ to $(7,8)$?

Answer: $13$

The displacement is $(5,12)$, and its length is $\sqrt{5^2+12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13$ — the distance formula applied to the difference.

$O$ is the origin and $P$, $Q$ are two points not on a line through $O$. What is $P + Q$?

  • The midpoint of $P$ and $Q$.
  • The point halfway between $O$ and $Q$.
  • The fourth vertex of the parallelogram $O$, $P$, $Q$, opposite the origin.
  • The reflection of $P$ in $Q$.

Answer: the fourth vertex of the parallelogram, opposite the origin

That is the parallelogram law. The midpoint would be $\tfrac12(P+Q)$ — the same point halved, which lies on the diagonal exactly halfway along it.

For any points $A$, $B$, $C$, what is $(B - A) + (C - B) + (A - C)$?

Answer: $(0,0)$, the origin

Every letter appears once positively and once negatively, so everything cancels: $$B - A + C - B + A - C = 0.$$

Geometrically: walk from $A$ to $B$ to $C$ and back to $A$. You are where you started, so the total displacement is zero — however the three points are placed.

The midpoint of the segment from $P = (-2,1)$ to $Q$ is $(1, 3.5)$. What is $Q$?

Answer: $(4,6)$

From $M = \tfrac12(P+Q)$ we get $Q = 2M - P$: $$2(1, 3.5) = (2,7), \qquad (2,7) - (-2,1) = (4,6).$$

Check: the midpoint of $(-2,1)$ and $(4,6)$ is $\left(\frac{-2+4}{2}, \frac{1+6}{2}\right) = (1, 3.5)$ ✓.

Recap

  • $tP = (tx,ty)$ keeps $P$ on its line through the origin, scaling its distance by $|t|$ and flipping sides when $t < 0$.
  • $P + Q$ adds coordinates, and lands on the fourth vertex of the parallelogram with corners $O$, $P$, $Q$.
  • Point addition obeys the same laws as number addition, because it is number addition, done twice.
  • $Q - P$ is a displacement, not a place: the arrow from $P$ to $Q$, and equivalent to any other arrow with the same difference.
  • The midpoint is $P + \tfrac12(Q-P) = \tfrac12(P+Q)$ — averaging coordinates, derived rather than remembered.
