Part III — Coordinate Geometry · Chapter 11

Trigonometry

Sine and cosine are usually introduced as ratios in a right triangle, which works until the angle exceeds $90°$ and then quietly stops. We define them instead as the coordinates of a point on the unit circle — true for every angle, and the definition everything else follows from.

By the end of this chapter you can convert between degrees and radians and explain why radians are the natural unit; give $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ for any angle from the unit circle; use $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; read period, amplitude, and shift off a graph or a formula; say where $\tan$ is undefined and why; and apply the addition formulas, including as a rotation of the plane.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with the equation of a circle (Chapter 8), slope and lines (Chapter 10), and rotation as a motion of the plane (Chapter 6). Sine and cosine are read straight off the unit circle, so that circle has to be familiar first.

11.1 Radian measure

Degrees are a convention: someone chose $360$. There is a measure that is not a convention at all. Put the angle at the centre of a circle of radius $1$ and measure the arc it cuts off. That length is the angle in radians.

Since the full circumference is $2\pi$ (Chapter 7), a full turn is $2\pi$ radians. Everything else follows by proportion:

Converting

$$360° = 2\pi \text{ rad}, \qquad 180° = \pi \text{ rad}, \qquad 1° = \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad}.$$ To go from degrees to radians multiply by $\pi/180$; the other way, multiply by $180/\pi$.

Worth memorising: $30° = \pi/6$, $45° = \pi/4$, $60° = \pi/3$, $90° = \pi/2$.

Why bother

In radians, arc length on a circle of radius $r$ is simply $s = r\theta$, and the area of the corresponding sector is $\tfrac12 r^2\theta$ — both formulas pick up an ugly $\pi/180$ if you insist on degrees. More importantly, every result in calculus about $\sin$ and $\cos$ is clean in radians and cluttered otherwise. Degrees survive for navigation and carpentry; radians are what the mathematics prefers.

11.2 Sine and cosine

Draw the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin. Start at the point $(1,0)$ and travel counterclockwise around the circle through an angle $\theta$. You arrive somewhere; call that point $(\cos\theta,\; \sin\theta)$.

Definition

For any real $\theta$, the point reached by turning through $\theta$ from $(1,0)$ on the unit circle is $$\bigl(\cos\theta,\; \sin\theta\bigr).$$ Cosine is the horizontal coordinate; sine is the vertical one.

Three things are immediate from the picture, with no calculation at all.

  • Both lie between $-1$ and $1$, since the circle does.
  • $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, because the point is on the circle $x^2+y^2=1$. This is the Pythagorean identity, and it is Pythagoras — the triangle with legs $|\cos\theta|$, $|\sin\theta|$ and hypotenuse $1$.
  • Both repeat every full turn: $\cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta$ and likewise for sine, since you end up at the same point.

For an acute angle in a right triangle the old definitions come back as a consequence. Scale the triangle so the hypotenuse is $1$ and drop it onto the unit circle: the adjacent side is $\cos\theta$ and the opposite side is $\sin\theta$. Undo the scaling and you get the usual "adjacent over hypotenuse, opposite over hypotenuse".

Example 1. Where the table comes from

Derive $\cos 60° = \tfrac12$ and $\sin 60° = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.

  1. Take an equilateral triangle of side $1$; all its angles are $60°$.
  2. Drop the perpendicular from one vertex. It bisects the opposite side, giving a right triangle with hypotenuse $1$, one leg $\tfrac12$, and angles $60°$ and $30°$.
  3. The leg of length $\tfrac12$ is adjacent to the $60°$ angle, so $\cos 60° = \tfrac12$.
  4. The other leg: $h^2 = 1 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$, so $h = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and $\sin 60° = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
  5. The same triangle read from the other angle gives $\cos 30° = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\sin 30° = \tfrac12$. $\blacksquare$

For $45°$, use the isosceles right triangle instead: legs $1,1$ and hypotenuse $\sqrt2$, so both values are $1/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2/2$. Two triangles generate the whole table.

11.3 The graphs

Unroll the circle. Plot $\theta$ horizontally and $\sin\theta$ vertically and you get a wave that rises to $1$ at $\pi/2$, returns to $0$ at $\pi$, falls to $-1$ at $3\pi/2$, and repeats forever with period $2\pi$. The cosine graph is the same wave shifted left by $\pi/2$, which is the identity $\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \pi/2)$.

Given constants $A$, $B$, $C$, the graph of $y = A\sin(Bx + C)$ is that wave, adjusted:

Reading $A\sin(Bx+C)$
  • Amplitude $|A|$: the height of the peaks. Negative $A$ also flips the wave.
  • Period $2\pi/|B|$: larger $B$ squeezes more waves into the same space.
  • Phase shift $-C/B$: the whole wave slides horizontally by that amount.

Two symmetries are worth naming, both visible in the circle. Replacing $\theta$ by $-\theta$ reflects the point in the $x$-axis, keeping the horizontal coordinate and flipping the vertical:

$$\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta, \qquad \sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta.$$

Cosine is an even function, sine an odd one — the vocabulary of Chapter 12, arriving early.

11.4 The tangent

Define

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta},$$

whenever $\cos\theta \neq 0$. Geometrically this is the slope of the ray from the origin at angle $\theta$: the ray passes through $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, so its rise over run is exactly $\sin\theta/\cos\theta$. Chapter 10's slope and this chapter's tangent are the same quantity, named twice.

Where it fails, and why that is correct

At $\theta = \pi/2$ the cosine is $0$ and $\tan\theta$ is undefined. That is not a defect of the formula but a fact about the picture: the ray is vertical, and a vertical line has no slope. As $\theta$ approaches $\pi/2$ from below the tangent runs off to $+\infty$; from above it comes back from $-\infty$. Hence the breaks in the graph.

Unlike sine and cosine, the tangent repeats every $\pi$, not every $2\pi$: turning by a half turn reverses the ray but leaves its slope unchanged.

11.5 Addition formulas

The central computational facts of the subject:

Addition formulas

$$\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta,$$ $$\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta.$$

Note that $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$ is emphatically not $\cos\alpha + \cos\beta$: try $\alpha=\beta=0$, which gives $1$ on the left and $2$ on the right. The mixing of the two functions in these formulas is the whole point, and §11.6 explains where it comes from.

Example 2. An exact value for $75°$

Compute $\cos 75°$ exactly.

  1. Write $75° = 45° + 30°$, both of which are in the table.
  2. $\cos 75° = \cos45°\cos30° - \sin45°\sin30°$.
  3. Substitute: $$\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12 = \frac{\sqrt6}{4} - \frac{\sqrt2}{4} = \frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}.$$
  4. Numerically: $(2.449 - 1.414)/4 \approx 0.259$, and $75°$ is close to $90°$ where the cosine is $0$, so a small positive value is right. $\blacksquare$

Example 3. The double-angle formulas

Put $\beta = \alpha$ in the addition formulas.

  1. $\sin 2\alpha = \sin\alpha\cos\alpha + \cos\alpha\sin\alpha = 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$.
  2. $\cos 2\alpha = \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha$.
  3. Using $\sin^2\alpha = 1 - \cos^2\alpha$, the second can be rewritten as $\cos 2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha - 1$, or as $1 - 2\sin^2\alpha$.
  4. These three forms of the same identity are all standard; which one to use depends on what you want to eliminate. $\blacksquare$

Nothing new was assumed here. Special cases of a general formula are free, and they are usually where the useful identities live.

11.6 Rotations

Chapter 6 described rotation as a motion and gave a formula without proof. We can now supply the proof — and in doing so, explain the addition formulas.

Example 4. Rotating a point

Rotate the point $P = (x,y)$ counterclockwise by an angle $\theta$ about the origin. Where does it go?

  1. Write $P$ in polar terms: let $r$ be its distance from the origin and $\alpha$ its angle, so $x = r\cos\alpha$ and $y = r\sin\alpha$.
  2. Rotating keeps the distance and adds $\theta$ to the angle. So the image is $\bigl(r\cos(\alpha+\theta),\; r\sin(\alpha+\theta)\bigr)$.
  3. Apply the addition formulas: $$r\cos(\alpha+\theta) = r\cos\alpha\cos\theta - r\sin\alpha\sin\theta = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,$$ $$r\sin(\alpha+\theta) = r\sin\alpha\cos\theta + r\cos\alpha\sin\theta = y\cos\theta + x\sin\theta.$$
  4. So rotation by $\theta$ is the mapping $$(x,y) \;\longmapsto\; (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\;\; x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta),$$ exactly the formula quoted in Chapter 6. $\blacksquare$
Which way round the logic goes

We used the addition formulas to derive the rotation formula, but the argument runs just as well backwards. Rotating $(1,0)$ by $\beta$ lands on $(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)$; rotating that by $\alpha$ must land on $(\cos(\alpha+\beta), \sin(\alpha+\beta))$, since two rotations compose into one. Writing out the second rotation in coordinates produces the addition formulas.

So the addition formulas are not a piece of algebraic trivia to be memorised. They are the statement that rotating by $\alpha$ and then by $\beta$ is the same as rotating by $\alpha+\beta$ — a fact about the plane, written in coordinates. Chapter 14 will write the same fact a third way, and it becomes a single multiplication.

Practice

Give exact values where the question asks for them; otherwise decimals to three places are fine. Write $\pi$ as pi.

Convert $60°$ to radians.

Answer: $\pi/3$

$60 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.047$. Sanity check: $\pi$ is half a turn, i.e. $180°$, and $60°$ is a third of that.

What is $\cos^2(37°) + \sin^2(37°)$?

Answer: $1$

The point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is on the unit circle $x^2+y^2 = 1$, so the sum is $1$ for every angle — no calculator needed, and $37°$ was a red herring.

What is $\cos(\pi/2)$?

Answer: $0$

A quarter turn from $(1,0)$ lands on $(0,1)$. The horizontal coordinate is the cosine, so $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$ — and the vertical one gives $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$.

This is also why $\tan(\pi/2)$ is undefined: its denominator is this $0$.

What is $\cos \pi$?

Answer: $-1$

Half a turn from $(1,0)$ lands on $(-1,0)$, so $\cos\pi = -1$ and $\sin\pi = 0$. Here the triangle definition is no help at all — there is no right triangle with a $180°$ angle — while the circle definition simply reads the answer off.

What is the period of $y = \sin(2x)$? Give it as a decimal.

Answer: $\pi \approx 3.142$

Period $= 2\pi/|B| = 2\pi/2 = \pi$. Doubling $B$ halves the period: the wave completes two full cycles in the space the plain sine needed for one.

What is the amplitude of $y = -5\cos(3x + 1)$?

Answer: $5$

The amplitude is $|A| = |-5| = 5$: the curve runs from $-5$ to $5$. The minus sign flips the wave upside down but does not change how tall it is.

For which of these is $\tan\theta$ undefined?

  • $\theta = 0$
  • $\theta = \pi/2$
  • $\theta = \pi$
  • $\theta = \pi/4$

Answer: $\theta = \pi/2$

$\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta$ fails exactly where $\cos\theta = 0$, which is $\pi/2$ plus any multiple of $\pi$. At $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi$ the cosine is $\pm1$ and the tangent is $0$; at $\pi/4$ both are $\sqrt2/2$ and the tangent is $1$.

Geometrically: the ray at $\pi/2$ is vertical, and vertical lines have no slope.

If $\sin\alpha = 0.6$ and $\cos\alpha = 0.8$, what is $\sin 2\alpha$?

Answer: $0.96$

$\sin2\alpha = 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha = 2(0.6)(0.8) = 0.96$.

The given values are consistent: $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$ ✓ — the $3,4,5$ triangle once more.

Rotate the point $(1,0)$ counterclockwise by $90°$ about the origin.

Answer: $(0,1)$

With $\theta = 90°$ we have $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 1$, so the formula gives $$(1\cdot0 - 0\cdot1,\;\; 1\cdot1 + 0\cdot0) = (0,1).$$

Which is also the definition at work: rotating $(1,0)$ by $\theta$ always lands on $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, here $(0,1)$.

Which of these is a correct identity?

  • $\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha + \cos\beta$
  • $\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$
  • $\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\sin\beta + \cos\alpha\cos\beta$
  • $\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$

Answer: $\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$

The first fails at $\alpha=\beta=0$: $1 \neq 2$. The second has the wrong sign — the cosine formula subtracts. The third fails at $\alpha=\beta=0$ too, giving $0$ on the left and $1$ on the right.

Substituting $0$ is the cheapest test there is, and it disposes of three of these four in a few seconds.

Mixed review: Part III

Eight problems pulled back across Chapters 8–11 — nothing new, just recall.

Find the distance between the points $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 6)$.

Answer: $5$

$\sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ — the distance formula from Chapter 8.

What is the radius of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 49$?

Answer: $7$

$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ is the circle of radius $r$ about the origin, and $\sqrt{49} = 7$ — the equation from Chapter 8.

Let $A = (2, 3)$ and $B = (5, -1)$. What is $A + B$?

Answer: $(7, 2)$

Add coordinate by coordinate: $(2+5,\ 3+(-1)) = (7, 2)$ — the parallelogram law from Chapter 9.

Reflect the point $(3, -2)$ across the $x$-axis. What are the new coordinates?

Answer: $(3, 2)$

Reflecting in the $x$-axis sends $(x, y)$ to $(x, -y)$, so $(3, -2) \mapsto (3, 2)$ — from Chapter 9.

What is the slope of the line through $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 10)$?

Answer: $4$

$\dfrac{10 - 2}{3 - 1} = \dfrac{8}{2} = 4$ — the slope formula from Chapter 10.

A line has slope $2$. What is the slope of a line perpendicular to it?

Answer: $-\dfrac12$

Perpendicular slopes satisfy $m_1 m_2 = -1$, so $2m_2 = -1$ gives $m_2 = -\tfrac12$ — from Chapter 10.

If $\cos\theta = \dfrac35$ and $\theta$ is in the first quadrant, what is $\sin\theta$?

Answer: $\dfrac45$

$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta = 1 - \tfrac{9}{25} = \tfrac{16}{25}$, and $\sin\theta$ is positive in the first quadrant, so $\sin\theta = \tfrac45$ — the identity from this chapter.

Simplify $\sin^2(40°) + \cos^2(40°)$.

Answer: $1$

The Pythagorean identity $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ holds for every $\theta$, so no table of values is needed — from this chapter.

Recap

  • A radian is arc length on the unit circle; $180° = \pi$ rad, and the formulas $s = r\theta$, $\tfrac12 r^2\theta$ only work in radians.
  • $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is the point reached by turning through $\theta$ from $(1,0)$ — a definition valid for every angle.
  • $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ is Pythagoras on the unit circle, and both functions repeat with period $2\pi$.
  • In $A\sin(Bx+C)$: amplitude $|A|$, period $2\pi/|B|$, shift $-C/B$.
  • $\tan\theta$ is the slope of the ray at angle $\theta$, undefined where that ray is vertical, with period $\pi$.
  • The addition formulas are the statement that two rotations compose into one; rotation by $\theta$ is $(x,y)\mapsto(x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\; x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)$.
