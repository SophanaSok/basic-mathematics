Skill packs · Pack F

Word Problems

Most of the algebra on a test is not an equation handed to you but a story that has to become one. This pack is about that step. It first says how to name the unknown, write the other quantities in terms of it and turn each fact of the story into an equation, with the phrases, ages and consecutive numbers that stories are built from. Then it takes the most common story of all, distance, rate and time, where one fact, $d = rt$, gives a row for each mover and one sentence ties the rows together: movers toward each other, a catch-up after a head start, and a round trip. The third section is about rates that combine, in work shared by two workers, a boat in a current and solutions or blends mixed together. The fourth is about checking an answer against the story, since an answer to the equation is not yet an answer to the question.

A puzzle to carry through the pack

A bat and a ball cost $\$1.10$ together. The bat costs $\$1$ more than the ball. What does the ball cost?

  • $10$ cents
  • $5$ cents
  • $15$ cents

The answer that comes first is the one most people give, and it can be tested against the story in a few seconds. It fails, and the reason is that two prices were never told apart. Giving one of them a letter forces that. The first section names the unknown and builds the equation. (The second section turns to movers and the one fact that ties distance, rate and time. The third takes up rates that combine, in work, currents and mixtures, and the fourth the checks that would have caught the quick answer.)

By the end of this pack you can name an unknown with its unit, write every other quantity in terms of it, write one equation for each fact of a story, solve it and answer the question that was asked, with units, checking against the story and not only against the equation. You can turn phrases such as “5 less than twice a number” into expressions, write consecutive integers and consecutive even or odd integers, and solve age and perimeter problems. For motion you can fill a row of rate, time and distance for each mover, tie the rows with the right sentence (the distances add to the gap, are equal, or the times add), change minutes to hours before multiplying, and find an average speed as total distance over total time. You can add the rates of two workers (and subtract a drain), add or take away a current from a boat's still-water speed, follow the pure amount through a mixture, and check an answer against every fact of the story, its units and kind, the question asked and an estimate.
Before you start

This pack leans on fractions and decimals as numbers (Chapter 1) and on solving an equation in one unknown (Chapter 2). Chapter 2 also has a section on turning words into equations, with ticket and drink-mix problems. This pack takes up other stories, and you can read it at any point alongside the course.

Three quick ones to wake up what the pack stands on. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution says where to look.

Evaluate $2(n + 3)$ when $n = 4$.

Answer: $14$

$4 + 3 = 7$, and $2 \cdot 7 = 14$. Check by spreading the $2$ first: $2 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 = 8 + 6 = 14$ ✓.

Solve $5x - 7 = 18$.

Answer: $x = 5$

Add $7$ to both sides to get $5x = 25$, then divide both sides by $5$. See §2.1. Check: $5 \cdot 5 - 7 = 18$ ✓.

How many hours is $45$ minutes? Give it as a decimal.

Answer: $0.75$ hours

$\dfrac{45}{60} = \dfrac{3}{4} = 0.75$. Check: $0.75 \cdot 60 = 45$ ✓. Rates in miles per hour need times in hours, so this change comes up in the second section.

F.1 Naming the unknown

Look at the answer the puzzle invites: the total is $\$1.10$, the bat is $\$1$ more, so the ball is the $10$ cents left over. Test it against the story. A ball of $10$ cents and a bat that costs a dollar more is a bat of $\$1.10$, and together they cost $\$1.20$. The quick answer subtracted the dollar from the total, but the dollar is the difference between the two prices and not one of the two parts of the total. A letter prevents the slip, because writing down what the letter stands for forces you to say which price is which.

The same four moves turn most stories into algebra.

  1. Name one unknown, with its unit. “Let $b$ be the price of the ball in dollars.” Choose the quantity that the others are built from, so that one letter does most of the work.
  2. Write every other quantity in terms of it. If the bat costs a dollar more, the bat is $b + 1$ dollars. Nothing new is unknown here, only a different way to say what you already named.
  3. Write one equation for each fact that is left. Each fact says two ways of writing the same amount are equal. The fact “the bat is a dollar more” was spent in the second move, and it cannot be used again; the fact “together they cost $\$1.10$” is still unused, and it is the equation.
  4. Solve, then answer the question that was asked. The letter may be a quantity the question does not ask for. Give the answer with its unit, and check it against the story. An equation written wrongly can be satisfied by a wrong answer, and only the story catches that.

Much of the work is translating phrases, and the order in which a phrase is said is not always the order in which it is written. “$5$ less than twice a number” starts from twice the number and takes $5$ away, so it is $2n - 5$, and the tempting $5 - 2n$ has the pieces backwards. Test a phrase with a number: for $n = 10$, twice the number is $20$ and $5$ less than that is $15$, and $2n - 5 = 15$ where $5 - 2n = -15$.

Example 1. An age problem

Maya is $12$ years older than her cousin Theo. In $6$ years she will be twice as old as he will be then. How old is Theo now?

  1. Let $T$ be Theo's age now, in years. Maya is $T + 12$.
  2. In $6$ years both ages grow by $6$: Theo is $T + 6$ and Maya is $T + 18$.
  3. The fact still unused is “then, Maya is twice Theo”: $T + 18 = 2(T + 6)$.
  4. $T + 18 = 2T + 12$, so $T = 6$. Theo is $6$ now, and Maya is $18$.
  5. Check against the story: in $6$ years they are $12$ and $24$, and $24 = 2 \cdot 12$ ✓. Check another way: the gap between their ages never changes, and it is $12$. When Maya is twice as old, the gap is equal to Theo's age, so Theo is then $12$, which is $6$ years from now, and so he is $6$ now ✓. $\blacksquare$

Every age moves by the same number of years, which is why the second move adds the same $6$ to both.

Write “$7$ less than three times a number $n$” as an expression in $n$.

Answer: $3n - 7$

Three times the number is $3n$, and $7$ less than that is $3n - 7$. Check with $n = 4$: three times $4$ is $12$, and $7$ less than $12$ is $5$; the expression gives $3 \cdot 4 - 7 = 5$ ✓.

Three consecutive integers add up to $78$. What is the largest of them?

Answer: $27$

$n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3n + 3 = 78$, so $3n = 75$ and $n = 25$. The integers are $25, 26, 27$, and the largest is $27$. Check: $25 + 26 + 27 = 78$ ✓.

Example 2. A perimeter, and the question that was asked

The length of a rectangular garden is $5$ meters less than three times its width, and its perimeter is $70$ meters. How long is the garden?

  1. Let $w$ be the width in meters. The length is “$5$ less than three times the width”: $3w - 5$.
  2. The perimeter is two widths and two lengths: $2w + 2(3w - 5) = 70$.
  3. $2w + 6w - 10 = 70$, so $8w = 80$ and $w = 10$.
  4. The question asks for the length, not the width: $3 \cdot 10 - 5 = 25$ meters.
  5. Check against the story: $25$ is $5$ less than $30$, which is three times $10$ ✓. Check another way: half the perimeter is $35$, and a width and a length are $10 + 25 = 35$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The letter names the quantity that makes the other easy to write, and the last step converts back to the quantity asked about.

A father is $24$ years older than his daughter. In $4$ years he will be three times as old as she will be. How old is the daughter now?

Answer: $8$ years

Let $d$ be her age now, so he is $d + 24$. In $4$ years: $d + 28 = 3(d + 4)$, which is $d + 28 = 3d + 12$, so $2d = 16$ and $d = 8$. Check: in $4$ years they are $12$ and $36$, and $36 = 3 \cdot 12$ ✓. Check another way: the gap $24$ is fixed, and when he is three times her age the gap is twice her age, so she is then $12$ and is $8$ now ✓.

F.2 Distance, rate and time

Distance, rate and time are one fact seen three ways. A rate says how much distance is covered in each unit of time (Pack D treats rates with their units), so a mover at rate $r$ covers $r$ in the first hour, another $r$ in the second, and after $t$ hours has covered $t$ copies of $r$: $$d = rt.$$ Everything in this section is that equation, written once for each mover. So draw one row for each mover with its rate, its time and its distance. Each row holds rate, time and distance; the story gives two of them, directly or in terms of one letter, and $d = rt$ fills the third. What turns the rows into an equation is one more sentence from the story, and three sentences cover most problems.

  • Toward each other from the two ends of a gap $D$: both have been moving for the same time when they meet, and between them they have covered the whole gap, so $d_1 + d_2 = D$.
  • A catch-up after a head start: when the second mover catches the first, both are at the same place, so $d_1 = d_2$. The times differ, because the first has been moving longer by the head start.
  • A round trip by the same road: out and back cover the same distance, so $d_{\text{out}} = d_{\text{back}}$, and the two times add up to the whole time.

The units must agree before you multiply. A rate in miles per hour wants a time in hours, so $45$ minutes is $\frac{45}{60} = 0.75$ hour. And an average speed is total distance over total time, because that is what “per” means. It is not the mean of the two speeds. Drive $60$ miles out at $30$ miles per hour and $60$ miles back at $60$: the legs take $2$ hours and $1$ hour, and the trip is $120$ miles in $3$ hours, an average of $40$ miles per hour. The mean of the speeds is $45$, and $120$ miles at $45$ miles per hour would take only $2\frac{2}{3}$ hours. The average is lower because more of the trip is spent at the slower speed.

Example 3. Two trains toward each other

Two stations are $420$ miles apart. A train leaves each at the same moment, running toward the other, one at $55$ miles per hour and the other at $65$ miles per hour. When do they meet, and how far from the first station?

One row for each train, with $t$ the hours until they meet
TrainRate (mph)Time (h)Distance (mi)
First$55$$t$$55t$
Second$65$$t$$65t$
  1. Toward each other, the distances add to the gap: $55t + 65t = 420$.
  2. $120t = 420$, so $t = 3.5$ hours.
  3. From the first station: $55 \cdot 3.5 = 192.5$ miles.
  4. Check against the story: the second train goes $65 \cdot 3.5 = 227.5$ miles, and $192.5 + 227.5 = 420$ ✓. Check another way: the trains close the gap at $55 + 65 = 120$ miles per hour, and $420 \div 120 = 3.5$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Both trains travel for the same time $t$, so one letter serves both rows.

Two hikers start at opposite ends of an $18$-mile trail and walk toward each other, one at $2$ miles per hour and the other at $4$ miles per hour. How many hours pass before they meet?

Answer: $3$ hours

$2t + 4t = 18$, so $6t = 18$ and $t = 3$. Check: in $3$ hours they walk $6$ and $12$ miles, and $6 + 12 = 18$ ✓.

Example 4. A catch-up after a head start

A plane leaves an airport at $320$ miles per hour. Forty-five minutes later a second plane leaves the same airport on the same route at $480$ miles per hour. How long after it leaves does the second plane catch the first, and how far from the airport?

One row for each plane, with $t$ the hours the second plane flies
PlaneRate (mph)Time (h)Distance (mi)
First$320$$t + 0.75$$320(t + 0.75)$
Second$480$$t$$480t$
  1. The head start in hours: $45$ minutes is $\frac{45}{60} = 0.75$ hour, so the first plane has flown $t + 0.75$ hours.
  2. At the catch-up the distances are equal: $320(t + 0.75) = 480t$.
  3. $320t + 240 = 480t$, so $240 = 160t$ and $t = 1.5$ hours.
  4. The distance is $480 \cdot 1.5 = 720$ miles.
  5. Check against the story: the first plane has flown $2.25$ hours, and $320 \cdot 2.25 = 720$ ✓. Check another way: in its head start the first plane gets $320 \cdot 0.75 = 240$ miles ahead, and the second gains $480 - 320 = 160$ miles each hour, so it needs $240 \div 160 = 1.5$ hours ✓. $\blacksquare$

The two times differ by the head start, and the two distances are equal. Those two facts make the equation.

A car leaves a town at $40$ miles per hour. Thirty minutes later a second car leaves the same town on the same road at $60$ miles per hour. How many hours after the second car leaves does it catch the first?

Answer: $1$ hour

With $t$ the hours the second car drives, $40(t + 0.5) = 60t$, so $20 = 20t$ and $t = 1$. Check: the first car has driven $1.5$ hours and gone $40 \cdot 1.5 = 60$ miles, and the second has gone $60 \cdot 1 = 60$ miles ✓.

A bus drives from a depot to a town at $40$ miles per hour and returns by the same road at $50$ miles per hour. The driving takes $4.5$ hours in all. How many miles is it from the depot to the town?

Answer: $100$ miles

$\dfrac{d}{40} + \dfrac{d}{50} = 4.5$. Multiply by $200$: $5d + 4d = 900$, so $9d = 900$ and $d = 100$. Check: $100 \div 40 = 2.5$ hours out and $100 \div 50 = 2$ hours back, and $2.5 + 2 = 4.5$ ✓.

F.3 Work, currents and mixtures

Three kinds of story, one idea: rates that combine. When two things act together, the quantity that adds is the rate, the amount done in one unit of time or carried in one unit of volume, and not the time or the percent.

Work. A painter who finishes a room alone in $a$ hours paints $\frac{1}{a}$ of it each hour. That fraction is the worker's rate. A second painter who takes $b$ hours alone paints $\frac{1}{b}$ of the room each hour, so the two together paint $\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}$ of it per hour. To find the time $t$ they take together, say what each has done when the work stops: in $t$ hours the first has done $\frac{t}{a}$ of the job and the second $\frac{t}{b}$, and between them they have done the whole job, which is $1$: $$\frac{t}{a} + \frac{t}{b} = 1.$$ Two answers tempt and both are wrong. Adding the times gives something longer than either painter alone, and averaging them gives something in between, yet two painters together finish sooner than the quicker one alone. So $t$ is less than the smaller of $a$ and $b$, a bound to test any answer against. A drain or a leak works against a fill, so its rate is subtracted: a hose that fills a pool in $a$ hours and a drain that empties it in $b$ hours leave $\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}$ of the pool per hour. The question can also run backward. If the pair takes $t$ hours and one worker alone takes $a$, the first has done $\frac{t}{a}$ of the job in that time, and the other has done the rest, $1 - \frac{t}{a}$.

Example 5. Two workers together

Kay can paint a room alone in $10$ hours, and Lou can paint it alone in $15$ hours. How long do they take working together?

  1. Let $t$ be the hours together. In $t$ hours Kay does $\frac{t}{10}$ of the room and Lou does $\frac{t}{15}$.
  2. Between them they do the whole room: $\frac{t}{10} + \frac{t}{15} = 1$. Multiply by $30$: $3t + 2t = 30$, so $t = 6$ hours.
  3. Against the bound: $6$ is less than $10$, the quicker painter's time alone, as it must be.
  4. Check against the story: in $6$ hours Kay does $\frac{6}{10} = \frac{3}{5}$ of the room and Lou does $\frac{6}{15} = \frac{2}{5}$, and $\frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5} = 1$ ✓. Check another way: the rates are $\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3}{30} + \frac{2}{30} = \frac{1}{6}$ of the room per hour, and a rate of $\frac{1}{6}$ finishes in $6$ hours ✓. $\blacksquare$

The times $10$ and $15$ are not added or averaged. The shares of the job are, because they are fractions of the same whole.

A newer copier finishes a print run alone in $4$ hours, and an older copier finishes the same run alone in $12$ hours. Run together, how many hours do they take?

Answer: $3$ hours

$\frac{t}{4} + \frac{t}{12} = 1$. Multiply by $12$: $3t + t = 12$, so $t = 3$. Check: in $3$ hours the newer copier does $\frac{3}{4}$ of the run and the older does $\frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}$, and they add to $1$ ✓. The answer is under $4$, the quicker copier's time alone ✓.

Currents. A river carries a boat along with it. A boat that makes $b$ miles per hour in still water, on a river that flows at $c$ miles per hour, goes $b + c$ miles per hour downstream and $b - c$ miles per hour upstream, because the water carries it. A plane in a wind is the same. So a current story is two rows of the distance, rate and time table, one for each direction, with the rates built from $b$ and $c$. A boat that makes $14$ miles per hour in a current of $2$ goes $16$ downstream and $12$ upstream, so $48$ miles takes $3$ hours with the current and $4$ hours against it. Run the other way, the two one-way speeds give the numbers behind them: they differ by $2c$ and add to $2b$, so the current is half their difference and the boat's still-water speed is half their sum. The upstream speed must be positive, so the current has to be less than $b$.

A boat makes $10$ miles per hour in still water on a river that flows at $2$ miles per hour. How many hours does the boat take to go $24$ miles upstream?

Answer: $3$ hours

Upstream the speed is $10 - 2 = 8$ miles per hour, and $24 \div 8 = 3$. Check: $8 \cdot 3 = 24$ ✓. Downstream the speed is $12$ and the trip takes $24 \div 12 = 2$ hours, less than upstream, as it should be with the current helping.

Mixtures. Track the pure stuff and not the percents. Then $x$ liters of a $20\%$ solution hold $0.2x$ liters of acid (the percent of a quantity is where this began). When solutions are poured together the liters add and the acid adds, so the mixture's percent is the total acid over the total liters. It is never the mean of the two percents unless the amounts are equal: $1$ liter of a $10\%$ solution and $3$ liters of a $50\%$ solution hold $0.1 + 1.5 = 1.6$ liters of acid in $4$ liters, which is $40\%$, and the mean of $10$ and $50$ is $30$. A table with a row for each solution (liters, percent, pure liters) and a total row keeps the bookkeeping. The same table does a price mixture: pounds of nuts at a price per pound give dollars, the weights add and the dollars add, and the blend's price per pound is the total dollars over the total weight.

How many liters of a $50\%$ acid solution must be added to $10$ liters of a $20\%$ acid solution to make a $40\%$ acid solution?

  2. $2 + 0.5x = 4 + 0.4x$, so $0.1x = 2$ and $x = 20$ liters.

A lab mixes $12$ liters of a $20\%$ salt solution with $4$ liters of a $60\%$ salt solution. What percent salt is the mixture? Give the percent without the percent sign.

Answer: $30\%$

The salt is $0.2 \cdot 12 = 2.4$ and $0.6 \cdot 4 = 2.4$ liters, in all $4.8$ liters, in $12 + 4 = 16$ liters of mixture, and $\frac{4.8}{16} = 0.3$. Check: the mean of the percents is $40$, which is too high because there is more of the weak solution. Another way: the weak solution is $\frac{12}{16}$ of the mixture, so the percent is $20 + \frac{4}{16} \cdot 40 = 30$ ✓.

F.4 Checking an answer against the story

A solution of the equation is not yet an answer. The equation may have been written wrongly, the letter may stand for a different quantity than the one asked about, or the arithmetic may have produced a number the story cannot use. Five checks catch these, and each is argued from a failure it catches. The naming procedure of turning words into equations is where this began, and the checks come after the solving.

  • Against the story, not the equation. An equation can be written wrongly and still be solved rightly. The bat-and-ball guess of $10$ cents satisfied “a dollar more” and failed “together $\$1.10$.” So test the number against every fact of the story in the story's own words, including the fact that was used to name the other quantities.
  • Against the units and the kind of thing. A count of buses is a whole number, a length is positive, an age is under about $110$, and a cyclist does not ride at $60$ miles per hour. A fractional count means the story needs rounding by its own logic: vans needed round up, vans that can be filled round down. A negative length means the setup was wrong and not the arithmetic. The answer of a work problem is less than each time alone, a mixture's percent lies between the two percents, and a meeting time is less than the gap over the slower speed.
  • Against the question that was asked. The letter often stands for the quantity that was easiest to name, and the question asks for the other one. The puzzle asked for the ball, and a story about a sum and a difference may ask for the larger piece. Reread the question after solving.
  • An estimate first. Round the numbers and solve in your head before the algebra. A result far from the estimate is a signal to look again.
  • Working back from a proposed answer is a legitimate check, and a legitimate way to find the equation: a guess row (the guess, each quantity computed from it, and the total it gives) shows the structure of the equation. The amount by which the total misses is what a better guess must remove.

Example 7. The wrong quantity, caught by the question

A board $140$ inches long is cut into two pieces, and one piece is $30$ inches longer than the other. How long is the longer piece?

  1. Let $s$ be the length of the shorter piece, in inches. The longer piece is $s + 30$.
  2. The two pieces make the board: $s + (s + 30) = 140$, so $2s = 110$ and $s = 55$. The first “answer” is $55$ inches.
  3. Reread the question. It asks for the longer piece, and $55$ is the shorter one, which is what the letter stands for. The longer piece is $55 + 30 = 85$ inches.
  4. Check against each fact: $55 + 85 = 140$, so the pieces make the board ✓, and $85 - 55 = 30$, so one is $30$ longer ✓. Check another way: the longer piece is half the board plus half the difference, $70 + 15 = 85$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The equation was written and solved correctly, and its solution was still not the answer. Only rereading the question showed it.

A plan charges a set-up fee of $\$25$ plus $\$7$ per month, and the total paid was $\$102$. A student solves the equation and says the answer is $10$ months. By how many dollars does the cost of $10$ months miss $\$102$?

Answer: $7$ dollars

The cost of $10$ months is $25 + 7 \cdot 10 = 95$ dollars, and $102 - 95 = 7$. Check: the true answer is $11$ months, since $25 + 7 \cdot 11 = 102$, so the student is one month off, and one month is $7$ dollars ✓.

Two numbers add to $64$ and differ by $10$. A student answers $32$ and $22$. Which statement about this answer is true?

  • The numbers do not differ by $10$.
  • One of the numbers is not positive.
  • The numbers do not add to $64$.
  • Both facts of the story hold.

Answer: the numbers do not add to $64$

$32 + 22 = 54$, not $64$, so the fact about the sum is broken, though $32 - 22 = 10$ holds. The pair that fits both facts is $27$ and $37$: $27 + 37 = 64$ and $37 - 27 = 10$ ✓.

Example 8. A fraction resolved by the story's logic

A school takes $130$ students on a trip in vans that each seat $12$. How many vans does it need?

  1. Estimate first. $130$ is a little over $120$, and $120 \div 12 = 10$, so the answer should be a little over $10$ vans.
  2. With $v$ vans the seats are $12v$, and they must cover the students: $12v \ge 130$, so $v \ge 10.83$.
  3. A part of a van cannot be hired. Ten vans seat $120$ and leave $10$ students without a seat, so the count rounds up to $11$ vans.
  4. Check against the story: $11 \cdot 12 = 132$ seats, enough for $130$ ✓, and $10 \cdot 12 = 120$, not enough ✓. Another way: $130 = 12 \cdot 10 + 10$, so ten vans fill and the $10$ left over need one more ✓. The estimate agrees. $\blacksquare$

Had the question asked how many vans can be completely filled, the same division would round down, to $10$. The story decides which way.

A class of $30$ students has $4$ more girls than boys. Let $b$ be the number of boys. How many girls are in the class?

Answer: $17$ girls

The girls number $b + 4$, and $b + (b + 4) = 30$ gives $2b = 26$ and $b = 13$. The question asks for the girls, not the boys: $13 + 4 = 17$. Check: $13 + 17 = 30$ and $17 - 13 = 4$ ✓.

Practice

Give each answer as a number in the unit the question names, without writing the unit. Use a decimal, such as $4.5$, where the answer is not whole. Where a question asks for an expression, use the letter it names. Money is in dollars and times are in hours unless the question says minutes. An equation is never typed as an answer: a question about one asks for one choice among the options shown, and so does a question about which fact is broken or which quantity was asked for.

A bicycle costs $\$40$ more than twice the price of a helmet. Together they cost $\$355$. With $c$ the price of the helmet in dollars, which equation fits the story?

  • $2c + 40 = 355$
  • $c + 2c + 40 = 355$
  • $c + 2(c + 40) = 355$
  • $c + 2c = 355 + 40$

Answer: $c + 2c + 40 = 355$

The bicycle is $2c + 40$ dollars, and the total of helmet and bicycle is $c + (2c + 40)$. The first option leaves out the helmet. The third doubles the sum $c + 40$, which would make the bicycle $2c + 80$. The fourth moves the $40$ to the wrong side. Check: the chosen equation gives $3c = 315$, so $c = 105$ and the bicycle is $250$; then $105 + 250 = 355$ and $250 = 2 \cdot 105 + 40$ ✓.

A number $n$ is increased by $9$, and the result is doubled. Write that as an expression in $n$.

Answer: $2(n + 9)$

The increased number is $n + 9$, and the doubled result is $2(n + 9)$, which is also $2n + 18$. Check with $n = 1$: $1 + 9 = 10$ and $10$ doubled is $20$; both forms give $20$ ✓.

Pat is $5$ years older than Sam, and Sam is $s$ years old now. Write the sum of their ages in terms of $s$.

Answer: $2s + 5$

Pat is $s + 5$, so the sum is $s + (s + 5) = 2s + 5$. Check with $s = 10$: the ages are $10$ and $15$, which add to $25$, and $2 \cdot 10 + 5 = 25$ ✓.

Three consecutive even integers add up to $114$. What is the smallest of them?

Answer: $36$

$n + (n + 2) + (n + 4) = 3n + 6 = 114$, so $3n = 108$ and $n = 36$. The integers are $36, 38, 40$. Check: $36 + 38 + 40 = 114$ ✓, and the middle one is the average, $114 \div 3 = 38$ ✓.

A father is $4$ times as old as his son. In $5$ years he will be $3$ times as old as his son will be then. How old is the son now?

Answer: $10$ years

With $s$ the son's age, the father is $4s$. In $5$ years: $4s + 5 = 3(s + 5)$, which is $4s + 5 = 3s + 15$, so $s = 10$. Check: the ages now are $10$ and $40$, in $5$ years $15$ and $45$, and $45 = 3 \cdot 15$ ✓. Check another way: the gap is $30$, and when the father is $3$ times the son the gap is twice the son's age, so the son is then $15$ and is $10$ now ✓.

A triangle has a first side, a second side that is $3$ centimeters longer than the first, and a third side that is twice as long as the first. Its perimeter is $47$ centimeters. How long is the third side?

Answer: $22$ centimeters

With $s$ the first side, $s + (s + 3) + 2s = 4s + 3 = 47$, so $4s = 44$ and $s = 11$. The third side is $2 \cdot 11 = 22$. Check: the sides are $11, 14, 22$ and $11 + 14 + 22 = 47$ ✓. They also form a triangle, since $11 + 14 = 25$ is more than $22$ ✓.

Ana, Ben and Cal share a $\$87$ bill. Ana pays $\$12$ more than Ben, and Cal pays half of what Ben pays. How many dollars does Ana pay?

Answer: $42$ dollars

With $b$ what Ben pays, $b + (b + 12) + \frac{b}{2} = 87$, so $2.5b + 12 = 87$ and $2.5b = 75$, which gives $b = 30$. Ana pays $30 + 12 = 42$. Check: Cal pays $15$, and $42 + 30 + 15 = 87$ ✓.

Two trucks start $400$ miles apart and drive toward each other, one at $45$ miles per hour and the other at $35$ miles per hour. With $t$ the hours until they meet, which equation fits the story?

  • $45t - 35t = 400$
  • $45t + 35t = 400$
  • $45 + 35 = 400t$
  • $45t = 400$

Answer: $45t + 35t = 400$

The trucks drive for the same $t$ hours, so their distances are $45t$ and $35t$, and together they cover the gap of $400$ miles. The first option would be the gap for trucks moving in the same direction, and the last uses one truck alone. Check: the chosen equation gives $80t = 400$, $t = 5$, and $225 + 175 = 400$ ✓.

Two airports are $1{,}400$ miles apart. A plane leaves each at the same moment, flying toward the other, one at $350$ miles per hour and the other at $450$ miles per hour. How many hours pass before the planes meet?

Answer: $1.75$ hours

$350t + 450t = 1400$, so $800t = 1400$ and $t = 1.75$. Check: in $1.75$ hours the planes fly $350 \cdot 1.75 = 612.5$ and $450 \cdot 1.75 = 787.5$ miles, and $612.5 + 787.5 = 1400$ ✓.

Two stations are $480$ miles apart. A train leaves each at the same moment, running toward the other, one at $45$ miles per hour and the other at $75$ miles per hour. How many miles from the slower train's station do they meet?

Answer: $180$ miles

$45t + 75t = 480$, so $120t = 480$ and $t = 4$ hours. The slower train has gone $45 \cdot 4 = 180$ miles. Check: the faster train has gone $75 \cdot 4 = 300$ miles, and $180 + 300 = 480$ ✓.

A freight train leaves a yard at $40$ miles per hour. Two hours later an express leaves the same yard on the same track at $56$ miles per hour. How many miles from the yard does the express catch the freight train?

Answer: $280$ miles

With $t$ the hours the express runs, $40(t + 2) = 56t$, so $80 = 16t$ and $t = 5$. The express has gone $56 \cdot 5 = 280$ miles. Check: the freight train has run $7$ hours and gone $40 \cdot 7 = 280$ miles ✓.

A boat leaves a dock at $12$ miles per hour. Twenty minutes later a second boat leaves the same dock by the same route at $18$ miles per hour. After how many minutes, counted from the second boat's departure, does the second boat catch the first?

Answer: $40$ minutes

Twenty minutes is $\frac{1}{3}$ hour. With $t$ the hours the second boat travels, $12\left(t + \frac{1}{3}\right) = 18t$, so $12t + 4 = 18t$, $4 = 6t$ and $t = \frac{2}{3}$ hour, which is $\frac{2}{3} \cdot 60 = 40$ minutes. Check: the first boat has traveled $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = 1$ hour and gone $12$ miles, and the second has gone $18 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 12$ miles ✓.

A car drives from a town to a lake at $30$ miles per hour and returns by the same road at $45$ miles per hour. The round trip takes $5$ hours of driving. How many miles is it from the town to the lake?

Answer: $90$ miles

$\dfrac{d}{30} + \dfrac{d}{45} = 5$. Multiply by $90$: $3d + 2d = 450$, so $5d = 450$ and $d = 90$. Check: $90 \div 30 = 3$ hours out and $90 \div 45 = 2$ hours back, and $3 + 2 = 5$ ✓.

A plane flies from city A to city B at $300$ miles per hour and returns at $450$ miles per hour. What is its average speed for the whole round trip, in miles per hour?

Answer: $360$ miles per hour

Take a one-way distance of $900$ miles (any distance gives the same average). Out: $900 \div 300 = 3$ hours. Back: $900 \div 450 = 2$ hours. The trip is $1800$ miles in $5$ hours, so the average is $\frac{1800}{5} = 360$. Check with another distance, $450$ miles: $1.5 + 1 = 2.5$ hours for $900$ miles, and $900 \div 2.5 = 360$ ✓. It is not the mean of the speeds, $375$.

Jo can paint a fence alone in $18$ hours, and Kim can paint it alone in $36$ hours. Working together, how many hours do they take?

Answer: $12$ hours

$\frac{t}{18} + \frac{t}{36} = 1$. Multiply by $36$: $2t + t = 36$, so $3t = 36$ and $t = 12$. Check: in $12$ hours Jo does $\frac{12}{18} = \frac{2}{3}$ of the fence and Kim does $\frac{12}{36} = \frac{1}{3}$, and $\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1$ ✓. The answer is under $18$, the quicker painter's time alone ✓.

Two pipes open together fill a pool in $12$ hours. The larger pipe alone fills it in $20$ hours. How many hours does the smaller pipe take alone?

Answer: $30$ hours

Let $x$ be the hours the smaller pipe takes alone. In $12$ hours the larger pipe fills $\frac{12}{20} = \frac{3}{5}$ of the pool, so $\frac{12}{x} = 1 - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{2}{5}$ and $x = 30$. Check: in $12$ hours the smaller pipe fills $\frac{12}{30} = \frac{2}{5}$, and $\frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5} = 1$ ✓. The pair's $12$ hours is under both times alone ✓.

A plane cruises at $320$ miles per hour in still air. Flying into a headwind of $40$ miles per hour, how many hours does a trip of $1{,}260$ miles take?

Answer: $4.5$ hours

Into the wind the speed over the ground is $320 - 40 = 280$ miles per hour, and $1260 \div 280 = 4.5$. Check: $280 \cdot 4.5 = 1260$ ✓. With a tailwind the speed would be $360$ and the trip $1260 \div 360 = 3.5$ hours, less than the headwind's, as it should be.

A boat goes $60$ miles downstream in $4$ hours and returns the same $60$ miles upstream in $6$ hours. What is the speed of the current, in miles per hour?

Answer: $2.5$ miles per hour

Downstream the speed is $60 \div 4 = 15$ and upstream it is $60 \div 6 = 10$. They differ by two currents: $15 - 10 = 5$, so the current is $2.5$ miles per hour. Check: the boat's speed in still water is $\frac{15 + 10}{2} = 12.5$, and $12.5 + 2.5 = 15$, $12.5 - 2.5 = 10$, with $15 \cdot 4 = 60$ and $10 \cdot 6 = 60$ ✓.

A chemist has $24$ liters of a $15\%$ salt solution. How many liters of a $55\%$ salt solution must be added to make a $25\%$ salt solution?

Answer: $8$ liters

The first solution holds $0.15 \cdot 24 = 3.6$ liters of salt. With $x$ liters added, $3.6 + 0.55x = 0.25(24 + x) = 6 + 0.25x$, so $0.3x = 2.4$ and $x = 8$. Check: the salt is $3.6 + 0.55 \cdot 8 = 3.6 + 4.4 = 8$ liters, and the mixture is $32$ liters, with $0.25 \cdot 32 = 8$ ✓. And $25$ lies between $15$ and $55$ ✓.

A shop mixes coffee beans that cost $\$3$ per pound with beans that cost $\$9$ per pound to make $48$ pounds of a blend worth $\$5$ per pound. How many pounds of the $\$9$ beans are needed?

Answer: $16$ pounds of the $\$9$ beans

With $x$ pounds of the $\$9$ beans there are $48 - x$ pounds of the $\$3$ beans, and the dollars add: $3(48 - x) + 9x = 5 \cdot 48$, so $144 + 6x = 240$ and $x = 16$. Check: $32$ pounds of the cheaper beans at $\$3$ per pound cost $\$96$ and $16$ pounds of the dearer at $\$9$ per pound cost $\$144$, in all $\$240$, and $48$ pounds of blend at $\$5$ per pound cost $\$240$ ✓.

A hose fills a pool alone in $6$ hours, and a drain empties the full pool alone in $10$ hours. With both open on an empty pool, let $t$ be the hours until the pool is full. Which equation fits the story?

  • $\dfrac{t}{6} + \dfrac{t}{10} = 1$
  • $\dfrac{t}{6} - \dfrac{t}{10} = 1$
  • $6t - 10t = 1$
  • $\dfrac{t}{6} - \dfrac{t}{10} = 0$

Answer: $\dfrac{t}{6} - \dfrac{t}{10} = 1$

In $t$ hours the hose adds $\frac{t}{6}$ of the pool and the drain removes $\frac{t}{10}$, and the net must be one whole pool. The first option lets the drain add water. The third has the times in place of the rates, and the fourth ends with an empty pool. Check: the chosen equation gives $\frac{t}{15} = 1$, so $t = 15$; in $15$ hours the hose adds $2.5$ pools and the drain removes $1.5$, and $2.5 - 1.5 = 1$ ✓.

A student mixes $6$ liters of a $15\%$ acid solution with $10$ liters of a $35\%$ acid solution and reports that the mixture is $45\%$ acid. Which statement is a reason to reject the report?

  • A percent in a mixture must be a whole number.
  • A mixture's percent lies between the two percents, and $45\%$ is above both.
  • The liters were added, and they should have been multiplied.
  • The mixture must have the same percent as the larger amount.

Answer: a mixture's percent lies between the two percents, and $45\%$ is above both

The mixture is made only of $15\%$ and $35\%$ solutions, so it cannot be richer than the richer one. The first reason is false, since a mixture's percent may be a decimal. Liters do add, which rules out the third, and the fourth is false too. Check: the true percent is $\frac{0.9 + 3.5}{16} = 0.275$, which is $27.5\%$, between $15\%$ and $35\%$ and closer to $35\%$, because there is more of that solution ✓.

Ana is $6$ years older than Ben, and Ben is $b$ years old now. The question asks how old Ana will be in $4$ years. Which expression gives it?

  • $b + 4$
  • $b + 6$
  • $b + 10$
  • $b + 2$

Answer: $b + 10$

Ana is $b + 6$ now, and in $4$ years she is $b + 6 + 4 = b + 10$. The first option is Ben's age in $4$ years, the second is Ana's age now, and the fourth takes the $4$ years away. Check with $b = 10$: Ana is $16$ now and $20$ in $4$ years, and $b + 10 = 20$ ✓.

A gym charges a joining fee of $\$30$ plus $\$14$ per month, and a member paid $\$198$ in all. A friend solves the equation and says the answer is $11$ months. By how many dollars does the cost of $11$ months miss $\$198$?

Answer: $14$ dollars

The cost of $11$ months is $30 + 14 \cdot 11 = 184$ dollars, and $198 - 184 = 14$. Check: the true answer is $12$ months, since $30 + 14 \cdot 12 = 198$, so the friend is one month off, and one month is $\$14$ ✓.

A hall has round tables that each seat $8$ guests. How many tables are needed for $150$ guests?

Answer: $19$ tables

$150 \div 8 = 18.75$. Eighteen tables seat $144$ and leave $6$ guests standing, so the count rounds up to $19$. Check: $19 \cdot 8 = 152$ seats are enough, and $18 \cdot 8 = 144$ are not ✓. The estimate agrees: $150 \div 8$ is about $19$.

A baker has $200$ muffins and packs them in boxes that each hold $9$. How many full boxes can the baker fill?

Answer: $22$ boxes

$200 \div 9 = 22.2$, and only whole boxes count, so the answer rounds down to $22$. Check: $22 \cdot 9 = 198$ muffins fill $22$ boxes, leaving $2$, and a $23$rd box would need $207$, more than the baker has ✓.

A board $85$ centimeters long is cut into two pieces, and the longer piece is $4$ times as long as the shorter. Let $s$ be the length of the shorter piece in centimeters. How long is the longer piece, in centimeters?

Answer: $68$ centimeters

The longer piece is $4s$, and $s + 4s = 85$ gives $5s = 85$ and $s = 17$. The letter is the shorter piece; the longer is $4 \cdot 17 = 68$. Check: $17 + 68 = 85$ and $68 \div 17 = 4$ ✓.

One pipe fills a pool alone in $9$ hours, and another fills it alone in $15$ hours. Open together, the two pipes take $t$ hours. Before solving, the time $t$ must be under what number of hours?

Answer: $9$ hours

Together the pipes fill faster than the quicker pipe alone, so $t$ is under the smaller time, $9$ hours. Neither the sum, $24$, nor the mean, $12$, can be the answer. Check by solving: the rates are $\frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{8}{45}$ of the pool per hour, so $t = \frac{45}{8} = 5.625$, which is under $9$ ✓.
