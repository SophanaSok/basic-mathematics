Units and Measurement

A number alone does not say how much. Five what? This pack treats the unit as part of the answer, shows why multiplying by a fraction equal to one changes the unit and not the size, and uses that one idea to convert inches, ounces, cups and seconds, both in the US customary system and in the metric system. Then it turns to rates such as miles per hour and dollars per pound, where the units themselves tell you whether the arithmetic is set up the right way up. Last come area and volume, where the factor is squared or cubed, and scale drawings and maps, where the scale is one more conversion factor.

A puzzle to carry through the pack

A car drives at $60$ miles per hour. About how far does it go in one second?

  • About $1$ foot
  • About $88$ feet
  • About $5{,}000$ feet

The speed is given in miles and hours, and the question is asked in feet and seconds, so one number has to pass through two conversions at once. The first section says how a single measurement changes units. (The second section converts a rate, by changing its top and its bottom separately. The third and fourth ask what happens to the factors for an area and for a drawing.)

By the end of this pack you can write a measurement with its unit, build a conversion factor from a known fact and put it the right way up so the unit you are leaving cancels, convert one-step and two-step measurements in the US customary and metric systems, check an answer by asking whether a smaller unit should give a bigger number, find a unit rate, use distance equals rate times time with the units lined up, convert a rate by converting its top and its bottom, compare offers given in different units, read the units of an answer to catch a setup that is upside down, convert square and cubic units with the length factor squared or cubed, find the cost of covering an area, and use a scale to move between a drawing and the real thing, remembering that areas change by the square of the scale factor.
Before you start

This pack leans on fractions and decimals as numbers (Chapter 1), multiplying and dividing them, and nothing else from the course. You can read the pack at any point alongside it.

Three quick ones to wake up what the pack stands on. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution says where to look.

Write $\dfrac{12}{60}$ in lowest terms.

Answer: $\dfrac15$

The largest number that divides both is $12$, and $\frac{12}{60} = \frac{1}{5}$. See §1.5. Minutes out of an hour reduce this way: $12$ minutes is $\frac15$ of an hour.

Compute $2.5 \cdot 40$.

Answer: $100$

$25 \cdot 40 = 1000$, and one decimal place in $2.5$ moves the point once: $100$. Check: $2 \cdot 40 + 0.5 \cdot 40 = 80 + 20 = 100$ ✓.

Write $\dfrac{90}{60}$ as a decimal.

Answer: $1.5$

$90 \div 60 = 1.5$. Check: $60 + 30 = 90$, and $30$ is half of $60$ ✓. This is how $90$ minutes becomes $1.5$ hours.

D.1 Converting units

A measurement is a number together with a unit: $5$ feet, $12$ ounces, $40$ miles per hour. The unit is part of the answer. The number $5$ by itself does not say whether a rope is that long or a bag is that heavy, and $5$ feet and $60$ inches are the same length written two ways. Converting a measurement changes the unit and the number together so that the size stays the same.

The tool is the conversion factor. Twelve inches and one foot are the same length, so the fraction $\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}$ equals $1$, and so does $\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}$. Multiplying by $1$ changes how a quantity is written and not how much it is. Units cancel top against bottom just as numbers do: $$5\text{ ft}\cdot\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}} = 60\text{ in}.$$

Which way up does the factor go? Argue it once. We want the unit we start with to disappear, and a unit disappears only when it stands on top in the measurement and on the bottom in the factor. So the unit you are leaving goes underneath the factor. For feet to inches the feet go underneath, as above. Turn the factor over and you get $5\text{ ft}\cdot\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}$, which leaves feet times feet per inch, a unit that means nothing here. With the rule “the unit I am leaving goes on the bottom” there is no need to remember whether to multiply or divide: the units choose.

A sanity check costs nothing. The same length measured in a smaller unit takes more of them, so converting to a smaller unit makes the number bigger ($5$ feet is $60$ inches) and converting to a larger unit makes it smaller ($60$ inches is $5$ feet). If your answer goes the wrong way, the factor is upside down.

The metric system is built on prefixes that always mean the same thing: milli is a thousandth, centi a hundredth and kilo a thousand. So $1$ kilometer is $1000$ meters, $1$ meter is $100$ centimeters, and the same prefixes work for grams and liters. A metric conversion moves the decimal point, because the factors are powers of ten.

Often there is no single fact joining two units, as with hours and seconds. Then chain two factors, each with the unit being left underneath, so that the middle unit cancels as well. Every factor is still a fraction equal to $1$, and so the whole product is too. Between the two systems you need one fact, which an exam states in the question: $1$ inch $= 2.54$ centimeters, and $1$ mile is about $1.6$ kilometers. Use it exactly like any other factor, and when the fact says “about” the answer is only about.

Example 1. A factor, turned the right way

A class lasts $150$ minutes. How many hours is that?

  1. The fact is $1$ hour $= 60$ minutes. We are leaving minutes, so minutes go on the bottom: the factor is $\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}}$.
  2. $150\text{ min}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}} = \frac{150}{60}\text{ h} = 2.5\text{ h}$.
  3. The sanity check agrees: hours are the larger unit, so the number fell, from $150$ to $2.5$.
  4. Check the other way: $2.5\text{ h}\cdot\frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}} = 150$ min ✓. $\blacksquare$

Two hours is $120$ minutes and three hours is $180$, so $150$ minutes must lie between two and three hours, and $2.5$ does.

A hallway is $9$ feet long. How many yards is that?

Answer: $3$ yards

$9\text{ ft}\cdot\frac{1\text{ yd}}{3\text{ ft}} = 3\text{ yd}$. Check: $3$ yards of $3$ feet each is $9$ feet ✓.

A bag of rice has a mass of $2.5$ kilograms. How many grams is that?

Answer: $2500$ grams

$2.5\text{ kg}\cdot\frac{1000\text{ g}}{1\text{ kg}} = 2500\text{ g}$. Check: the decimal point moved three places to the right, and $2500\text{ g}\div 1000 = 2.5$ kg ✓.

Example 2. Two factors in a chain

A hiking trail is $2$ miles long. How many inches is that?

  1. There is no one fact for miles and inches, but $1$ mile $= 5280$ feet and $1$ foot $= 12$ inches. Go through feet.
  2. $2\text{ mi}\cdot\frac{5280\text{ ft}}{1\text{ mi}}\cdot\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}$: the miles cancel, then the feet cancel, and inches are left.
  3. $2\cdot 5280 = 10{,}560$ feet, and $10{,}560\cdot 12 = 126{,}720$ inches.
  4. Check by a different path, through yards: $1$ mile is $1760$ yards, so $2$ miles is $3520$ yards, and $3520\cdot 36 = 126{,}720$ inches ✓. The size also fits: inches are small, so the number should be large. $\blacksquare$

Each factor on its own is a fraction equal to $1$, so the product of the two is too, and the trail is no longer or shorter than before.

A flight takes $3$ hours. How many seconds is that?

Answer: $10{,}800$ seconds

$3\text{ h}\cdot\frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}}\cdot\frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}} = 3\cdot 3600 = 10{,}800$ s. Check: $3$ hours is $180$ minutes, and $180\cdot 60 = 10{,}800$ ✓.

D.2 Rates with units

A rate is a quotient of two measurements, and it carries a unit of its own. If a car travels $150$ miles in $3$ hours, the rate is $\frac{150\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}} = 50$ miles per hour. Other rates are dollars per pound, words per minute and gallons per minute. The word “per” means divided by, so the unit after it goes on the bottom. The unit rate is the rate per one of the bottom unit: $50$ miles for each hour, found by dividing the top by the bottom. (Pack A, where rates began, goes further into ratios, but this pack does not need it.)

The rule for motion is $d = r\,t$, distance equals rate times time. Put the units in and it explains itself: $\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\cdot\text{hours}$, and the hours cancel, leaving miles. The two rearrangements are $r = \frac{d}{t}$ and $t = \frac{d}{r}$. The units tell you which to use. Miles divided by miles per hour is $\text{miles}\cdot\frac{\text{hours}}{\text{miles}}$, which is hours. So a time is a distance over a rate. The same pattern holds for prices ($\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{lb}}\cdot\text{lb}$ is dollars) and for pay.

Before you multiply, the units must line up. A speed in miles per hour times a time in minutes leaves a mixed unit, miles times minutes per hour, which is neither a distance nor a mistake you can see in the digits. Convert one so that the time is in the unit on the bottom of the rate: $40$ minutes is $\frac{40}{60}$ of an hour.

A rate has two units, so converting it takes two factors, one for the top and one for the bottom. Take $12$ kilometers per hour in meters per minute. The top changes by $\frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}}$, and the bottom, per hour, changes by $\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}}$, since an hour is $60$ minutes: $$12\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}\cdot\frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}} = \frac{12{,}000}{60}\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}} = 200\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}}.$$ The kilometers cancel and the hours cancel, which is how you know both factors are the right way up. Note the hours factor: a unit on the bottom of the rate leaves from the bottom, so its factor puts that unit on top. Either way the rule is the same: the unit you are leaving goes where it cancels. To compare two offers or two speeds given in different units, bring both to one unit first and then compare the unit rates. Comparing $\$3.50$ for a bag with $\$5.20$ for a bigger bag tells you nothing until both are per pound, or per ounce.

Last, the units are a check you can run on your own setup. If a calculation for a speed ends in hours per mile, the fraction is upside down. If the unit of an answer is miles times hours, you multiplied where a division was needed. Write the unit with every number, let them cancel, and accept the result only when the unit that remains is the one the question asked for.

Example 3. Distance when the time is in minutes

A train moves at $72$ miles per hour for $40$ minutes. How far does it go?

  1. The rate has hours on the bottom, so put the time in hours: $40\text{ min}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ h}$.
  2. $d = r\,t = 72\,\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\text{ h} = 48$ miles. The hours cancel, and miles are what a distance should be in.
  3. Check by changing the rate instead: $72$ miles per hour is $\frac{72}{60} = 1.2$ miles per minute, and $1.2\cdot 40 = 48$ miles ✓. $\blacksquare$

In a whole hour the train would go $72$ miles, and $40$ minutes is less than an hour, so an answer below $72$ is the right size.

A faucet fills $36$ gallons in $9$ minutes. How many gallons per minute is that?

Answer: $4$ gallons per minute

$\frac{36\text{ gal}}{9\text{ min}} = 4$ gallons per minute. Check: $4\cdot 9 = 36$ ✓.

How many hours does a trip of $90$ miles take at $36$ miles per hour?

Answer: $2.5$ hours

$t = \frac{90\text{ mi}}{36\text{ mi/h}} = 2.5$ h. Check: $36\cdot 2 = 72$ miles in two hours, and the other $18$ miles is half of $36$, so half an hour more ✓.

Example 4. Comparing two offers in different units

Store A sells $6$ pounds of rice for $\$8.70$. Store B sells $40$ ounces of the same rice for $\$3.50$. Which is cheaper per pound, and by how much?

  1. The offers are in different units, so bring both to dollars per pound. Store A: $\frac{\$8.70}{6\text{ lb}} = \$1.45$ per pound.
  2. Store B first needs its ounces in pounds: $40\text{ oz}\cdot\frac{1\text{ lb}}{16\text{ oz}} = 2.5\text{ lb}$. Then $\frac{\$3.50}{2.5\text{ lb}} = \$1.40$ per pound.
  3. Store B is cheaper by $\$1.45 - \$1.40 = \$0.05$ per pound.
  4. Check with one amount for both. At Store A, $2.5$ lb would cost $2.5\cdot 1.45 = \$3.625$, more than Store B's $\$3.50$ for the same $2.5$ lb ✓. $\blacksquare$

The comparison is fair only because both prices are for one pound (lb). Comparing the totals, $\$8.70$ and $\$3.50$, would have said nothing.

A walker covers $6$ kilometers per hour. How many meters per hour is that?

Answer: $6000$ meters per hour

$6\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}\cdot\frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}} = 6000$ m per hour. Check: $6000$ m is $6$ km, so the walker still covers $6$ km each hour ✓.

D.3 Area and volume units

A conversion factor for a length does not carry over to an area unchanged. A square foot is a square that is one foot on each side. Cut it into small squares one inch on a side: the square is $12$ inches wide, so each row holds $12$ of them, and there are $12$ rows. That is $12 \cdot 12 = 144$ square inches, not $12$. The same argument works for any unit, because an area is a length times a length, and so a factor for an area is the length factor squared: $$\left(\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}\right)^2 = \frac{144\text{ in}^2}{1\text{ ft}^2}.$$ A volume is a length times a length times a length, so a volume factor is the length factor cubed. A cubic yard is a cube $3$ feet on each edge and holds $3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 27$ cubic feet. In the metric system the factors are powers of a hundred and a thousand: $1\text{ m}^2 = 100 \cdot 100 = 10{,}000\text{ cm}^2$ and $1\text{ m}^3 = 100^3 = 1{,}000{,}000\text{ cm}^3$.

Use the squared factor exactly as you used the others: the unit you are leaving goes where it cancels. To go from square yards to square feet you leave yards, so yards go underneath, and the factor $\left(\frac{3\text{ ft}}{1\text{ yd}}\right)^2 = \frac{9\text{ ft}^2}{1\text{ yd}^2}$ cancels the square yards. The common mistake is to multiply an area by the length factor, $12$ or $3$, when the factor is $144$ or $9$. The sanity check from D.1 catches it, and catches it harder for area: the smaller unit takes more of them, and for an area much more. If a room of $20$ square feet comes out as $60$ square inches, think again: a square inch is a speck beside a square foot.

Capacity and volume meet in the metric system, which is built to make them agree. One milliliter is exactly the capacity of a cube $1$ centimeter on each edge: $1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3$. Since $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL}$, a liter is $1000\text{ cm}^3$. So when a box has a volume of $480\text{ cm}^3$, it holds $480$ milliliters. The US system has no such luck. Gallons and quarts measure capacity, cubic inches and cubic feet measure volume, and a question that joins them states the fact it needs.

Area gives rates of their own. A price per square foot is a rate whose bottom unit is an area, and it multiplies the number of square feet to give a cost: $\frac{\$}{\text{ft}^2}\cdot\text{ft}^2$ leaves dollars. The rate must match the unit of the area. If the floor is measured in square feet and the price is per square yard, change one of them first. Nine square feet make a square yard, so a price per square yard is nine times the price per square foot, since the bigger unit costs more. (If you want the proportion route first, Pack A's proportions section is the place; nothing here depends on it.)

Example 5. Square yards to square feet

A rug has an area of $4.5$ square yards. What is its area in square feet?

  1. Yards are the unit we are leaving, so yards go underneath. A yard is $3$ feet, so the factor is $\left(\frac{3\text{ ft}}{1\text{ yd}}\right)^2 = \frac{9\text{ ft}^2}{1\text{ yd}^2}$.
  2. $4.5\text{ yd}^2\cdot\frac{9\text{ ft}^2}{1\text{ yd}^2} = 40.5\text{ ft}^2$.
  3. The sanity check agrees: feet are the smaller unit, so the number rose, and by a factor of $9$, not $3$.
  4. Check by the sides. A rug $1.5$ yards by $3$ yards has area $4.5$ square yards. In feet it is $4.5$ by $9$, and $4.5 \cdot 9 = 40.5$ square feet ✓. $\blacksquare$

Had we multiplied by $3$ we would have got $13.5$, which is the area of a square rug less than $4$ feet on a side: far too small.

A shelf has a top of $3$ square feet. How many square inches is that?

Answer: $432$ square inches

$3\text{ ft}^2\cdot\frac{144\text{ in}^2}{1\text{ ft}^2} = 432\text{ in}^2$. Check: a shelf $3$ feet by $1$ foot is $36$ inches by $12$ inches, and $36 \cdot 12 = 432$ ✓.

A box is $10$ centimeters long, $5$ centimeters wide and $4$ centimeters high. How many milliliters does it hold when it is full?

Answer: $200$ milliliters

$10 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 = 200\text{ cm}^3$, and $1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ mL}$, so the box holds $200$ mL. Check: the bottom holds $50$ cubic centimeters in a layer, and there are $4$ layers: $4 \cdot 50 = 200$ ✓.

Example 6. The cost of carpeting a room

A room is $12$ feet by $15$ feet. Carpet costs $\$4.50$ per square foot. What does it cost to carpet the room?

  1. The price is per square foot, so find the area in square feet: $12 \cdot 15 = 180\text{ ft}^2$.
  2. $180\text{ ft}^2\cdot\frac{\$4.50}{1\text{ ft}^2} = \$810$. The square feet cancel and dollars are left.
  3. The size is plausible: a hundred square feet would cost $\$450$, and this room is nearly twice that.
  4. Check by square yards. The price per square yard is $9 \cdot \$4.50 = \$40.50$. The room is $4$ yards by $5$ yards, which is $20\text{ yd}^2$, and $20 \cdot 40.50 = \$810$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Both routes cost the same because a square yard of carpet is nine square feet of the same carpet, with nine times the price.

Tile costs $\$6$ per square foot. How many dollars does it cost to tile a floor of $25$ square feet?

Answer: $\$150$

$25\text{ ft}^2\cdot\frac{\$6}{1\text{ ft}^2} = \$150$. Check: $25$ is a quarter of $100$, and $100$ square feet would cost $\$600$, a quarter of which is $\$150$ ✓.

D.4 Scale

A scale drawing or a map keeps every length in the same ratio to the real length it shows. The scale says what the ratio is. “$1$ inch $= 50$ miles” is a statement that one inch on the map and $50$ miles on the ground stand for the same distance. That makes it a conversion factor between the drawing and the world, and you use it exactly as the factors of D.1: the unit you are leaving goes where it cancels, which is underneath here. A distance measured on the map in inches leaves inches, so inches go underneath, $\frac{50\text{ mi}}{1\text{ in}}$, and miles are what is left. Going the other way, from a real distance to the drawing, you are leaving miles, so the factor is $\frac{1\text{ in}}{50\text{ mi}}$.

A scale is also written as a ratio with no units, $1 : 200$. It says the real length is $200$ times the drawn one, in whatever unit you measure. A line $2$ centimeters long on a plan at $1 : 200$ stands for $2 \cdot 200 = 400$ centimeters, which is $4$ meters. The same $2$ in a different unit would give $400$ of that unit. Nothing in the ratio says centimeters; you pick the unit for the measurement and change units afterwards if you want a more sensible one for the answer.

To fit a page, choose the scale the other way round. A hall $36$ feet long has to fit a sheet $9$ inches long. The drawing is $9$ inches for $36$ feet, so $1$ inch stands for $36 \div 9 = 4$ feet. Round to a friendly scale such as $1$ inch $= 5$ feet if you want an easy one, as long as the drawing still fits.

Two figures with the same shape and different sizes are similar, and the scale factor $k$ is the number that takes one to the other. Every length is multiplied by $k$. An area is not. Take a square with side $s$. Its area is $s \cdot s$. Make the side $k s$ and the area is $ks \cdot ks = k^2\,s^2$, so the area was multiplied by $k^2$. The same argument works for any shape, because any area is built of lengths multiplied in twos. Double every length and the area is four times as big; triple every length and it is nine times as big. In the same way, a map at $1 : 1000$ has real lengths $1000$ times the map's, and so its real areas are $1000^2 = 1{,}000{,}000$ times the map's: the map shows an area that is a million times smaller than the ground.

Example 7. A real distance from a map

A map says $1$ inch $= 50$ miles. Two towns are $3.5$ inches apart on the map. How far apart are they?

  1. The scale is the factor $\frac{50\text{ mi}}{1\text{ in}}$, with inches underneath because inches are what we are leaving.
  2. $3.5\text{ in}\cdot\frac{50\text{ mi}}{1\text{ in}} = 175$ miles.
  3. The answer is bigger than $3.5$, as it should be: the map shrinks the ground.
  4. Check by proportion: $\frac{1\text{ in}}{50\text{ mi}}$ must equal $\frac{3.5\text{ in}}{175\text{ mi}}$, and $175 \div 3.5 = 50$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Two inches would be $100$ miles and four inches $200$ miles, so $3.5$ inches must lie between them, and $175$ does.

On a map, $1$ centimeter stands for $5$ kilometers. A trail is $7$ centimeters long on the map. How many kilometers long is the real trail?

Answer: $35$ kilometers

$7\text{ cm}\cdot\frac{5\text{ km}}{1\text{ cm}} = 35$ km. Check: $35 \div 5 = 7$ centimeters back on the map ✓.

A plan has the scale $1 : 200$. A hall measures $3$ centimeters on the plan. How many meters long is the real hall?

Answer: $6$ meters

$3 \cdot 200 = 600$ centimeters, and $600\text{ cm}\cdot\frac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}} = 6$ m. Check: $6$ meters is $600$ centimeters, and $600 \div 200 = 3$ ✓.

Example 8. Lengths times $k$, areas times $k^2$

A rectangle is $4$ cm by $6$ cm. It is enlarged by the scale factor $k = 3$. What are its new dimensions and its new area?

  1. Each length is multiplied by $3$: the new rectangle is $12$ cm by $18$ cm.
  2. The new area is $12 \cdot 18 = 216\text{ cm}^2$, and the old area was $4 \cdot 6 = 24\text{ cm}^2$.
  3. The area was multiplied by $216 \div 24 = 9 = 3^2$, not by $3$.
  4. Check from the other side: $24 \cdot k^2 = 24 \cdot 9 = 216$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Multiplying the lengths by $3$ makes three copies along one side and three along the other, so nine copies of the old rectangle fit in the new one.

A figure with an area of $20$ square centimeters is enlarged by a scale factor of $2$. What is the area of the enlargement, in square centimeters?

Answer: $80$ square centimeters

$20 \cdot 2^2 = 80$. Check: a rectangle $4$ by $5$ becomes $8$ by $10$, and $8 \cdot 10 = 80$ ✓.

Practice

Give each answer as a number in the unit the question names, without writing the unit. Use a decimal, such as $4.5$, where the answer is not whole. Where a question states a fact joining two systems, use it exactly as stated. For an area or a volume, give the number of square or cubic units. A scale of the form $1 : N$ has no units, so the real length is $N$ times the drawn one. Money is in dollars.

A banner is $84$ inches long. How many feet long is it?

Answer: $7$ feet

$84\text{ in}\cdot\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}} = 7$ ft. Check: $7$ feet of $12$ inches each is $7\cdot 12 = 84$ inches ✓.

A bag of flour weighs $3.5$ pounds. How many ounces is that?

Answer: $56$ ounces

$3.5\text{ lb}\cdot\frac{16\text{ oz}}{1\text{ lb}} = 56$ oz. Check: $56\div 16 = 3.5$ lb ✓.

A trail is $4500$ meters long. How many kilometers long is it?

Answer: $4.5$ kilometers

$4500\text{ m}\cdot\frac{1\text{ km}}{1000\text{ m}} = 4.5$ km. Check: $4.5\cdot 1000 = 4500$ meters ✓.

A bottle holds $750$ milliliters. How many liters does it hold?

Answer: $0.75$ liters

$750\text{ mL}\cdot\frac{1\text{ L}}{1000\text{ mL}} = 0.75$ L. Check: $0.75\cdot 1000 = 750$ mL ✓.

A recipe needs $6$ quarts of broth. How many cups is that?

Answer: $24$ cups

$6\text{ qt}\cdot\frac{2\text{ pt}}{1\text{ qt}}\cdot\frac{2\text{ cups}}{1\text{ pt}} = 6\cdot 2\cdot 2 = 24$ cups. Check: one quart is $4$ cups, and $6\cdot 4 = 24$ ✓.

A film lasts $7200$ seconds. How many hours is that?

Answer: $2$ hours

$7200\text{ s}\cdot\frac{1\text{ min}}{60\text{ s}}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}} = \frac{7200}{3600} = 2$ h. Check: $2$ hours of $3600$ seconds each is $7200$ seconds ✓.

A phone is $6$ inches long. Use $1$ inch $= 2.54$ centimeters. How many centimeters long is the phone?

Answer: $15.24$ centimeters

$6\text{ in}\cdot\frac{2.54\text{ cm}}{1\text{ in}} = 15.24$ cm. Check by an estimate: $6\cdot 2.5 = 15$, and the extra $6\cdot 0.04 = 0.24$ makes $15.24$ ✓.

$5$ pounds of potatoes cost $\$6.25$. How many dollars per pound is that?

Answer: $\$1.25$ per pound

$\frac{\$6.25}{5\text{ lb}} = \$1.25$ per pound. Check: $5\cdot 1.25 = 6.25$ ✓.

A hiker walks $14$ miles in $4$ hours. What is the speed in miles per hour?

Answer: $3.5$ miles per hour

$\frac{14\text{ mi}}{4\text{ h}} = 3.5$ miles per hour. Check: $3.5\cdot 4 = 14$ miles ✓.

A cyclist rides at $15$ miles per hour for $20$ minutes. How many miles does the cyclist ride?

Answer: $5$ miles

$20$ minutes is $\frac{20}{60} = \frac13$ hour, so $d = 15\cdot\frac13 = 5$ miles. Check: $15$ miles per hour is $\frac{15}{60} = 0.25$ mile per minute, and $0.25\cdot 20 = 5$ ✓.

A car travels $35$ miles at $28$ miles per hour. How many minutes does the trip take?

Answer: $75$ minutes

$t = \frac{35}{28} = 1.25$ hours, and $1.25\cdot 60 = 75$ minutes. Check: $28$ miles per hour is $\frac{28}{60}$ mile per minute, and $75\cdot\frac{28}{60} = 35$ miles ✓.

A bus moves at $54$ kilometers per hour. How many meters per second is that?

Answer: $15$ meters per second

$54\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}\cdot\frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{54{,}000}{3600} = 15$ m/s. Check: at $15$ meters per second, one hour is $15\cdot 3600 = 54{,}000$ m, which is $54$ km ✓.

Coffee costs $\$9.60$ per pound. How many dollars per ounce is that?

Answer: $\$0.60$ per ounce

$\frac{\$9.60}{1\text{ lb}}\cdot\frac{1\text{ lb}}{16\text{ oz}} = \$0.60$ per ounce. Check: $16$ ounces at $\$0.60$ each is $16\cdot 0.6 = \$9.60$ ✓.

Brand A sells $12$ ounces of peanut butter for $\$3.00$. Brand B sells $1.5$ pounds for $\$5.28$. How many cents per ounce cheaper is brand B than brand A?

Answer: $3$ cents per ounce

Brand A: $\frac{300\text{ cents}}{12\text{ oz}} = 25$ cents per ounce. Brand B: $1.5$ pounds is $1.5\cdot 16 = 24$ ounces, and $\frac{528\text{ cents}}{24\text{ oz}} = 22$ cents per ounce. The difference is $25 - 22 = 3$ cents. Check with one amount: $24$ ounces of brand A would cost $2\cdot\$3.00 = \$6.00$, which is $\$0.72$ more than brand B, and $72\div 24 = 3$ ✓.

A tabletop has an area of $7$ square feet. How many square inches is that?

Answer: $1008$ square inches

$7\text{ ft}^2\cdot\frac{144\text{ in}^2}{1\text{ ft}^2} = 1008\text{ in}^2$. Check: $7 \cdot 100 = 700$ and $7 \cdot 44 = 308$, and $700 + 308 = 1008$ ✓.

A patio covers $54$ square feet. How many square yards is that?

Answer: $6$ square yards

$54\text{ ft}^2\cdot\frac{1\text{ yd}^2}{9\text{ ft}^2} = 6\text{ yd}^2$. Check: $6$ square yards of $9$ square feet each is $6 \cdot 9 = 54$ ✓.

A sheet of plywood has an area of $3.5$ square meters. How many square centimeters is that?

Answer: $35{,}000$ square centimeters

$3.5\text{ m}^2\cdot\frac{10{,}000\text{ cm}^2}{1\text{ m}^2} = 35{,}000\text{ cm}^2$. Check: $35{,}000 \div 10{,}000 = 3.5$ square meters ✓.

A truck delivers $5$ cubic yards of soil. How many cubic feet is that?

Answer: $135$ cubic feet

$5\text{ yd}^3\cdot\frac{27\text{ ft}^3}{1\text{ yd}^3} = 135\text{ ft}^3$. Check: $135 \div 27 = 5$ cubic yards ✓.

A tank is $20$ centimeters long, $15$ centimeters wide and $10$ centimeters high. How many liters does it hold when it is full?

Answer: $3$ liters

$20 \cdot 15 \cdot 10 = 3000\text{ cm}^3 = 3000$ mL, and $3000\text{ mL}\cdot\frac{1\text{ L}}{1000\text{ mL}} = 3$ L. Check: the bottom is $300\text{ cm}^2$, and $10$ layers of $300$ make $3000$ ✓.

A room is $9$ feet long and $12$ feet wide. Flooring costs $\$3.50$ per square foot. How many dollars does it cost to cover the room?

Answer: $\$378$

The area is $9 \cdot 12 = 108\text{ ft}^2$, and $108\cdot\$3.50 = \$378$. Check: $100 \cdot 3.50 = 350$ and $8 \cdot 3.50 = 28$, and $350 + 28 = 378$ ✓.

Fabric costs $\$4$ per square foot. How many dollars per square yard is that?

Answer: $\$36$ per square yard

$\frac{\$4}{1\text{ ft}^2}\cdot\frac{9\text{ ft}^2}{1\text{ yd}^2} = \$36$ per square yard. Check: $\$36 \div 9 = \$4$ per square foot ✓.

A map has the scale $1$ inch $= 20$ miles. Two cities are $4.5$ inches apart on the map. How many miles apart are they?

Answer: $90$ miles

$4.5\text{ in}\cdot\frac{20\text{ mi}}{1\text{ in}} = 90$ mi. Check: $90 \div 20 = 4.5$ inches ✓.

A map uses the scale $1$ inch $= 15$ miles. A highway is really $135$ miles long. How many inches long is it on the map?

Answer: $9$ inches

$135\text{ mi}\cdot\frac{1\text{ in}}{15\text{ mi}} = 9$ in. Check: $9 \cdot 15 = 135$ miles ✓.

A site plan has the scale $1 : 500$. A fence measures $4$ centimeters on the plan. How many meters long is the real fence?

Answer: $20$ meters

$4 \cdot 500 = 2000$ cm, and $2000\text{ cm}\cdot\frac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}} = 20$ m. Check: $20$ m is $2000$ cm, and $2000 \div 500 = 4$ ✓.

A model car is built to the scale $1 : 24$. The real car is $4.8$ meters long. How many centimeters long is the model?

Answer: $20$ centimeters

$4.8$ m is $480$ cm, and $480 \div 24 = 20$ cm. Check: $20 \cdot 24 = 480$ cm, which is $4.8$ m ✓.

A banner is $42$ feet long. To fit a page it is drawn $6$ inches long. How many feet does $1$ inch on the drawing stand for?

Answer: $7$ feet per inch

$\frac{42\text{ ft}}{6\text{ in}} = 7$ feet per inch. Check: $6 \cdot 7 = 42$ feet ✓.

A figure with an area of $7$ square centimeters is enlarged by a scale factor of $4$. What is the area of the enlargement, in square centimeters?

Answer: $112$ square centimeters

$7 \cdot 4^2 = 7 \cdot 16 = 112$. Check: a rectangle $1$ by $7$ becomes $4$ by $28$, and $4 \cdot 28 = 112$ ✓.

A floor plan has the scale $1 : 50$. A room covers $24$ square centimeters on the plan. What is the real floor area, in square meters?

Answer: $6$ square meters

$24 \cdot 50^2 = 60{,}000\text{ cm}^2$, and $60{,}000 \div 10{,}000 = 6\text{ m}^2$. Check: a plan room $4$ cm by $6$ cm is $200$ cm by $300$ cm, which is $2$ m by $3$ m, and $2 \cdot 3 = 6$ ✓.

The car goes about $88$ feet in a second. Convert the top and the bottom of the rate: $60\,\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}}\cdot\frac{5280\text{ ft}}{1\text{ mi}}\cdot\frac{1\text{ h}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{316{,}800}{3600} = 88$ feet per second. A quicker route is that $60$ miles per hour is a mile a minute, and a mile is $5280$ feet in $60$ seconds, which is $88$ feet in each. The size of a speed does not change with the unit; only the number and the unit written beside it do.

  • A measurement is a number and a unit, and the unit is part of the answer.
  • A conversion factor is a fraction equal to $1$, such as $\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}$. Put the unit you are leaving where it cancels, which is on the bottom for a unit on top of the measurement. The size stays the same.
  • A smaller unit needs a bigger number and a larger unit a smaller one. Use this to check every conversion.
  • Metric prefixes mean the same thing everywhere: milli is $\frac{1}{1000}$, centi is $\frac{1}{100}$, kilo is $1000$. Where there is no single fact, chain factors (hours to seconds, miles to inches). A fact between systems is given in the question, and “about” stays about.
  • A rate is a quotient of two measurements with a unit of its own, and the unit rate is the rate per one of the bottom unit.
  • $d = r\,t$, $r = \frac{d}{t}$, $t = \frac{d}{r}$. Line up the units before you multiply, and let them cancel.
  • Convert a rate by converting its top and its bottom separately, and compare offers by bringing both to one unit rate.
  • The unit of the result is a check on the setup: hours per mile when you wanted miles per hour means the fraction is upside down.
  • A factor for an area is the length factor squared, and for a volume cubed: $1$ ft$^2 = 144$ in$^2$, $1$ yd$^3 = 27$ ft$^3$. Multiplying an area by the length factor is the common mistake. A milliliter is a cubic centimeter, and a price per square foot times the area is the cost.
  • A scale is a conversion factor between the drawing and the world, with the unit you are leaving underneath. A ratio such as $1 : 200$ multiplies a drawn length by $200$ in any unit. A scale factor $k$ multiplies lengths by $k$ and areas by $k^2$.
