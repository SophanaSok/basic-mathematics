Skill packs · Pack G

Growth, Decay and Sequences

A list of numbers with a rule behind it is a sequence, and two rules cover most of what the gate exams ask: add the same amount each step, or multiply by the same amount each step. This pack starts with those two sequences, how to say the rule step by step and how to say it from the position, and how to reach a far term, find which position holds a value and fill in a missing term. Its second section turns the two sequences into two kinds of change: a constant difference per period is linear, a constant ratio per period is exponential, and a percent change is a multiplier. It reads and writes growth and decay models, explains why an exponential quantity passes a linear one in the end, and counts the periods until a value is reached. Its third section puts money in the story: compound interest is growth with a balance, set against simple interest, and a rate compounded more often than yearly applies a smaller rate more times. Its fourth names the answer to “how many periods”: a logarithm is an exponent, two rules from the exponent laws solve $a \cdot b^t = c$, and a calculator reads the answer when it is not whole.

A puzzle to carry through the pack

A patch of lily pads on a pond doubles in size every day. On day $30$ it covers the whole pond. On which day did it cover half the pond?

  • day $15$
  • day $29$
  • day $20$

Half of the time feels like the right place for half of the pond, and that feeling is the one a steady, linear story would earn. A story that doubles is a different kind of story, and the two sections that follow say how it differs: the first builds the two sequences, adding and multiplying, and the second sets the multiplying one against the adding one. The third puts money in the story, as compound interest, and the fourth turns the question “how many periods?” into one step with logarithms.

By the end of this pack you can tell an arithmetic sequence from a geometric one by taking differences and then ratios, write the rule both ways (from step to step, and as a formula for the $n$th term), and use the formula to find a far term, to find which position holds a value, to fill in a missing middle term and to add a short arithmetic list by pairing its ends. You can tell linear change from exponential change, turn a percent increase or decrease into a multiplier, read the starting value and the multiplier from a model or a table and the percent per period from the multiplier, write a growth or decay model from a story, say why an exponential quantity overtakes a linear one however the two begin, and count the periods until a value is reached by listing. You can find a balance under compound interest and set it against simple interest, apply a yearly rate compounded more often than yearly, read the principal and the rate from a formula, estimate a doubling time, evaluate a logarithm as an exponent, and solve $a \cdot b^t = c$ exactly or with a calculator, choosing the expression that gives $t$.
Before you start

Three quick ones to wake up what the pack stands on. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution says where to look.

Evaluate $5 \cdot 2^3$.

Answer: $40$

The power comes first: $2^3 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$, and $5 \cdot 8 = 40$. Check: $5 \cdot 2 = 10$, $10 \cdot 2 = 20$, $20 \cdot 2 = 40$ ✓.

Evaluate $7 + 4(n - 1)$ when $n = 6$.

Answer: $27$

$n - 1 = 5$, then $4 \cdot 5 = 20$ and $7 + 20 = 27$. Check by spreading the $4$ first: $7 + 4 \cdot 6 - 4 \cdot 1 = 7 + 24 - 4 = 27$ ✓.

A price of $\$80$ goes up by $25\%$. What is the new price in dollars?

Answer: $\$100$

Of $80$, $25\%$ is $20$, and $80 + 20 = 100$. Check another way: a rise of $25\%$ leaves $125\%$ of the price, and $1.25 \cdot 80 = 100$ ✓. That second way, one multiplication by $1.25$, is the idea of the second section.

G.1 Arithmetic and geometric sequences

A sequence is a list of numbers with a rule behind it. Its entries are the terms, written $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, where $a_n$ is the term in position $n$. A sequence is a function whose inputs are the positions $1, 2, 3, \dots$, and the rule can be said two ways. The step-to-step way (the recursive form) says how to get from one term to the next: “add $3$ each time.” The straight-from-the-position way (the explicit form) says the term at once: “the $n$th term is $3n + 2$.”

An arithmetic sequence adds the same number, the common difference $d$, at every step. The $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps away from the first, since position $1$ is no steps away, position $2$ is one step and position $n$ is $n - 1$ steps. Each step adds $d$, so $$a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d.$$ Test it: $n = 1$ gives $a_1$, and $n = 2$ gives $a_1 + d$. A geometric sequence multiplies by the same number, the common ratio $r$, at every step. Again the $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps from the first, and each step multiplies by $r$, so $$a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}.$$ Test it the same way: $n = 1$ gives $a_1 \cdot r^0 = a_1$, and $n = 2$ gives $a_1 r$.

To tell which kind a list is, take differences first and then ratios. If the differences between neighbors are all the same, the list is arithmetic. If the ratios are all the same, it is geometric. A list where neither is constant is neither, so $1, 2, 4, 7, 11$ (differences $1, 2, 3, 4$) is not arithmetic and not geometric. These formulas do four jobs.

  • The next term, or a far one without listing everything before it: put the position in the formula.
  • Which position holds a given value: in an arithmetic sequence, solve $a_1 + (n - 1)d = v$ for $n$, and check that $n$ is a whole number, since a value that gives $n = 7.4$ is not a term of the sequence at all.
  • A missing middle term between two neighbors: in an arithmetic sequence it is the mean of its neighbors, since it sits the same distance from each; in a geometric sequence it is the square root of their product, taking the sign the problem gives when it says the terms are positive (or negative), since the ratio from the first to the middle equals the ratio from the middle to the last.
  • The common difference from two terms that are not neighbors: from position $i$ to position $j$ is $j - i$ steps, so $d = \dfrac{a_j - a_i}{j - i}$.

One more use is the sum of an arithmetic list, which pairing makes short. Take $2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17$. The first and last add to $19$, the second and second-to-last add to $5 + 14 = 19$, and the middle pair adds to $8 + 11 = 19$. Every pair has the same sum, there are $3$ pairs, and the total is $3 \cdot 19 = 57$. In general the sum is the number of terms, halved, times the sum of the first and last. Check by adding in order: $2 + 5 = 7$, then $15$, $26$, $40$, and $57$ ✓. The book argues the same idea for the numbers $1$ to $100$ in Chapter 15, summations, and its geometric series add up a geometric list. This is where the topic continues, and nothing here depends on it.

Example 1. A far term, and which position

The sequence $5, 11, 17, 23, \dots$ is arithmetic. Find its $12$th term, and say whether $107$ and $100$ are terms.

  1. The differences are $11 - 5 = 6$, $17 - 11 = 6$ and $23 - 17 = 6$, so $a_1 = 5$ and $d = 6$.
  2. The $12$th term is $11$ steps from the first: $a_{12} = 5 + 11 \cdot 6 = 5 + 66 = 71$.
  3. For $107$, solve $5 + (n - 1) \cdot 6 = 107$: $(n - 1) \cdot 6 = 102$, so $n - 1 = 17$ and $n = 18$. The value $107$ is the $18$th term.
  4. For $100$: $(n - 1) \cdot 6 = 95$ gives $n - 1 = \frac{95}{6}$, which is not a whole number. So $100$ is not a term.
  5. Check against a list: $5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59, 65, 71$ has $12$ terms and ends at $71$ ✓. Check the position by listing on from $71$: $77, 83, 89, 95, 101, 107$ are the $13$th through $18$th terms, so $107$ is the $18$th ✓, and $100$ falls between $95$ and $101$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

A value is a term only when the number of steps comes out whole.

The sequence $9, 13, 17, 21, \dots$ is arithmetic. What is its $15$th term?

Answer: $65$

The difference is $4$ and $a_1 = 9$, so $a_{15} = 9 + 14 \cdot 4 = 9 + 56 = 65$. Check by listing: $9, 13, 17, 21, 25, 29, 33, 37, 41, 45, 49, 53, 57, 61, 65$ has $15$ terms and ends at $65$ ✓.

Write the $n$th term of the arithmetic sequence $2, 7, 12, 17, \dots$ as an expression in $n$.

Answer: $5n - 3$

Here $a_1 = 2$ and $d = 5$, so $a_n = 2 + 5(n - 1) = 2 + 5n - 5 = 5n - 3$. Check against the list: $n = 1$ gives $2$, $n = 2$ gives $7$, and $n = 4$ gives $5 \cdot 4 - 3 = 17$ ✓.

Example 2. A geometric term, and a missing term

The sequence $3, 6, 12, 24, \dots$ is geometric. Find its $8$th term. Then find the missing middle term in $2,\ \square,\ 50$, a geometric sequence of positive numbers.

  1. The ratios are $6 \div 3 = 2$, $12 \div 6 = 2$ and $24 \div 12 = 2$, so $a_1 = 3$ and $r = 2$.
  2. The $8$th term is $7$ steps from the first: $a_8 = 3 \cdot 2^7 = 3 \cdot 128 = 384$.
  3. For the missing term, the ratio from the first to the middle equals the ratio from the middle to the last, so the middle term $m$ satisfies $m^2 = 2 \cdot 50 = 100$. It is positive, so $m = 10$.
  4. Check the first by listing: $3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192, 384$ has $8$ terms and ends at $384$ ✓. Check the second by ratios: $10 \div 2 = 5$ and $50 \div 10 = 5$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Had the problem allowed negative terms, $2, -10, 50$ would also be geometric, with ratio $-5$. The problem says the terms are positive, and that decides which root to take. Two neighbors of opposite sign, such as $4$ and $-9$, have no real middle term at all, since their product is negative.

The sequence $1, 4, 16, 64, \dots$ is geometric. What is its $6$th term?

Answer: $1{,}024$

The ratio is $4$ and $a_1 = 1$, so $a_6 = 1 \cdot 4^5 = 1{,}024$. Check by listing: $1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1{,}024$ has $6$ terms and ends at $1{,}024$ ✓.

G.2 Exponential growth and decay

The two kinds of sequence become the two kinds of change. When a quantity gains the same amount in every period, it changes linearly: after $t$ periods it is $y = a + dt$, the arithmetic rule with the period count in place of the position. When it is multiplied by the same factor in every period, it changes exponentially: $$y = a \cdot b^{\,t}.$$ Here $a$ is the value at $t = 0$, and $b$ is the multiplier for one period. The multiplier has to be read from the story. A rise of $r\%$ keeps the whole amount and adds $r\%$ of it, so the multiplier is $b = 1 + \frac{r}{100}$; a fall of $r\%$ leaves $b = 1 - \frac{r}{100}$. (Pack A is where this began, with repeated percent changes.) Doubling is $b = 2$, tripling is $b = 3$, and halving is $b = \frac12$. Decay is the same formula with $b < 1$, and a half-life is the length of the period in which a quantity halves.

The same facts read the other way. To read a model $y = a \cdot b^{\,t}$, take $a$ from the value at $t = 0$ and $b$ from the ratio of two values one period apart. Then the percent per period comes from $b$: $1.08$ is a rise of $8\%$, and $0.85$ is a fall of $15\%$. To write a model from a story, name the starting value, find the multiplier for one period, and say what $t$ counts.

Why does an exponential quantity pass a linear one, however large the linear gain and however small the multiplier above $1$? Each period's increase of an exponential quantity is a fixed share of an amount that keeps growing, so the increases themselves keep growing, while a linear quantity gains the same amount every time. A table shows it. Plan A starts at $100$ and gains $30$ each week; Plan B starts at only $20$ and doubles.

A linear plan and an exponential plan, week by week
Week $t$Plan A: $100 + 30t$Plan B: $20 \cdot 2^t$
$0$$100$$20$

Plan B is far behind for three weeks and ahead in week $4$. Its gains are $20, 40, 80, 160$, each double the one before, while Plan A's gain is $30$ every time. With a multiplier such as $1.1$ the pass takes far longer, but it comes, because a share of a growing amount outgrows any fixed gain. To find how many periods a quantity needs to reach a value, list the amounts period by period until one passes it. Logarithms, in G.4, turn that search into one step.

Example 3. A growth story, and the percent read back

A culture of $320$ yeast cells grows by $25\%$ every hour. How many cells are there after $3$ hours? Then, from the counts of $400$ and $500$ cells in the first two hours, read the percent back.

  1. A rise of $25\%$ gives the multiplier $b = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25$, and the start is $a = 320$, so $y = 320 \cdot 1.25^t$.
  2. After $3$ hours: $y = 320 \cdot 1.25^3 = 320 \cdot 1.953125 = 625$ cells.
  3. The counts in the first two hours are $400$ and $500$. Their ratio is $500 \div 400 = 1.25$, which is $1 + 0.25$, a rise of $25\%$ per hour.
  4. Check period by period: $320 \cdot 1.25 = 400$, $400 \cdot 1.25 = 500$ and $500 \cdot 1.25 = 625$ ✓. The percent read back from the multiplier $1.25$ is $25\%$, the story's ✓. $\blacksquare$

Growing by $25\%$ three times is not growing by $75\%$ once: the second rise is $25\%$ of a larger amount, and $320 \cdot 1.75 = 560$ is short of $625$.

A video has $200$ views today, and its views triple each day. How many views does it have after $3$ days?

Answer: $5{,}400$ views

The multiplier is $3$, so $y = 200 \cdot 3^3 = 200 \cdot 27 = 5{,}400$. Check day by day: $200$, then $600$, then $1{,}800$, then $5{,}400$ ✓.

Example 4. Decay and a half-life

A sample of $640$ grams of a tracer has a half-life of $3$ days. How much is left after $12$ days? The amount is measured at the end of each half-life. After how many days does a measurement first show less than $10$ grams?

  1. The period is $3$ days and the multiplier per period is $\frac12$. Twelve days is $12 \div 3 = 4$ periods, so $y = 640 \cdot \left(\frac12\right)^4 = 640 \div 16 = 40$ grams.
  2. For the second question, list the measurement after each period: $640, 320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, 5$. The measurement is $10$ after $6$ periods, which is not less than $10$, and $5$ after $7$ periods, which is.
  3. Seven periods is $7 \cdot 3 = 21$ days.
  4. Check the first answer by halving: $640 \to 320 \to 160 \to 80 \to 40$ is $4$ halvings ✓. Check the second by the power: $640 \div 2^7 = 640 \div 128 = 5 < 10$, and $640 \div 2^6 = 10$, not less ✓. $\blacksquare$

The unit of $t$ is the period, so the number of days must be changed into a number of half-lives before the power is taken.

A herd shrank from $1{,}200$ animals to $900$ in one year. By what percent does it shrink each year? Give the percent without the percent sign.

Answer: $25$

The multiplier is $900 \div 1{,}200 = 0.75$, which is $1 - 0.25$, a fall of $25\%$. Check: $25\%$ of $1{,}200$ is $300$, and $1{,}200 - 300 = 900$ ✓.

A savings jar holds $\$3$ today (day $0$) and the amount in it doubles each day. On which day is it first more than $\$100$?

Answer: day $6$

The amounts are $3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192$ on days $0$ through $6$, and $96$ is under $100$ while $192$ is over. Check by the power: $3 \cdot 2^5 = 96 < 100$ and $3 \cdot 2^6 = 192 > 100$ ✓.

G.3 Compound interest

Interest is G.2's growth with money. A deposit (or a loan) of a principal $P$ dollars earns interest at a yearly rate of $r\%$. When each year's interest is added to the balance, and the next year's interest is figured on the new balance, the interest is compounded yearly. A rise of $r\%$ is the multiplier $1 + \frac{r}{100}$, so one year turns $P$ into $P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)$, and the next year multiplies that by the same number again. After $t$ years $$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^{t}.$$ This is G.2's argument over again: each year's interest is a fixed share of a balance that keeps growing, so the interest itself grows. (Pack A is where the multiplier idea began, and G.2 is where it became $y = a \cdot b^{\,t}$.)

Simple interest is different: it is always $\frac{Pr}{100}$, a share of the original principal, so the balance is $A = P + t \cdot \frac{Pr}{100}$. That adds the same amount each year, a linear model, the arithmetic rule of G.1. The table sets the two side by side for $\$2{,}500$ at $6\%$, where one year's interest on the principal is $\$150$.

$\$2{,}500$ at $6\%$ a year, simple against compound interest, in dollars
Year $t$Simple: $2{,}500 + 150t$Compound: $2{,}500 \cdot 1.06^t$
$0$$2{,}500$$2{,}500$
$1$$2{,}650$$2{,}650$
$2$$2{,}800$$2{,}809$
$3$$2{,}950$$2{,}977.54$

The two agree after one year. In year $2$ the compound account earns $6\%$ of $\$2{,}650$, which is $\$159$, against $\$150$ for the simple one. The extra $\$9$ is $6\%$ of the first year's $\$150$ of interest: it is interest on interest, and that is the whole gap. By year $3$ the compound account is ahead by $\$27.54$, and the lead keeps growing, since it is a share of a growing balance.

A bank may add interest more often than once a year. A yearly rate of $r\%$ compounded $n$ times a year applies $\frac{r}{n}\%$ in each of $n$ periods, so the multiplier for one period is $1 + \frac{r}{100n}$ and it is used $nt$ times in $t$ years: $$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100n}\right)^{nt}.$$ That is G.2's multiplier applied $nt$ times. Take $\$1{,}000$ at $12\%$ compounded twice a year. Each half-year applies $6\%$, the multiplier $1.06$, twice in one year: $1{,}000 \cdot 1.06^2 = 1{,}000 \cdot 1.1236 = \$1{,}123.60$. Compounded once a year the same deposit gives $1{,}000 \cdot 1.12 = \$1{,}120$. Compounding more often does help, but by a little: $\$3.60$ on $\$1{,}000$. The extra comes only from interest on the half-year's interest, a share of a share, so it is small.

A balance can also be given as a formula. In $A = 3{,}000 \cdot 1.04^{t}$ the form is $a \cdot b^{\,t}$, so the principal is $a = \$3{,}000$ and the multiplier is $b = 1.04 = 1 + \frac{4}{100}$, a rate of $4\%$ a year. Since $b > 1$ the balance grows. To see how long a balance takes to double, list the powers of the multiplier until one passes $2$. At $6\%$, $1.06^{11} \approx 1.898$ is not enough and $1.06^{12} \approx 2.012$ is over, so it doubles in $12$ years. The rule of $72$ is an estimate for the same question: the years to double are about $72 \div r$. At $6\%$ it gives $72 \div 6 = 12$, which agrees. It is an estimate and no more: at $9\%$ it gives $8$, but $1.09^{8} \approx 1.993$ is still under $2$, and $1.09^{9} \approx 2.172$ is over, so the true count is $9$ years.

Example 5. Compound against simple on one principal

A deposit of $\$10{,}000$ earns $7\%$ a year for $3$ years. Find the balance with yearly compounding, the balance with simple interest, and how much more the compound account holds.

  1. A rise of $7\%$ is the multiplier $1.07$, so the compound balance is $10{,}000 \cdot 1.07^3 = 10{,}000 \cdot 1.225043 = \$12{,}250.43$.
  2. Simple interest is $7\%$ of $10{,}000$, which is $\$700$ each year. In $3$ years that is $\$2{,}100$, so the simple balance is $\$12{,}100$.
  3. The compound account holds $12{,}250.43 - 12{,}100 = \$150.43$ more.
  4. Check the compound balance year by year: $10{,}000 \to 10{,}700 \to 11{,}449 \to 12{,}250.43$ ✓. Check the gap by algebra: it is $10{,}000\,(1.07^3 - 1 - 3 \cdot 0.07) = 10{,}000 \cdot 0.015043 = 150.43$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The gap is interest on interest, so it grows every year, while the simple account's interest stays at $\$700$.

A balance of $\$1{,}200$ earns $5\%$ a year, compounded yearly. What is the balance, in dollars, after $2$ years?

Answer: $\$1{,}323$

The multiplier is $1.05$, so $A = 1{,}200 \cdot 1.05^2 = 1{,}200 \cdot 1.1025 = 1{,}323$. Check year by year: $1{,}200 \to 1{,}260 \to 1{,}323$ ✓.

Example 6. Reading a formula, with half-yearly compounding

An account follows $A = 3{,}500 \cdot 1.04^{2t}$ dollars after $t$ years. Name the principal, the yearly rate and how often the interest is added, and find the balance after $1$ year.

  1. Match the form $P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100n}\right)^{nt}$. The principal is $P = \$3{,}500$.
  2. The exponent is $2t$, so $n = 2$: the interest is added twice a year. The multiplier $1.04 = 1 + \frac{4}{100}$ is a rise of $4\%$ per half-year, so the yearly rate is $r = 2 \cdot 4 = 8\%$. The multiplier is above $1$, so the balance grows.
  3. For $t = 1$ the exponent is $2$: $A = 3{,}500 \cdot 1.04^2 = 3{,}500 \cdot 1.0816 = \$3{,}785.60$.
  4. Check half-year by half-year: $3{,}500 \to 3{,}640 \to 3{,}785.60$ ✓. Check against yearly compounding at $8\%$, which gives $3{,}500 \cdot 1.08 = \$3{,}780$: the twice-a-year account holds $\$5.60$ more, which is $4\%$ of the first half-year's $\$140$ of interest ✓. $\blacksquare$

The yearly rate is not the number in the multiplier: $1.04$ is the rate per period, and the exponent says how many periods there are.

By the rule of $72$, about how many years does money take to double at $8\%$ a year?

Answer: $9$ years

The rule gives $72 \div 8 = 9$ years. Check the division: $8 \cdot 9 = 72$ ✓. It is only an estimate: listing the powers of $1.08$ gives about $1.999$ after $9$ years, a hair under $2$, so the true count is $10$.

A bank pays $6\%$ a year, compounded twice a year. A deposit of $\$4{,}000$ is worth how many dollars after $1$ year?

Answer: $\$4{,}243.60$

Each half-year applies $3\%$, the multiplier $1.03$, and it is used twice: $4{,}000 \cdot 1.03^2 = 4{,}000 \cdot 1.0609 = 4{,}243.60$. Check half-year by half-year: $4{,}000 \to 4{,}120 \to 4{,}243.60$ ✓.

G.4 Solving with logarithms

G.2 and G.3 found how many periods a quantity needs by listing powers. A logarithm is the name for that answer. The number $\log_b y$ is the exponent $t$ with $b^t = y$, and it is nothing more than that. So $\log_2 32 = 5$ because $2^5 = 32$, $\log_{10} 1{,}000 = 3$ because $10^3 = 1{,}000$, $\log_5 1 = 0$ because $5^0 = 1$, and $\log_9 3 = \frac12$ because $9^{1/2} = 3$. With a base above $1$, a number less than $1$ has a negative logarithm, since a negative exponent makes a fraction: $\log_{10} \frac{1}{100} = -2$. Chapter 12's logarithms section is where this began, with the logarithm as the inverse of a power.

Two rules do the solving, and both come from the exponent laws. Let $x = \log_b u$ and $y = \log_b v$, so that $u = b^x$ and $v = b^y$. Then $uv = b^x b^y = b^{x+y}$, which says $$\log_b(uv) = \log_b u + \log_b v.$$ Test it: $\log_2 4 + \log_2 8 = 2 + 3 = 5 = \log_2 32$, and $4 \cdot 8 = 32$. For a power, $u^k = (b^x)^k = b^{xk}$, which says $$\log_b(u^k) = k \log_b u.$$ Test it: $\log_3 81 = 4$, and $81 = 9^2$, so $2 \log_3 9 = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$.

To solve $a \cdot b^t = c$, divide by $a$ first, which leaves $b^t = \dfrac{c}{a}$. If $\dfrac{c}{a}$ is a power of $b$ you can see $t$ at once. For $6 \cdot 5^t = 750$, dividing gives $5^t = 125 = 5^3$, so $t = 3$ exactly. If it is not, take the logarithm of both sides. The power rule turns $\log(b^t)$ into $t \log b$, so $$t = \frac{\log(c/a)}{\log b}.$$ Here $\log$ is the key on a calculator, which the gate exams allow. For $4 \cdot 3^t = 100$, dividing gives $3^t = 25$, and $t = \dfrac{\log 25}{\log 3} \approx 2.93$. The answer is about $2.93$, not exactly, and it is sensible: $3^2 = 9$ is under $25$ and $3^3 = 27$ is just over, so $t$ is a little under $3$. That quotient is the change of base: $\log_b y = \dfrac{\log y}{\log b}$ is the same fact (take $t = \log_b y$, so $b^t = y$), and it lets a calculator find a logarithm in any base. When the exam asks for an exact answer that is not whole, it asks for the expression, or for the two whole numbers the logarithm lies between.

Example 7. An exact solve from a growth story

A lab culture starts with $25$ bacteria and the number triples every hour. After how many hours are there exactly $2{,}025$ bacteria?

  1. The multiplier is $3$ and the start is $25$, so the count after $t$ hours is $25 \cdot 3^t$. Set it equal to the target: $25 \cdot 3^t = 2{,}025$.
  2. Divide by $25$: $3^t = 81$.
  3. Since $81 = 3^4$, $t = \log_3 81 = 4$ hours.
  4. Check by listing: $25 \to 75 \to 225 \to 675 \to 2{,}025$ is $4$ triplings ✓. Check with the power: $25 \cdot 3^4 = 25 \cdot 81 = 2{,}025$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Dividing by the start comes first. Taking a logarithm of $25 \cdot 3^t$ before dividing would leave a sum of logarithms to untangle.

Evaluate $\log_4 64$.

Answer: $3$

The question is which exponent $t$ has $4^t = 64$. Listing powers, $4, 16, 64$ reaches $64$ on the third, so $t = 3$. Check: $4 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 64$ ✓.

Example 8. A doubling time, set up for the calculator

A town of $9{,}000$ people grows by $4\%$ a year. After how many years does it reach $18{,}000$? Give the exact answer as an expression, estimate it, and check the estimate.

  1. The multiplier is $1.04$, so $9{,}000 \cdot 1.04^t = 18{,}000$. Divide by $9{,}000$: $1.04^t = 2$.
  2. The number $2$ is not a whole power of $1.04$, so take the logarithm of both sides: $t \log 1.04 = \log 2$. The exact answer is $t = \dfrac{\log 2}{\log 1.04}$ years.
  3. On a calculator, $\log 2 \approx 0.3010$ and $\log 1.04 \approx 0.01703$, so $t \approx 17.7$ years.
  4. Check the estimate with two powers: $1.04^{17} \approx 1.948$ is under $2$ and $1.04^{18} \approx 2.026$ is over, so $t$ lies between $17$ and $18$ ✓. The rule of $72$ gives $72 \div 4 = 18$, close to it ✓. $\blacksquare$

The starting size drops out: any population that grows by $4\%$ a year takes the same time to double.

Recap

Back to the puzzle

The patch covered half the pond on day $29$. It doubles every day, so the day before it was full it was half as big: run the doubling backward one day, from the whole pond on day $30$ to half the pond on day $29$. A second route: if half the pond is covered on day $29$, one doubling makes the whole pond on day $30$ ✓. The tempting day $15$ treats the story as linear, where half of the time gives half of the pond. In a doubling story, running the doubling back $15$ more days from day $29$, halving each time, leaves the patch at $\frac{1}{2^{15}}$ of the pond on day $15$. Each day the patch adds as much as the whole patch had the day before. Half the time is the linear answer to an exponential story.

  • A sequence is a list with a rule, which can be said step to step (recursive) or straight from the position (explicit); it is a function whose inputs are the positions $1, 2, 3, \dots$.
  • Arithmetic adds the common difference $d$ each step: $a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$, since the $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps from the first. Geometric multiplies by the common ratio $r$: $a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}$.
  • To tell them apart, take differences, then ratios; a list where neither is constant is neither.
  • A value is a term of an arithmetic sequence only when solving $a_1 + (n - 1)d = v$ gives a whole $n$.
  • A missing middle term is the mean of its neighbors in an arithmetic sequence, and the square root of their product (taking the sign the problem gives when it says the terms are positive or negative; two neighbors of opposite sign have no real middle term) in a geometric one.
  • Pairing the first and last, the second and second-to-last, and so on, sums an arithmetic list: half the number of terms times the sum of the first and last.
  • Linear change adds the same amount each period, $y = a + dt$. Exponential change multiplies by the same factor each period, $y = a \cdot b^{\,t}$, with $a$ the value at $t = 0$.
  • Up $r\%$ is the multiplier $1 + \frac{r}{100}$ and down $r\%$ is $1 - \frac{r}{100}$; read the percent back from $b$, so $1.08$ is up $8\%$ and $0.85$ is down $15\%$. Doubling is $b = 2$, halving is $b = \frac12$, and a half-life is the period of halving.
  • An exponential quantity passes a linear one however they start, when the multiplier is above $1$, because its increases are a share of a growing amount and so keep growing.
  • Compound interest is growth with money: $A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^{t}$, since each year's interest is a share of a growing balance. Simple interest adds $\frac{Pr}{100}$ each year, a linear model, and the gap between the two is interest on interest.
  • A yearly rate of $r\%$ compounded $n$ times a year is $P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100n}\right)^{nt}$: the rate per period times the number of periods. More often is a little more, because the gain is interest on the within-year interest. In a formula $a \cdot b^{\,t}$, the principal is $a$ and the rate is $b - 1$ as a percent.
  • The years to double are found by listing powers of the multiplier until one passes $2$. The rule of $72$ ($72 \div r$ years) is an estimate only.
  • $\log_b y$ is the exponent $t$ with $b^t = y$, so $\log_2 32 = 5$, $\log_5 1 = 0$, $\log_9 3 = \frac12$, and a number below $1$ has a negative logarithm when $b > 1$.
  • The product rule $\log_b(uv) = \log_b u + \log_b v$ comes from $b^x b^y = b^{x+y}$, and the power rule $\log_b(u^k) = k \log_b u$ from $(b^x)^k = b^{xk}$.
  • To solve $a \cdot b^t = c$, divide by $a$, then read a power of $b$ for an exact answer or take logs of both sides: $t = \dfrac{\log(c/a)}{\log b}$, which is the change of base, $\log_b y = \dfrac{\log y}{\log b}$. A calculator answer is “about”; two bracketing powers check it.
