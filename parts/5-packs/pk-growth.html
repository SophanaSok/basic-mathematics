Growth, Decay and Sequences

A list of numbers with a rule behind it is a sequence, and two rules cover most of what the gate exams ask: add the same amount each step, or multiply by the same amount each step. This pack starts with those two sequences, how to say the rule step by step and how to say it from the position, and how to reach a far term, find which position holds a value and fill in a missing term. Its second section turns the two sequences into two kinds of change: a constant difference per period is linear, a constant ratio per period is exponential, and a percent change is a multiplier. It reads and writes growth and decay models, explains why an exponential quantity passes a linear one in the end, and counts the periods until a value is reached. Compound interest and solving with logarithms come in later sections of the pack.

A puzzle to carry through the pack

A patch of lily pads on a pond doubles in size every day. On day $30$ it covers the whole pond. On which day did it cover half the pond?

  • day $15$
  • day $29$
  • day $20$

Half of the time feels like the right place for half of the pond, and that feeling is the one a steady, linear story would earn. A story that doubles is a different kind of story, and the two sections that follow say how it differs: the first builds the two sequences, adding and multiplying, and the second sets the multiplying one against the adding one. (Later sections of this pack turn to compound interest and to solving with logarithms.)

By the end of this pack you can tell an arithmetic sequence from a geometric one by taking differences and then ratios, write the rule both ways (from step to step, and as a formula for the $n$th term), and use the formula to find a far term, to find which position holds a value, to fill in a missing middle term and to add a short arithmetic list by pairing its ends. You can tell linear change from exponential change, turn a percent increase or decrease into a multiplier, read the starting value and the multiplier from a model or a table and the percent per period from the multiplier, write a growth or decay model from a story, say why an exponential quantity overtakes a linear one however the two begin, and count the periods until a value is reached by listing.

Three quick ones to wake up what the pack stands on. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution says where to look.

Evaluate $5 \cdot 2^3$.

Answer: $40$

The power comes first: $2^3 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$, and $5 \cdot 8 = 40$. Check: $5 \cdot 2 = 10$, $10 \cdot 2 = 20$, $20 \cdot 2 = 40$ ✓.

Evaluate $7 + 4(n - 1)$ when $n = 6$.

Answer: $27$

$n - 1 = 5$, then $4 \cdot 5 = 20$ and $7 + 20 = 27$. Check by spreading the $4$ first: $7 + 4 \cdot 6 - 4 \cdot 1 = 7 + 24 - 4 = 27$ ✓.

A price of $\$80$ goes up by $25\%$. What is the new price in dollars?

Answer: $\$100$

$25\%$ of $80$ is $20$, and $80 + 20 = 100$. Check another way: a rise of $25\%$ leaves $125\%$ of the price, and $1.25 \cdot 80 = 100$ ✓. That second way, one multiplication by $1.25$, is the idea of the second section.

G.1 Arithmetic and geometric sequences

A sequence is a list of numbers with a rule behind it. Its entries are the terms, written $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, where $a_n$ is the term in position $n$. A sequence is a function whose inputs are the positions $1, 2, 3, \dots$, and the rule can be said two ways. The step-to-step way (the recursive form) says how to get from one term to the next: “add $3$ each time.” The straight-from-the-position way (the explicit form) says the term at once: “the $n$th term is $3n + 2$.”

An arithmetic sequence adds the same number, the common difference $d$, at every step. The $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps away from the first, since position $1$ is no steps away, position $2$ is one step and position $n$ is $n - 1$ steps. Each step adds $d$, so $$a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d.$$ Test it: $n = 1$ gives $a_1$, and $n = 2$ gives $a_1 + d$. A geometric sequence multiplies by the same number, the common ratio $r$, at every step. Again the $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps from the first, and each step multiplies by $r$, so $$a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}.$$ Test it the same way: $n = 1$ gives $a_1 \cdot r^0 = a_1$, and $n = 2$ gives $a_1 r$.

To tell which kind a list is, take differences first and then ratios. If the differences between neighbors are all the same, the list is arithmetic. If the ratios are all the same, it is geometric. A list where neither is constant is neither, so $1, 2, 4, 7, 11$ (differences $1, 2, 3, 4$) is not arithmetic and not geometric. These formulas do four jobs.

  • The next term, or a far one without listing everything before it: put the position in the formula.
  • Which position holds a given value: in an arithmetic sequence, solve $a_1 + (n - 1)d = v$ for $n$, and check that $n$ is a whole number, since a value that gives $n = 15.83$ is not a term of the sequence at all.
  • A missing middle term between two neighbors: in an arithmetic sequence it is the mean of its neighbors, since it sits the same distance from each; in a geometric sequence it is the square root of their product, with the sign the sequence shows, since the ratio from the first to the middle equals the ratio from the middle to the last.
  • The common difference from two terms that are not neighbors: from position $i$ to position $j$ is $j - i$ steps, so $d = \dfrac{a_j - a_i}{j - i}$.

One more use is the sum of an arithmetic list, which pairing makes short. Take $2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17$. The first and last add to $19$, the second and second-to-last add to $5 + 14 = 19$, and the middle pair adds to $8 + 11 = 19$. Every pair has the same sum, there are $3$ pairs, and the total is $3 \cdot 19 = 57$. In general the sum is the number of terms, halved, times the sum of the first and last. Check by adding in order: $2 + 5 = 7$, then $15$, $26$, $40$, and $57$ ✓. The book argues the same idea for the numbers $1$ to $100$ in Chapter 15, summations, and its geometric series add up a geometric list. This is where the topic continues, and nothing here depends on it.

Example 1. A far term, and which position

The sequence $5, 11, 17, 23, \dots$ is arithmetic. Find its $12$th term, and say whether $107$ and $100$ are terms.

  1. The differences are $11 - 5 = 6$, $17 - 11 = 6$ and $23 - 17 = 6$, so $a_1 = 5$ and $d = 6$.
  2. The $12$th term is $11$ steps from the first: $a_{12} = 5 + 11 \cdot 6 = 5 + 66 = 71$.
  3. For $107$, solve $5 + (n - 1) \cdot 6 = 107$: $(n - 1) \cdot 6 = 102$, so $n - 1 = 17$ and $n = 18$. The value $107$ is the $18$th term.
  4. For $100$: $(n - 1) \cdot 6 = 95$ gives $n - 1 = \frac{95}{6}$, which is not a whole number. So $100$ is not a term.
  5. Check against a list: $5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59, 65, 71$ has $12$ terms and ends at $71$ ✓. Check the position by counting steps: from $5$ up to $107$ is $102$, which is exactly $17$ steps of $6$, so $107$ is the term $17$ steps after the first, the $18$th ✓. $\blacksquare$

A value is a term only when the number of steps comes out whole.

The sequence $9, 13, 17, 21, \dots$ is arithmetic. What is its $15$th term?

Answer: $65$

The difference is $4$ and $a_1 = 9$, so $a_{15} = 9 + 14 \cdot 4 = 9 + 56 = 65$. Check by listing: $9, 13, 17, 21, 25, 29, 33, 37, 41, 45, 49, 53, 57, 61, 65$ has $15$ terms and ends at $65$ ✓.

Write the $n$th term of the arithmetic sequence $2, 7, 12, 17, \dots$ as an expression in $n$.

Answer: $5n - 3$

Here $a_1 = 2$ and $d = 5$, so $a_n = 2 + 5(n - 1) = 2 + 5n - 5 = 5n - 3$. Check against the list: $n = 1$ gives $2$, $n = 2$ gives $7$, and $n = 4$ gives $5 \cdot 4 - 3 = 17$ ✓.

Example 2. A geometric term, and a missing term

The sequence $3, 6, 12, 24, \dots$ is geometric. Find its $8$th term. Then find the missing middle term in $2,\ \square,\ 50$, a geometric sequence of positive numbers.

  1. The ratios are $6 \div 3 = 2$, $12 \div 6 = 2$ and $24 \div 12 = 2$, so $a_1 = 3$ and $r = 2$.
  2. The $8$th term is $7$ steps from the first: $a_8 = 3 \cdot 2^7 = 3 \cdot 128 = 384$.
  3. For the missing term, the ratio from the first to the middle equals the ratio from the middle to the last, so the middle term $m$ satisfies $m^2 = 2 \cdot 50 = 100$. It is positive, so $m = 10$.
  4. Check the first by listing: $3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192, 384$ has $8$ terms and ends at $384$ ✓. Check the second by ratios: $10 \div 2 = 5$ and $50 \div 10 = 5$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Had the problem allowed negative terms, $2, -10, 50$ would also be geometric, with ratio $-5$. The sign the sequence shows decides which root to take.

The sequence $1, 4, 16, 64, \dots$ is geometric. What is its $6$th term?

Answer: $1024$

The ratio is $4$ and $a_1 = 1$, so $a_6 = 1 \cdot 4^5 = 1024$. Check by listing: $1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024$ has $6$ terms and ends at $1024$ ✓.

G.2 Exponential growth and decay

The two kinds of sequence become the two kinds of change. When a quantity gains the same amount in every period, it changes linearly: after $t$ periods it is $y = a + dt$, the arithmetic rule with the period count in place of the position. When it is multiplied by the same factor in every period, it changes exponentially: $$y = a \cdot b^{\,t}.$$ Here $a$ is the value at $t = 0$, and $b$ is the multiplier for one period. The multiplier has to be read from the story. A rise of $r\%$ keeps the whole amount and adds $r\%$ of it, so the multiplier is $b = 1 + \frac{r}{100}$; a fall of $r\%$ leaves $b = 1 - \frac{r}{100}$. (Pack A is where this began, with repeated percent changes.) Doubling is $b = 2$, tripling is $b = 3$, and halving is $b = \frac12$. Decay is the same formula with $b < 1$, and a half-life is the length of the period in which a quantity halves.

The same facts read the other way. To read a model $y = a \cdot b^{\,t}$, take $a$ from the value at $t = 0$ and $b$ from the ratio of two values one period apart. Then the percent per period comes from $b$: $1.08$ is a rise of $8\%$, and $0.85$ is a fall of $15\%$. To write a model from a story, name the starting value, find the multiplier for one period, and say what $t$ counts.

Why does an exponential quantity pass a linear one, however large the linear gain and however small the multiplier above $1$? Each period's increase of an exponential quantity is a fixed share of an amount that keeps growing, so the increases themselves keep growing, while a linear quantity gains the same amount every time. A table shows it. Plan A starts at $100$ and gains $30$ each week; Plan B starts at only $20$ and doubles.

Plan B is far behind for three weeks and ahead in week $4$. Its gains are $20, 40, 80, 160$, each double the one before, while Plan A's gain is $30$ every time. With a multiplier such as $1.1$ the pass takes far longer, but it comes, because a share of a growing amount outgrows any fixed gain. To find how many periods a quantity needs to reach a value, list the amounts period by period until one passes it. Logarithms, in a later section, turn that search into one step.

Example 3. A growth story, and the percent read back

A culture of $320$ yeast cells grows by $25\%$ every hour. How many cells are there after $3$ hours? Then, from the counts of $400$ and $500$ cells in the first two hours, read the percent back.

  1. A rise of $25\%$ gives the multiplier $b = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25$, and the start is $a = 320$, so $y = 320 \cdot 1.25^t$.
  2. After $3$ hours: $y = 320 \cdot 1.25^3 = 320 \cdot 1.953125 = 625$ cells.
  3. The counts in the first two hours are $400$ and $500$. Their ratio is $500 \div 400 = 1.25$, which is $1 + 0.25$, a rise of $25\%$ per hour.
  4. Check period by period: $320 \cdot 1.25 = 400$, $400 \cdot 1.25 = 500$ and $500 \cdot 1.25 = 625$ ✓. The percent read back from the multiplier $1.25$ is $25\%$, the story's ✓. $\blacksquare$

Growing by $25\%$ three times is not growing by $75\%$ once: the second rise is $25\%$ of a larger amount, and $320 \cdot 1.75 = 560$ is short of $625$.

A video has $200$ views today, and its views triple each day. How many views does it have after $3$ days?

Answer: $5{,}400$ views

The multiplier is $3$, so $y = 200 \cdot 3^3 = 200 \cdot 27 = 5{,}400$. Check day by day: $200$, then $600$, then $1{,}800$, then $5{,}400$ ✓.

Example 4. Decay and a half-life

A sample of $640$ grams of a tracer has a half-life of $3$ days. How much is left after $12$ days? After how many days is less than $10$ grams left?

  1. The period is $3$ days and the multiplier per period is $\frac12$. Twelve days is $12 \div 3 = 4$ periods, so $y = 640 \cdot \left(\frac12\right)^4 = 640 \div 16 = 40$ grams.
  2. For the second question, list the amount after each period: $640, 320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, 5$. The amount is $10$ after $6$ periods, which is not less than $10$, and $5$ after $7$ periods, which is.
  3. Seven periods is $7 \cdot 3 = 21$ days.
  4. Check the first answer by halving: $640 \to 320 \to 160 \to 80 \to 40$ is $4$ halvings ✓. Check the second by the power: $640 \div 2^7 = 640 \div 128 = 5 < 10$, and $640 \div 2^6 = 10$, not less ✓. $\blacksquare$

The unit of $t$ is the period, so the number of days must be changed into a number of half-lives before the power is taken.

A herd shrank from $1{,}200$ animals to $900$ in one year. By what percent does it shrink each year? Give the percent without the percent sign.

Answer: $25$

The multiplier is $900 \div 1{,}200 = 0.75$, which is $1 - 0.25$, a fall of $25\%$. Check: $25\%$ of $1{,}200$ is $300$, and $1{,}200 - 300 = 900$ ✓.

A savings jar holds $\$3$ today (day $0$) and the amount in it doubles each day. On which day is it first more than $\$100$?

Answer: day $6$

The amounts are $3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192$ on days $0$ through $6$, and $96$ is under $100$ while $192$ is over. Check by the power: $3 \cdot 2^5 = 96 < 100$ and $3 \cdot 2^6 = 192 > 100$ ✓.

Practice

Give each answer as a number in the unit the question names, without writing the unit and without a comma. Where a question asks for an expression, use the letter it names. A question that asks which formula or which model fits is answered by choosing one of the options shown, since a formula or an equation is never typed.

A theater has $18$ seats in the first row, and each row behind it has $2$ more seats than the row in front. How many seats are in the $25$th row?

Answer: $66$ seats

The $25$th row is $24$ steps from the first: $a_{25} = 18 + 24 \cdot 2 = 18 + 48 = 66$. Check through the middle: the $10$th row has $18 + 9 \cdot 2 = 36$ seats, and the $15$ rows after it add $15 \cdot 2 = 30$, which makes $66$ ✓.

A stack of logs has $31$ logs in the bottom layer, and each layer above it has $3$ fewer logs than the layer below. Which layer, counting the bottom layer as layer $1$, has $4$ logs?

Answer: layer $10$

Solve $31 - 3(n - 1) = 4$: $3(n - 1) = 27$, so $n - 1 = 9$ and $n = 10$. Check by listing: $31, 28, 25, 22, 19, 16, 13, 10, 7, 4$ has $4$ in the $10$th place ✓.

A ball rebounds to the same fraction of its previous height after every bounce. Two bounces reach $72$ centimeters and $8$ centimeters, with exactly one bounce between them. How high, in centimeters, is the bounce between them?

Answer: $24$ centimeters

The middle height $m$ satisfies $m^2 = 72 \cdot 8 = 576$, and heights are positive, so $m = 24$. Check by ratios: $24 \div 72 = \frac13$ and $8 \div 24 = \frac13$ ✓. Check another way: two steps shrink $72$ to $8$, a ratio of $\frac{8}{72} = \frac19$ for the two steps, so one step is $\frac13$ and $72 \cdot \frac13 = 24$ ✓.

In an arithmetic sequence the $3$rd term is $17$ and the $8$th term is $52$. What is the common difference?

Answer: $7$

From the $3$rd term to the $8$th is $5$ steps, and the terms differ by $52 - 17 = 35$, so $d = 35 \div 5 = 7$. Check by listing from the $3$rd term: $17, 24, 31, 38, 45, 52$ reaches the $8$th term after $5$ steps ✓.

A game gives $10$ points for level $1$, $16$ points for level $2$, $22$ points for level $3$, and so on, with the same increase each level. Write the points for level $n$ as an expression in $n$.

Answer: $6n + 4$

Here $a_1 = 10$ and $d = 6$, so $a_n = 10 + 6(n - 1) = 10 + 6n - 6 = 6n + 4$. Check against the list: $n = 1$ gives $10$, $n = 2$ gives $16$ and $n = 3$ gives $22$ ✓.

A display of cans has $8$ rows. The top row holds $12$ cans, each row down holds $3$ more than the one above, and the bottom row holds $33$. How many cans are in the display?

Answer: $180$ cans

The first and last rows hold $12 + 33 = 45$ cans between them, and so does every pair that is the same distance from each end. The $8$ rows make $4$ pairs, so the total is $4 \cdot 45 = 180$. Check by adding the rows in order: $12, 27, 45, 66, 90, 117, 147, 180$ ✓.

The geometric sequence $3, 6, 12, 24, \dots$ is described by a formula for its $n$th term. Which formula gives it?

  • $3 \cdot 2^{n}$
  • $2 \cdot 3^{n-1}$
  • $3 \cdot 2^{n-1}$
  • $3 + 3(n - 1)$

Answer: $3 \cdot 2^{n-1}$

The first term is $3$ and the ratio is $2$, which is the third option. The first option gives $6$ at $n = 1$, one multiplication too many. The second swaps the first term and the ratio, giving $2$ at $n = 1$. The last is the arithmetic formula for a list that adds $3$ each time. Check the chosen formula: $n = 1$ gives $3$, $n = 3$ gives $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ and $n = 4$ gives $3 \cdot 8 = 24$ ✓.

A culture starts with $250$ bacteria and grows by $20\%$ every hour. How many bacteria are in it after $3$ hours?

Answer: $432$ bacteria

The multiplier is $1.2$, so $y = 250 \cdot 1.2^3 = 250 \cdot 1.728 = 432$. Check hour by hour: $250 \to 300 \to 360 \to 432$ ✓.

A printing press is worth $\$48{,}000$ new and loses $25\%$ of its value each year. What is it worth, in dollars, after $3$ years?

Answer: $\$20{,}250$

The multiplier is $1 - 0.25 = 0.75$, so $y = 48{,}000 \cdot 0.75^3 = 48{,}000 \cdot 0.421875 = 20{,}250$. Check year by year: $48{,}000 \to 36{,}000 \to 27{,}000 \to 20{,}250$ ✓. The value fell, as a decay should.

A forest had $4{,}000$ trees one year and $4{,}400$ trees the next. If it keeps changing by the same percent each year, by what percent does it grow each year? Give the percent without the percent sign.

Answer: $10$

The multiplier is $4{,}400 \div 4{,}000 = 1.1$, which is a rise of $10\%$. Check: $10\%$ of $4{,}000$ is $400$, and $4{,}000 + 400 = 4{,}400$ ✓.

A flock of birds grows by $20\%$ each year. After $2$ years it numbers $720$. How many birds were in it at the start?

Answer: $500$ birds

Two years multiply the start by $1.2^2 = 1.44$, so the start is $720 \div 1.44 = 500$. Check forward: $500 \cdot 1.2 = 600$ and $600 \cdot 1.2 = 720$ ✓.

A culture of $5$ cells triples every hour. After how many hours is it first over $1{,}000$ cells?

Answer: $5$ hours

The counts are $5, 15, 45, 135, 405, 1{,}215$ after $0$ through $5$ hours, and $405$ is under $1{,}000$ while $1{,}215$ is over. Check by the power: $5 \cdot 3^4 = 405 < 1{,}000$ and $5 \cdot 3^5 = 1{,}215 > 1{,}000$ ✓.

Channel A has $100$ subscribers today (week $0$) and gains $50$ each week. Channel B has $4$ subscribers today and doubles each week. In which week does Channel B first have more subscribers than Channel A?

Answer: week $7$

Channel A has $100 + 50t$ and Channel B has $4 \cdot 2^t$. In week $6$ they are $400$ and $256$, and in week $7$ they are $450$ and $512$, so B is first ahead in week $7$. Check the earlier weeks one at a time: A leads with $100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350$ against B's $4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128$ in weeks $0$ through $5$ ✓.

Recap

Back to the puzzle

The patch covered half the pond on day $29$. It doubles every day, so the day before it was full it was half as big: run the doubling backward one day, from the whole pond on day $30$ to half the pond on day $29$. A second route: if half the pond is covered on day $29$, one doubling makes the whole pond on day $30$ ✓. The tempting day $15$ treats the story as linear, where half of the time gives half of the pond. In a doubling story the pond is only $\frac{1}{2^{15}}$ of its final size on day $15$, because the last day alone adds as much as all the days before it put together. Half the time is the linear answer to an exponential story.

  • A sequence is a list with a rule, which can be said step to step (recursive) or straight from the position (explicit); it is a function whose inputs are the positions $1, 2, 3, \dots$.
  • Arithmetic adds the common difference $d$ each step: $a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$, since the $n$th term is $n - 1$ steps from the first. Geometric multiplies by the common ratio $r$: $a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}$.
  • To tell them apart, take differences, then ratios; a list where neither is constant is neither.
  • A value is a term of an arithmetic sequence only when solving $a_1 + (n - 1)d = v$ gives a whole $n$.
  • A missing middle term is the mean of its neighbors in an arithmetic sequence, and the square root of their product (with the sign the sequence shows) in a geometric one.
  • Pairing the first and last, the second and second-to-last, and so on, sums an arithmetic list: half the number of terms times the sum of the first and last.
  • Linear change adds the same amount each period, $y = a + dt$. Exponential change multiplies by the same factor each period, $y = a \cdot b^{\,t}$, with $a$ the value at $t = 0$.
  • Up $r\%$ is the multiplier $1 + \frac{r}{100}$ and down $r\%$ is $1 - \frac{r}{100}$; read the percent back from $b$, so $1.08$ is up $8\%$ and $0.85$ is down $15\%$. Doubling is $b = 2$, halving is $b = \frac12$, and a half-life is the period of halving.
  • An exponential quantity passes a linear one however they start, because its increases are a share of a growing amount and so keep growing.
