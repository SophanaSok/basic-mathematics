Chance and Counting

Chance sounds vague, but when the outcomes are equally likely it comes down to counting. This pack says what a probability is and why it is a fraction, how to find the chance that something does not happen, how an experiment estimates a probability that counting would give exactly, and how to count the outcomes of two steps without missing any.

A puzzle to carry through the pack

Two fair coins are flipped. Alex reasons: “There are three things that can happen: two heads, one head, or no heads. So the chance of two heads is $\frac13$.” Is Alex right?

  • Yes: the chance of two heads is $\frac13$
  • No: the chance of two heads is $\frac14$
  • No: the chance of two heads is $\frac12$

Alex counted the outcomes, which is the right idea, and still got a wrong answer. The first section says what a probability counts. (The second section is about listing the outcomes of two steps, so that you never miscount them.)

By the end of this pack you can find the probability of an event as the number of favorable outcomes over the number of equally likely outcomes, find the probability that it does not happen, tell an experimental probability from a theoretical one, predict how many times an event should occur in a number of trials, list the outcomes of two steps, and find the probability that two events both happen, that one or the other happens, or that two draws without replacement both go a given way.
Before you start

This pack leans on fractions as single numbers (Chapter 1) and on the words “and”, “or” and “not” as they are used for sets (the Interlude). You need no other part of the course, and you can read the pack at any point alongside it.

Three quick ones to wake up what the pack stands on. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution says where to look.

Write $\dfrac{6}{20}$ in lowest terms.

Answer: $\dfrac{3}{10}$

The largest number that divides both is $2$, and $\frac{6}{20} = \frac{3}{10}$. See §1.5. A probability is a fraction, and it is usually written this way.

Compute $1 - \dfrac{3}{8}$.

Answer: $\dfrac58$

Write $1 = \frac88$, so $\frac88 - \frac38 = \frac58$. Check: $\frac38 + \frac58 = \frac88 = 1$ ✓. This is exactly how “not” is computed in the first section.

What is $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of $30$?

Answer: $12$

$30 \div 5 = 6$ and $2 \cdot 6 = 12$, so $\frac25 \cdot 30 = 12$. Check: $12$ out of $30$ reduces to $\frac25$ ✓. This is how an expected count is found.

C.1 Probability as a fraction of outcomes

An experiment is anything with an uncertain result: flipping a coin, rolling a die, drawing a marble from a bag. Each possible result is an outcome, and the list of all of them is the sample space. For a die it is $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$. An event is a set of outcomes we care about, such as “an even number”, which is $2, 4, 6$. Suppose the outcomes are equally likely: each is as likely as any other. Then there is no reason to favor any of them, so each gets the same share of the chance, and with $N$ outcomes that share is $\frac1N$. An event with $k$ outcomes in it gets $k$ shares, and so $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{number of outcomes in all}} = \frac{k}{N}.$$ That is the whole idea, and the care goes into the counting. The chance of an even number on a die is $\frac36 = \frac12$. The fraction is never negative and never more than $1$, because $k$ is between $0$ and $N$. A probability of $0$ means the event cannot happen, a probability of $1$ means it must, and the same number written as a percent says it per hundred: $\frac14$ is $25\%$. A spinner needs one extra thought. If it is cut into $8$ equal sections, three red and five blue, the outcomes to count are the eight sections, not the two colors: the chance of red is $\frac38$, not $\frac12$.

Every outcome is either in the event or out of it. So the favorable outcomes and the others together make all $N$, which says that $P(\text{event}) + P(\text{not the event}) = 1$. This is the complement rule: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. It is a shortcut whenever “not $A$” is the hard thing to count: the chance of rain is $35\%$, so the chance of no rain is $100\% - 35\% = 65\%$. These are the set words of the Interlude at work, and “not” means “everything else”.

Counting gives the theoretical probability, what the setup says should happen. You can also run the experiment and see. The experimental probability is the relative frequency, the number of times the event happened over the number of trials. If a coin lands heads $46$ times in $80$ tosses, the experimental probability of heads is $\frac{46}{80}$, which is a bit above the theoretical $\frac12$. Nothing is wrong with the coin. Chance wanders, and short runs wander the most. The more trials there are, the more the relative frequency tends to settle near the theoretical value, and that is why a large survey can be trusted more than a small one, and why a theoretical value, when you can find it, is better than a few trials.

The same fraction predicts counts. In $n$ trials of an event with probability $p$, the expected count is $p \cdot n$. In $60$ rolls of a die, a six has probability $\frac16$, so we expect $\frac16 \cdot 60 = 10$ sixes. “Expect” is not “get”. It is the average over many such runs, and a single run of $60$ rolls may well give $8$ or $13$.

Example 1. Counting outcomes, and the complement

A bag holds $9$ red, $6$ blue and $5$ yellow marbles. One is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is blue, the probability that it is not blue, and the first as a percent.

  1. The sample space is the marbles in the bag, all equally likely: $9 + 6 + 5 = 20$ outcomes.
  2. The favorable outcomes are the blue marbles: $6$. So $P(\text{blue}) = \frac{6}{20} = \frac{3}{10}$.
  3. By the complement rule, $P(\text{not blue}) = 1 - \frac{3}{10} = \frac{7}{10}$.
  4. As a percent, $\frac{3}{10} = \frac{30}{100} = 30\%$.
  5. Check by counting the others: $9 + 5 = 14$ marbles are not blue, and $\frac{14}{20} = \frac{7}{10}$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The answer $\frac{6}{20}$ is also correct. We reduce it only because the smaller fraction is easier to compare.

A fair die has $8$ faces numbered $1$ to $8$. It is rolled once. What is the probability that it shows a number greater than $5$?

Answer: $\dfrac38$

The outcomes greater than $5$ are $6, 7, 8$: three of the eight. So the probability is $\frac38$. Check: the others, $1$ to $5$, are five faces, and $\frac38 + \frac58 = 1$ ✓.

Example 2. Experiment, theory and expected count

A spinner has $8$ equal sections: $3$ red, $4$ blue and $1$ gold. It is spun $40$ times and lands on red $14$ times. Find the experimental probability of red, the theoretical probability of red, and the number of reds expected in $40$ spins.

  1. Experimental: the event happened $14$ times in $40$ trials, so the relative frequency is $\frac{14}{40} = \frac{7}{20}$, or $35\%$.
  2. Theoretical: $3$ of the $8$ equal sections are red, so $P(\text{red}) = \frac38$.
  3. Expected count: $\frac38 \cdot 40 = 15$, since $40 \div 8 = 5$ and $3 \cdot 5 = 15$.
  4. Compare: the run gave $14$, one short of the $15$ expected. That is the ordinary wandering of chance, not a sign that the spinner is unfair ✓. $\blacksquare$

Here the experimental $\frac{7}{20}$ is $0.35$ and the theoretical $\frac38$ is $0.375$. With $400$ spins the two would usually be closer.

The forecast gives a $35\%$ chance of rain tomorrow. What is the chance, as a percent, that it does not rain?

Answer: $65\%$

$100\% - 35\% = 65\%$. Check: $35\% + 65\% = 100\%$ ✓.

The probability that a spinner lands on blue is $\dfrac25$. If it is spun $60$ times, how many blues do you expect?

Answer: $24$

$\frac25 \cdot 60$: $60 \div 5 = 12$, and $2 \cdot 12 = 24$. Check: $24$ out of $60$ reduces to $\frac25$ ✓.

C.2 Compound events

Many events take two steps: flip a coin and roll a die, draw a card and then another. The way not to lose outcomes is to list them in an organized way, one step after the other. For two coins the list is $HH, HT, TH, TT$. That is four equally likely outcomes, and the answer to the puzzle: “one head” is two of them, $HT$ and $TH$, so it is not one outcome to count beside the others. The chance of two heads is $\frac14$. A table does the same job: rows for the first step, columns for the second, one cell for each pair. A tree draws it as branches, and the number of pairs is the number of choices at the first step times the number at the second. A coin and a die make $2 \cdot 6 = 12$ pairs.

Now ask for “heads and an even number”. In the table those are $H2, H4, H6$: $3$ of $12$, which is $\frac14$. The same answer comes without the table. Half of the pairs have heads, and of those, half have an even die, so the chance is $\frac12$ of $\frac12$, which is a product. When the two steps do not affect each other (the events are independent), $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. The reason is the one just given: $P(B)$ is the share of the second step's outcomes in $B$, and that share is the same whatever the first step did, so it takes that share of the share $P(A)$.

For “or”, adding is the right first move but it counts twice any outcome that is in both events. For $A$ or $B$, the outcomes in both are counted once in $A$ and once in $B$, so we take them away once: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$. If the two events cannot happen together, there is nothing to take away. This is the same overlap rule as for counting the members of two sets.

The last case is where the steps do affect each other. Draw two marbles from a bag without replacement, which means the first stays out. The second draw is made from a smaller bag, so its fraction has changed. With $5$ red and $3$ blue marbles, the chance that the first is red is $\frac58$. Given that, $4$ red remain among $7$ marbles, so the chance that the second is red is $\frac47$. The chance of both is the product, $\frac58 \cdot \frac47$. If the first marble were put back, the second fraction would stay $\frac58$.

Example 3. A probability with “or”

Cards numbered $1$ to $20$ are shuffled and one is drawn. What is the probability that its number is a multiple of $3$ or a multiple of $5$?

  1. Multiples of $3$: $3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18$, which is $6$ cards. Multiples of $5$: $5, 10, 15, 20$, which is $4$ cards.
  2. The card $15$ is in both lists, so adding $6 + 4$ counts it twice. Cards in either list: $6 + 4 - 1 = 9$.
  3. There are $20$ equally likely cards, so the probability is $\frac{9}{20}$.
  4. Check by listing the cards once each: $3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20$ is $9$ numbers ✓. $\blacksquare$

In probabilities, the same steps read $\frac{6}{20} + \frac{4}{20} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{9}{20}$.

A fair coin is flipped and a fair six-sided die is rolled. What is the probability of tails and an odd number?

Answer: $\dfrac14$

$P(\text{tails}) = \frac12$ and $P(\text{odd}) = \frac36 = \frac12$, so the probability of both is $\frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14$. Check: in the table, $T1, T3, T5$ are $3$ of $12$ pairs, and $\frac{3}{12} = \frac14$ ✓.

Example 4. Two draws without replacement

A bag holds $5$ red and $3$ blue marbles. Two are drawn one after the other without putting the first back. What is the probability that both are red?

  1. The first draw is red with probability $\frac58$.
  2. Given that, $4$ red marbles are left among $7$, so the second is red with probability $\frac47$.
  3. Both happen: $\frac58 \cdot \frac47 = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14}$.
  4. Check by counting ordered pairs of different marbles: $8 \cdot 7 = 56$ in all, and $5 \cdot 4 = 20$ of them are red then red. $\frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14}$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Had the first marble been put back, the answer would be $\frac58 \cdot \frac58 = \frac{25}{64}$, a little larger.

A number from $1$ to $10$ is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is even or greater than $6$?

Answer: $\dfrac{7}{10}$

Even: $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$ (5 numbers). Greater than $6$: $7, 8, 9, 10$ (4 numbers). In both: $8, 10$ (2 numbers). In either: $5 + 4 - 2 = 7$. Check by listing: $2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10$ is $7$ numbers ✓.

A bag holds $4$ red and $6$ blue marbles. Two are drawn one after the other without replacement. What is the probability that both are blue?

Answer: $\dfrac13$

$\frac{6}{10} \cdot \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac13$. Check by counting ordered pairs: $10 \cdot 9 = 90$ in all, and $6 \cdot 5 = 30$ are blue then blue ✓.

Practice

Give each probability as a fraction, each count as a single number, and each answer that asks for a percent as a percent. Every die, coin, spinner section, card and marble is equally likely unless the question says otherwise, and a draw is made at random.

A bag holds $5$ red, $7$ blue and $8$ green marbles. One marble is drawn at random. What is the probability that it is green?

Answer: $\dfrac25$

The bag holds $5 + 7 + 8 = 20$ marbles, and $8$ are green. So $P = \frac{8}{20} = \frac25$. Check: the other colors are $12$ marbles, and $\frac{8}{20} + \frac{12}{20} = 1$ ✓.

A fair $12$-sided die is numbered $1$ to $12$. It is rolled once. What is the probability of a multiple of $4$?

Answer: $\dfrac14$

The multiples of $4$ from $1$ to $12$ are $4, 8, 12$: three outcomes of twelve, so $P = \frac{3}{12} = \frac14$.

Each letter of the word PROBABILITY is written on its own card, and the cards are shuffled face down. One card is drawn. What is the probability that it shows a vowel? (Count Y as a consonant.)

Answer: $\dfrac{4}{11}$

The word has $11$ letters, so there are $11$ cards. The vowels are O, A, I, I: four cards. So $P = \frac{4}{11}$. It cannot be reduced, because $11$ is prime.

A drawer holds $6$ black, $5$ white and $3$ gray socks. One sock is taken out without looking. What is the probability that it is not black?

Answer: $\dfrac47$

There are $6 + 5 + 3 = 14$ socks, and $14 - 6 = 8$ are not black. So $P = \frac{8}{14} = \frac47$. Check by the complement: $1 - \frac{6}{14} = \frac{8}{14}$ ✓.

Of the $25$ students in a class, $7$ walk to school. One student is chosen at random. What is the probability, as a percent, that the student does not walk?

Answer: $72\%$

$25 - 7 = 18$ students do not walk, so $P = \frac{18}{25} = \frac{72}{100} = 72\%$. Check: $\frac{7}{25} = 28\%$ walk, and $28\% + 72\% = 100\%$ ✓.

A coin is tossed $80$ times and lands heads $46$ times. What is the experimental probability of heads?

Answer: $\dfrac{23}{40}$

The relative frequency is $\frac{46}{80}$, and dividing both by $2$ gives $\frac{23}{40}$. It is a little above the theoretical $\frac12 = \frac{20}{40}$, which is ordinary for $80$ tosses.

A spinner has $8$ equal sections: $3$ red, $4$ blue and $1$ gold. It is spun $96$ times. About how many times would you expect it to land on red?

Answer: $36$

$P(\text{red}) = \frac38$, so the expected count is $\frac38 \cdot 96$: $96 \div 8 = 12$ and $3 \cdot 12 = 36$.

Spinner A has $4$ equal sections numbered $1$ to $4$. Spinner B has $5$ equal sections numbered $1$ to $5$. Both are spun. What is the probability that both show an even number?

Answer: $\dfrac15$

Spinner A: $2$ of $4$, so $\frac12$. Spinner B: $2, 4$, so $\frac25$. Both: $\frac12 \cdot \frac25 = \frac{2}{10} = \frac15$. Check by counting pairs: $4 \cdot 5 = 20$ in all, and $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ have two even numbers, $\frac{4}{20} = \frac15$ ✓.

A bag holds $3$ red and $5$ green marbles. A marble is drawn, put back, and the bag is shaken. A second marble is drawn. What is the probability that the first is red and the second is green?

Answer: $\dfrac{15}{64}$

Each draw is from $8$ marbles. $P(\text{red}) = \frac38$ and $P(\text{green}) = \frac58$, so $\frac38 \cdot \frac58 = \frac{15}{64}$. Check: there are $8 \cdot 8 = 64$ ordered pairs of draws, and $3 \cdot 5 = 15$ are red then green ✓.

A pizza shop offers $3$ crusts and $5$ toppings. A pizza has one crust and one topping. How many different pizzas can be ordered?

Answer: $15$

The table has $3$ rows (crusts) and $5$ columns (toppings), and each cell is a different pizza: $3 \cdot 5 = 15$. A tree gives the same count: $3$ branches, each splitting into $5$.

Cards numbered $1$ to $30$ are shuffled and one is drawn. What is the probability that its number is a multiple of $4$ or a multiple of $6$?

Answer: $\dfrac13$

Multiples of $4$: $4, 8, \dots, 28$ is $7$ cards. Multiples of $6$: $6, 12, \dots, 30$ is $5$ cards. Both, the multiples of $12$: $12, 24$ is $2$ cards. In either: $7 + 5 - 2 = 10$, so $P = \frac{10}{30} = \frac13$. Check by listing: $4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 18, 20, 24, 28, 30$ is $10$ numbers ✓.

In a class of $28$ students, $15$ play soccer, $12$ are in the band, and $6$ do both. One student is chosen at random. What is the probability, as a percent, that the student plays soccer or is in the band?

Answer: $75\%$

In either group: $15 + 12 - 6 = 21$ students. $P = \frac{21}{28} = \frac34 = 75\%$. Check by regions: $9$ only soccer, $6$ both, $6$ only band is $21$ ✓.

Six cards are numbered $1$ to $6$. Two are drawn one after the other without replacement. What is the probability that both are even?

Answer: $\dfrac15$

The first is even with probability $\frac36$. Then $2$ even cards remain among $5$, so the second is even with probability $\frac25$. Both: $\frac36 \cdot \frac25 = \frac{6}{30} = \frac15$. Check by listing the pairs of evens: $2$ and $4$, $2$ and $6$, $4$ and $6$ is $3$ pairs, out of $15$ pairs of cards, and $\frac{3}{15} = \frac15$ ✓.

A box holds $6$ red pens and $4$ blue pens. Two are drawn one after the other without replacement. What is the probability that one is red and one is blue?

Answer: $\dfrac{8}{15}$

Red then blue: $\frac{6}{10} \cdot \frac{4}{9} = \frac{24}{90}$. Blue then red: $\frac{4}{10} \cdot \frac{6}{9} = \frac{24}{90}$. Add: $\frac{48}{90} = \frac{8}{15}$. Check by counting ordered pairs: $10 \cdot 9 = 90$ in all, and $2 \cdot 6 \cdot 4 = 48$ have one of each color ✓.

Recap

Back to the puzzle

Alex's three cases are not equally likely. The equally likely outcomes of two coins are $HH, HT, TH, TT$, and “one head” is two of the four. So the chance of two heads is $\frac14$, which is also $\frac12 \cdot \frac12$ by the rule for independent events. Before you count, check that the things you count are equally likely.

  • With equally likely outcomes, $P(\text{event})$ is the number of favorable outcomes over the number of outcomes in all. It lies from $0$ (cannot happen) to $1$ (must happen) and can be written as a percent.
  • Count the outcomes, not the colors: a spinner of $8$ equal sections, $3$ of them red, gives $\frac38$.
  • The complement rule: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$.
  • Experimental probability is the relative frequency from trials. It wanders, and settles nearer the theoretical value as the trials grow.
  • The expected count in $n$ trials is $p \cdot n$. It is an average, not a promise.
  • List the outcomes of two steps in order, in a table or in a tree. The number of pairs is the number of first choices times the number of second choices.
  • Independent events: $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. For “or”, add and take away the overlap once: $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$.
  • Without replacement the second fraction changes: the bottom always loses the marble that was taken, and the top loses it too when the second draw is the same kind.
