Part II — Intuitive Geometry · Chapter 6

Isometries

Two figures are "the same" when one can be moved onto the other. This chapter takes that casual phrase and makes it precise — and in doing so replaces a list of congruence rules with a single idea: a map of the plane that does not change distances.

A puzzle to carry through the chapter

Stand a book upright in front of you, cover towards you. Give it a quarter turn clockwise, then flip it over left to right. Now start again and do the same two moves in the other order: flip first, then turn. Does the book finish in the same position both times?

  • Yes — the same two moves, so the same result
  • No — the order changes the result
  • Only if the book is square

Try it with a real book before reading on. The answer is the most important fact in the chapter.

By the end of this chapter you can describe translations, rotations, reflections, and dilations as mappings of the plane; test whether a mapping is an isometry; compose two isometries and identify the result; explain congruence as the existence of an isometry; and list the symmetries of a figure and see that they compose like a system of arithmetic in their own right.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with distance and the Pythagorean theorem (Chapter 5). An isometry is defined entirely by what it does to distance, so that notion has to be solid first.

One loan from later chapters: this chapter writes points as pairs of numbers. $(x, y)$ means: start where two perpendicular number lines cross, go $x$ along the horizontal one and $y$ along the vertical one — so $(3,2)$ is three to the right and two up, and $(-1,0)$ is one step to the left. Chapter 8 builds this properly, including the formula for the distance between two such points, which Example 1 borrows. If the formulas get in the way on a first reading, the tracing-paper description of each motion is the real content.

Three quick ones from earlier chapters, to wake up what this chapter stands on. They are not scored. If one will not come, its solution says where to look.

A right triangle has legs $5$ and $12$. How long is its hypotenuse?

Answer: $13$

$25 + 144 = 169 = 13^2$. The Pythagorean theorem, §5.4 — and the reason distance in this chapter can be computed at all.

Which property of distance says $d(P,Q) = d(Q,P)$?

  • Positivity.
  • Symmetry.
  • The triangle inequality.

Answer: symmetry

Symmetry: distance has no preferred direction. The other two were positivity and the triangle inequality — §5.1.

For any number $u$, how does $(-u)^2$ compare with $u^2$?

  • They are equal.
  • They are opposites.
  • It depends on the sign of $u$.

Answer: they are equal

$(-u)(-u) = u \cdot u$, by Example 4 of Chapter 1. Squaring forgets the sign — which is exactly why a reflection will turn out not to change distances.

6.1 Some standard mappings of the plane

A mapping $F$ of the plane is a rule that assigns to every point $P$ a single point $F(P)$, its image. Nothing more is required at this stage: any rule at all, as long as each point gets exactly one image. (Chapter 13 treats mappings in general; here we only need those of the plane to itself.)

Four such rules do most of the work in geometry. We describe each twice — once as a motion you could perform with a sheet of tracing paper, and once as a formula, anticipating Part III where points become pairs of numbers.

Four mappings
  • Translation by a fixed displacement: slide everything the same distance in the same direction. In coordinates, $(x,y) \mapsto (x + a,\; y + b)$.
  • Rotation about a point $O$ through an angle $\theta$: turn the whole plane, with $O$ held fixed. About the origin, $(x,y) \mapsto (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\; x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)$ — a formula we will not need until Chapter 11.
  • Reflection in a line $L$: flip the plane over, keeping $L$ fixed. In the $x$-axis, $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$; in the line $y = x$, $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$.
  • Dilation by a factor $r > 0$ about a point $O$: push every point away from $O$ to $r$ times its distance. About the origin, $(x,y)\mapsto(rx,ry)$.

The first three feel like motions: the figure arrives somewhere else, unchanged. The fourth does not — it resizes. Section 6.2 makes that difference exact.

The F is not decoration

A letter with no symmetry of its own is the standard test object, because a symmetric shape hides what the mapping did. A rotated square looks like an unrotated square; a reflected F is unmistakable. When you check your own work, pick a lopsided figure on purpose.

Where does the translation $(x,y) \mapsto (x+2,\; y-3)$ send the point $(4,1)$?

Answer: $(6,-2)$

$(4 + 2,\; 1 - 3) = (6, -2)$: two to the right and three down, like every other point of the plane.

Reflection in the $y$-axis sends $(3, -2)$ to $($ $,$ $)$.

Answer: $(-3, -2)$

The rule is $(x, y) \mapsto (-x, y)$: the first coordinate changes sign, the second is untouched.

6.2 Isometries

Definition

A mapping $F$ of the plane is an isometry if it preserves distance: for all points $P$ and $Q$, $$d\bigl(F(P), F(Q)\bigr) = d(P, Q).$$

The word says it: iso, same; metry, measure. Translations, rotations, and reflections are isometries. A dilation by $r$ multiplies every distance by $r$, so it is an isometry only in the trivial case $r = 1$.

Example 1. Checking a mapping against the definition

Show that the reflection $F(x,y) = (x,-y)$ is an isometry.

  1. Take two points $P = (x_1,y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2,y_2)$. Their images are $(x_1,-y_1)$ and $(x_2,-y_2)$.
  2. The distance between two points is Pythagoras (Chapter 5) applied to the horizontal and vertical legs between them — the distance formula, derived in Chapter 8: $$d(P,Q) = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}.$$
  3. For the images, the vertical difference is $-y_2 - (-y_1) = -(y_2-y_1)$, and squaring kills the sign: $$d(F(P),F(Q)) = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + \bigl(-(y_2-y_1)\bigr)^2} = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}.$$
  4. The two expressions are identical, so distance is preserved. $\blacksquare$

The same two lines handle a translation: the $+a$ and $+b$ cancel inside each difference, leaving the distance untouched.

Is the mapping $(x,y) \mapsto (x, 2y)$ an isometry?

  • Yes — it keeps every horizontal distance.
  • No — it changes some distances.

Answer: no

$(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are $1$ apart; their images $(0,0)$ and $(0,2)$ are $2$ apart. One pair whose distance changes is enough to fail the definition, which says for all $P$ and $Q$. (Horizontal distances happen to survive — but "some distances preserved" is not the definition.)

Your turn, with help. A translation is an isometry

Show that $T(x,y) = (x + a,\; y + b)$ preserves distance. Try each step before opening it.

  1. Take $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$. Their images are $(x_1 + a,\; y_1 + b)$ and $(x_2 + a,\; y_2 + b)$.
  2. Compute the horizontal and vertical differences between the images

    $(x_2 + a) - (x_1 + a) = x_2 - x_1$, and likewise $(y_2 + b) - (y_1 + b) = y_2 - y_1$. The $a$ and $b$ cancel.

  3. Conclude

    The distance formula uses only those two differences, and they are unchanged. So $d(T(P), T(Q)) = d(P, Q)$. $\blacksquare$

Isometries can be performed one after another, and the result is again a mapping: the composite $G \circ F$, meaning "do $F$, then $G$". This is worth stating as a fact, because it is what lets us build complicated motions out of simple ones.

Composition

If $F$ and $G$ are isometries, so is $G \circ F$. Every isometry can be undone: there is an inverse isometry $F^{-1}$ with $F^{-1}\circ F$ the identity mapping, which leaves every point where it is.

Why composition works. $d\bigl(G(F(P)), G(F(Q))\bigr) = d\bigl(F(P), F(Q)\bigr) = d(P,Q)$: apply $G$'s property, then $F$'s. Nothing more is needed. $\blacksquare$

Example 2. Two reflections make a rotation

Reflect in the $x$-axis, then in the $y$-axis. What single motion does the same job?

  1. First reflection: $(x,y) \mapsto (x,-y)$.
  2. Second reflection applied to that: $(x,-y) \mapsto (-x,-y)$.
  3. So the composite is $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$ — every point sent to the opposite side of the origin, at the same distance.
  4. That is exactly rotation by $180°$ about the origin. $\blacksquare$

The general statement: reflecting in two lines that meet at angle $\theta$ composes to a rotation by $2\theta$ about their intersection. Here the axes meet at $90°$, giving $180°$. Reflecting in two parallel lines composes to a translation instead.

Reflect the point $(3,5)$ in the $y$-axis, and then reflect the result in the $x$-axis. Where does it end up?

Answer: $(-3,-5)$

$(3,5) \mapsto (-3,5) \mapsto (-3,-5)$. The composite is the half turn about the origin, as in Example 2 — for these two reflections the order happens not to matter.

What congruence really says

Two figures are congruent when some isometry carries one exactly onto the other. Every congruence criterion you may have met — SSS, SAS, ASA — is a claim that a small amount of matching data is already enough to guarantee such a motion exists. The criteria are useful shortcuts; the definition is the idea.

Notice also what congruence does not care about: position and orientation. Two triangles in different corners of the page, one of them upside down, are the same triangle. Similarity is the same story with dilations allowed in as well — shape preserved, size not.

Which of these mappings of the plane are isometries? Choose every one.

  • A translation.
  • A rotation about a point.
  • A dilation by the factor $2$.
  • A reflection in a line.

Answer: translation, rotation, reflection

Those three move figures without changing any distance. A dilation by $2$ doubles every distance, so it is not an isometry.

6.3 Symmetry

Now turn the question around. Instead of asking which motions carry one figure to another, ask which motions carry a figure to itself.

Definition

A symmetry of a figure $S$ is an isometry $F$ with $F(S) = S$: the figure lands exactly on top of where it started. Individual points may move; the set as a whole does not.

Every figure has at least one symmetry, the identity — do nothing. A figure with only that one is called asymmetric, which is why the letter F was a good test object.

Example 3. The symmetries of a square

List every isometry carrying a square onto itself.

  1. Rotations about the centre by $0°$, $90°$, $180°$, $270°$. Anything else moves a corner to a non-corner. That is four.
  2. Reflections in the two diagonals, and in the two lines through the midpoints of opposite sides. That is four more.
  3. No other isometry qualifies: a symmetry must send corners to corners and fix the centre, and the eight listed already exhaust the possibilities.
  4. So the square has exactly $8$ symmetries. $\blacksquare$

Compare: a non-square rectangle has $4$ (identity, the $180°$ rotation, and two reflections), and a circle has infinitely many — every rotation about its centre, and every reflection in a diameter.

How many symmetries does an equilateral triangle have?

Answer: $6$

Three rotations about the centre ($0°$, $120°$, $240°$) and three reflections, one in the line through each vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side. Six in all — $2n$ for a regular $n$-gon.

The symmetries of a figure are not just a list; they interact. Compose two symmetries and you get a symmetry (the figure is put back twice). Undo a symmetry and you get a symmetry. The identity is there. These three facts — closure, inverses, an identity — are the definition of a group, and the symmetries of any figure form one.

Why that matters more than it looks

You have seen this pattern before. In Chapter 1, the integers under addition had an identity ($0$), inverses ($-a$), and associativity. Here rotations and reflections do the same under composition, with the identity motion in place of $0$. The objects are unrecognisably different; the structure is the same. Noticing such repetitions is much of what higher mathematics consists of.

Let $R$ be the quarter turn counterclockwise about the origin and $M$ the reflection in the $x$-axis. Do you expect "$M$ then $R$" and "$R$ then $M$" to be the same motion? Predict, then follow the point $(1,0)$ through both.Predict, then open

They differ. $M$ leaves $(1,0)$ where it is, and $R$ then carries it to $(0,1)$. In the other order, $R$ carries $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$ and $M$ then sends that to $(0,-1)$. One point with two different destinations is enough: the two composites are different motions. Example 4 writes this out.

Example 4. Composition is not commutative

Let $R$ be rotation by $90°$ counterclockwise about the origin and $M$ reflection in the $x$-axis. Compare $R \circ M$ with $M \circ R$ at the point $(1,0)$.

  1. $M$ fixes $(1,0)$, since it is on the axis. Then $R$ sends it to $(0,1)$. So $(R\circ M)(1,0) = (0,1)$.
  2. The other order: $R$ sends $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$, and then $M$ sends that to $(0,-1)$. So $(M\circ R)(1,0) = (0,-1)$.
  3. $(0,1) \neq (0,-1)$, so $R\circ M \neq M\circ R$. $\blacksquare$

Order matters here in a way it never did for adding or multiplying numbers. If that seems strange, try it with a book: rotate it a quarter turn and then flip it over, then start again in the other order.

Here is a book: the one from the opening puzzle, laid on its back on a table, so that looking down on it is looking at its cover. Put the origin at the centre of the book, with $x$ to the right and $y$ towards the book's top edge. A turn is a quarter turn clockwise, seen from above: $(x,y) \mapsto (y,-x)$. A flip turns the book over left to right. Seen from above, the flip is the reflection in the vertical line through the middle of the book, $(x,y) \mapsto (-x,y)$; but the book gets there by a half turn through space, about that line. A reflection of the plane is a half turn of the page through space.

After "turn, then flip", which cover will be on top, and which way will the book's top edge point? And after "flip, then turn"?Predict, then open

Each run has exactly one flip, so the back cover ends on top both times. The top edge is where they differ. Turn first: the top edge swings round to point right, and the flip, a reflection in the vertical line, sends right to left. Flip first: the top edge still points away from you, and the turn then swings it to the right. Left in one run, right in the other: the same two moves, two different positions. Try both in the figure.

Do "turn, then flip" and look where the book ends. Start again and reach that same position beginning with a flip, in as few moves as you can.

Answer: flip, turn, turn, turn

Turn, then flip leaves the back cover up with the top edge pointing left. After a first flip the back cover is up with the top edge pointing away from you, and each turn swings the top edge a quarter turn clockwise: one turn points it right, two towards you, three left. So flip, turn, turn, turn: four moves. Nothing shorter works. The back cover needs an odd number of flips, so three moves starting with a flip are either one flip and at most two turns, which leave the top edge pointing away, right or towards you, or three flips, which leave it pointing away.

In the chapter's notation, with $T(x,y) = (y,-x)$ the turn and $F(x,y) = (-x,y)$ the flip, turn then flip is $F \circ T$: $$(x,y) \mapsto (y,-x) \mapsto (-y,-x).$$ Flip then three turns is $T \circ T \circ T \circ F$: $$(x,y) \mapsto (-x,y) \mapsto (y,x) \mapsto (x,-y) \mapsto (-y,-x).$$ The same rule, so the same position. Three clockwise quarter turns make one counterclockwise quarter turn, $T \circ T \circ T = T^{-1}$, and that is how a turn made before the flip looks once the flip has been done: $F \circ T = T^{-1} \circ F$. (Flip, flip, turn, flip also takes four moves, because the first two flips cancel.)

With $R$ and $M$ as in Example 4, where does $M \circ R$ send the point $(0,1)$?

Answer: $(-1,0)$

$R$ turns $(0,1)$ a quarter turn counterclockwise to $(-1,0)$. That point is on the $x$-axis, so $M$ leaves it alone: $(M \circ R)(0,1) = (-1,0)$. In the other order, $M$ sends $(0,1)$ to $(0,-1)$ and $R$ then sends that to $(1,0)$ — different again.

Practice

Where a point is asked for, write it as a pair like (3,-2).

Where does reflection in the $x$-axis send the point $(5,2)$?

Answer: $(5,-2)$

The rule is $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$: the horizontal position is untouched, the vertical one flips. Points already on the axis, where $y = 0$, do not move at all — as befits the line being reflected in.

Where does rotation by $180°$ about the origin send $(4,7)$?

Answer: $(-4,-7)$

A half turn about the origin carries each point to the opposite side, the same distance away: $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$. Example 2 showed this is also what you get by reflecting in both axes, in either order.

How many symmetries does a square have?

Answer: $8$

Four rotations ($0°, 90°, 180°, 270°$) and four reflections (two diagonals, two mid-side lines). In general a regular $n$-gon has $2n$ symmetries: $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections.

How many symmetries does the letter Z have, drawn with perfect strokes?

Answer: $2$

The identity, and the $180°$ rotation about its centre — turn a Z upside down and it is a Z again. It has no reflectional symmetry: a mirror turns Z into S.

Which of these mappings of the plane is not an isometry?

  • $(x,y)\mapsto(x+3,\,y-1)$
  • $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$
  • $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)$
  • $(x,y)\mapsto(2x,2y)$

Answer: $(x,y)\mapsto(2x,2y)$

That is a dilation: it doubles every distance, so it fails the definition. The first is a translation, the second is reflection in the line $y = x$, and the third is reflection in the $y$-axis — all three preserve distance.

Note that the dilation still preserves shape: angles are unchanged and figures stay similar. Isometry is the stronger condition.

Reflecting in the line $y = x$ and then in the $x$-axis gives which single motion?

  • A translation.
  • A rotation about the origin.
  • A reflection in some line.
  • A dilation.

Answer: a rotation about the origin

Follow a point: $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)\mapsto(y,-x)$. That is rotation by $90°$ clockwise about the origin — for instance $(1,0)\mapsto(0,-1)$.

Consistent with the general rule: the two mirror lines meet at $45°$ at the origin, and $2\times45° = 90°$.

$F$ is an isometry, and $d(P,Q) = 5$. What is $d\bigl(F(P),F(Q)\bigr)$?

Answer: $5$

This is the definition and nothing else: an isometry preserves every distance, whatever the points and whichever isometry it is. You cannot say where $F(P)$ is without knowing $F$ — but you can always say how far apart the images are.

Two figures are congruent. What exactly does that mean?

  • They have the same area.
  • They are in the same position on the page.
  • Some isometry carries one exactly onto the other.
  • One is a dilation of the other.

Answer: some isometry carries one exactly onto the other

Equal area is necessary but nowhere near sufficient — a $1\times 4$ rectangle and a $2\times 2$ square both have area $4$ and are not congruent. Position is irrelevant to congruence. Allowing dilations gives the weaker notion of similarity.

Let $T(x,y) = (x+3,y-1)$ and $M(x,y) = (x,-y)$. Compute $(M \circ T)(-2,-5)$, that is, apply $T$ first and then $M$.

Answer: $(1,6)$

$T(-2,-5) = (-2+3,\,-5-1) = (1,-6)$, and then $M(1,-6) = (1,6)$.

In the other order: $M(-2,-5) = (-2,5)$ and then $T(-2,5) = (1,4)$ — a different answer, another reminder that composition depends on order.

Why do the symmetries of a figure form a group under composition?

  • Composing two symmetries gives a symmetry, each has an inverse, and the identity is one of them.
  • Because composition of symmetries is commutative.
  • Because every figure has finitely many symmetries.
  • Because symmetries preserve area.

Answer: closure, inverses, and an identity

Those three conditions (with associativity, which composition always has) are what the word "group" means. Commutativity is not required, and Example 4 showed it can fail. A circle has infinitely many symmetries, so finiteness is not required either, and preserving area is true but not the point.

Recap

Back to the puzzle

No. Turn-then-flip and flip-then-turn leave the book in different positions. Motions are composed, and composition is not commutative: $R \circ M \neq M \circ R$ (Example 4). That never happened when adding or multiplying numbers, and it is the first sign that the symmetries of a figure are an arithmetic of a genuinely new kind.

  • A mapping of the plane assigns one image point to each point; translations, rotations, reflections, and dilations are the four to know.
  • An isometry is a mapping that preserves distance. The first three are isometries; a dilation by $r \neq 1$ is not.
  • Composites and inverses of isometries are isometries — and composition is not commutative.
  • Congruent means "related by an isometry". Similar means "related by an isometry and a dilation".
  • The symmetries of a figure are the isometries carrying it to itself; they always form a group, the same structure the integers showed under addition.
