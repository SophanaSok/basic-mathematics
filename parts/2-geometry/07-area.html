Part II — Intuitive Geometry · Chapter 7

Area and Applications

Three assumptions about area — a rectangle's is base times height, congruent figures have the same area, and areas add — are enough to derive every formula in this chapter, including the area of a circle.

By the end of this chapter you can derive the triangle formula from the rectangle rather than recall it, apply the scaling law (lengths $\times r$, areas $\times r^2$) to real questions, explain why the area of a disc is $\pi r^2$ by way of inscribed polygons, and say precisely what $\pi$ is and why the same $\pi$ appears in both $2\pi r$ and $\pi r^2$.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with the Pythagorean theorem (Chapter 5) and with isometries and congruence (Chapter 6) — the three assumptions about area below are stated in terms of both.

7.1 Area of rectangles and triangles

Area is to regions what distance was to pairs of points: a number attached to a figure. We take three properties as given.

The three assumptions
  1. A rectangle with sides $b$ and $h$ has area $bh$.
  2. Congruent figures have the same area — moving a shape does not change how much room it takes up.
  3. If a figure is cut into non-overlapping pieces, the areas of the pieces add to the area of the whole.

The first fixes the unit and the scale; the other two say area is a sensible notion of "amount of plane". Notice that (2) is a statement about the isometries of Chapter 6, and that we already used (2) and (3) together in the dissection proof of Pythagoras. Everything below is those three facts, applied with a pair of scissors.

Example 1. The area of a right triangle

A right triangle has legs $b$ and $h$. Find its area.

  1. Take a second copy of the triangle and rotate it by $180°$ about the midpoint of the hypotenuse.
  2. The two together form a rectangle with sides $b$ and $h$. Check the corners: the two acute angles of the triangle add to $90°$, so each corner of the assembled figure is a right angle.
  3. By assumptions (1) and (3), the two triangles have total area $bh$; by (2) they have the same area as each other.
  4. So each has area $\tfrac12 bh$. $\blacksquare$

For a triangle that is not right-angled, drop the perpendicular from one vertex to the opposite side — the height $h$ relative to that base $b$. The perpendicular splits the triangle into two right triangles (or, if the foot falls outside, into a difference of two). Either way the arithmetic collapses to the same statement:

Area of a triangle

$$\text{area} = \tfrac12\,b\,h,$$ where $h$ is the perpendicular distance from the base $b$ to the opposite vertex.

One consequence worth noticing

Only the perpendicular distance matters, not where the far vertex sits along that direction. Slide the top vertex parallel to the base and the area does not budge, though the triangle becomes unrecognisably long and thin. That is why any of the three sides may be called the base: all three choices give the same number.

7.2 Scaling

Apply a dilation by $r$ to a figure. Every length is multiplied by $r$. What happens to the area?

For a rectangle the answer is immediate: sides $b$ and $h$ become $rb$ and $rh$, and the area $bh$ becomes $(rb)(rh) = r^2 bh$. The same then holds for triangles, since a triangle is half a rectangle; and for any figure that can be approximated by small rectangles, which is to say all of them.

The scaling law

Scale a figure by a factor $r$. Then every length — side, perimeter, diagonal — is multiplied by $r$, and the area is multiplied by $r^{2}$.

Example 2. A pizza question

A $30$ cm pizza costs $\$12$. At the same price per unit of pizza, what should a $45$ cm pizza cost?

  1. The diameters are in the ratio $45/30 = 1.5$, so the larger pizza is the smaller one scaled by $r = 1.5$.
  2. Area scales by $r^2 = 2.25$.
  3. So the fair price is $12 \times 2.25 = \$27$.
  4. Not $\$18$, which is what scaling the price by the diameter would give. $\blacksquare$

We never needed the formula for the area of a disc. The scaling law alone settles it, because both pizzas are the same shape.

Where the law bites

Doubling the sides of a map's scale bar quadruples the land it represents. A pipe of twice the radius carries about four times the water. And in three dimensions the exponent becomes $3$: an animal twice as tall is roughly eight times as heavy, while the cross-sections of its bones are only four times as large — which is why large animals are not simply scaled-up small ones.

7.3 Area of a disc

A circle has no straight sides, so none of the cutting arguments above applies directly. The way forward is to approximate — to trap the disc between shapes we can measure, and squeeze.

Inscribe a regular $n$-gon in a circle of radius $r$ and cut it into $n$ triangles from the centre. Each triangle has area $\tfrac12 (\text{base})(\text{height})$, where the height is the distance $a_n$ from the centre to a side. Adding them up:

$$\text{area of the } n\text{-gon} = \tfrac12\,a_n \cdot p_n,$$

where $p_n$ is the polygon's perimeter. Now let $n$ grow. The sides shorten, the polygon presses outward against the circle, $a_n \to r$, and $p_n$ approaches the circle's circumference $C$. So the area of the disc is $\tfrac12 r C$.

7.4 Circumference, and what $\pi$ is

The scaling law does one more job. All circles are similar — any two are related by a dilation — so the ratio of circumference to diameter cannot depend on which circle you picked. That constant ratio is the definition of $\pi$:

$$\pi = \frac{C}{2r}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad C = 2\pi r.$$
Why the same $\pi$ twice

Substitute $C = 2\pi r$ into the result of §7.3: $$\text{area} = \tfrac12\,r\,C = \tfrac12\,r\,(2\pi r) = \pi r^{2}.$$ So $\pi$ was defined by the circumference, and the area formula is a consequence. The two $\pi$'s are the same number not by coincidence but because the polygon argument ties the area to the perimeter, before either has been evaluated.

Two remarks on the number itself. First, $\pi$ is irrational — like $\sqrt2$ in Chapter 3, it is not the ratio of two integers, so $22/7$ and $3.14159$ are approximations and nothing more. Second, this makes the area of a disc a genuinely new kind of quantity: no dissection into finitely many polygonal pieces will ever produce it exactly. The limit is not laziness; it is unavoidable.

Example 3. An annulus

A circular pond of radius $5$ m is surrounded by a path $2$ m wide. What is the area of the path?

  1. The path is a disc of radius $7$ with a disc of radius $5$ removed.
  2. By assumption (3), area of path $=$ area of big disc $-$ area of small disc: $$\pi(7)^2 - \pi(5)^2 = \pi(49 - 25) = 24\pi.$$
  3. Numerically $24\pi \approx 75.4$ square metres. $\blacksquare$

Factor before multiplying out — $\pi(49-25)$ is less work and less error-prone than $153.94 - 78.54$, and it leaves the answer exact.

Example 4. Area from three sides

Find the area of the triangle with sides $13$, $14$, $15$.

  1. Take the side $14$ as the base and let the foot of the height split it into $x$ and $14 - x$.
  2. Pythagoras on each half, with the common height $h$: $$13^2 - x^2 = h^2 = 15^2 - (14-x)^2.$$
  3. Expand the right side: $225 - (196 - 28x + x^2) = 29 + 28x - x^2$. So $169 - x^2 = 29 + 28x - x^2$, and the $x^2$ cancels.
  4. $140 = 28x$, so $x = 5$, and then $h^2 = 169 - 25 = 144$, giving $h = 12$.
  5. Area $= \tfrac12(14)(12) = 84$. $\blacksquare$

Notice how the problem became a linear equation the moment the $x^2$ terms cancelled — exactly the elimination of Chapter 2, arriving unannounced in a geometry problem.

Practice

Give exact answers where you can. Where an answer involves $\pi$, write it as a decimal to one place unless the question says otherwise.

A triangle has base $12$ and height $5$. What is its area?

Answer: $30$

$\tfrac12 bh = \tfrac12(12)(5) = 30$. Two copies of this triangle assemble into a $12 \times 5$ rectangle of area $60$ — which is where the factor $\tfrac12$ comes from.

Every length of a figure is multiplied by $3$. By what factor is its area multiplied?

Answer: $9$

By the scaling law, area is multiplied by $r^2 = 3^2 = 9$. You can see it in the simplest case: a $1\times1$ square scales to $3\times3$, holding nine copies of the original.

A circle has diameter $10$. What is its area, to one decimal place?

Answer: $78.5$

The radius is $5$, so the area is $\pi(5)^2 = 25\pi \approx 78.54$.

The commonest slip here is using the diameter in place of the radius, which gives $100\pi$ — four times too much, the scaling law again.

One square has area $9$ and another has area $144$. How many times longer is the side of the second?

Answer: $4$

Areas are in the ratio $144/9 = 16$. Since areas scale as $r^2$, we need $r^2 = 16$, so $r = 4$.

Check directly: sides $3$ and $12$, and $12 = 4 \times 3$ ✓.

A triangle's apex is slid along a line parallel to its base, keeping the base fixed. What happens to its area?

  • It increases as the triangle becomes more slanted.
  • It stays exactly the same.
  • It decreases.
  • It depends on which direction the apex is slid.

Answer: it stays exactly the same

The area is $\tfrac12 bh$, and neither the base $b$ nor the perpendicular height $h$ changes when the apex moves parallel to the base. The triangle becomes visibly more lopsided, and the other two sides get longer, but the area is untouched.

A trapezium has parallel sides $10$ and $6$, and the perpendicular distance between them is $6$. What is its area?

Answer: $48$

A diagonal cuts it into two triangles, both of height $6$, with bases $10$ and $6$: $$\tfrac12(10)(6) + \tfrac12(6)(6) = 30 + 18 = 48.$$

In general this gives the trapezium formula $\tfrac12(b_1 + b_2)h$ — the average of the two parallel sides, times the distance between them.

A ring lies between circles of radius $5$ and $7$, with the same centre. Its area is $k\pi$. What is $k$?

Answer: $k = 24$

$\pi(7^2) - \pi(5^2) = \pi(49-25) = 24\pi$, so $k = 24$.

Note it is not $\pi(7-5)^2 = 4\pi$: squaring does not distribute over subtraction, a point Chapter 1 made about $(a-b)^2$ and one that costs marks forever after.

A square has the same area as a $9 \times 4$ rectangle. What is the square's side?

Answer: $6$

The rectangle's area is $36$, so the square's side $s$ satisfies $s^2 = 36$, giving $s = 6$ (the negative root is not a length).

The two figures have equal area but different perimeters — $26$ for the rectangle, $24$ for the square. Among all rectangles of a given area, the square has the least perimeter.

What is $\pi$, as this chapter defines it?

  • The number $22/7$.
  • The area of a circle of radius $1$, by definition.
  • The ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter — the same for every circle.
  • A number chosen so that the area formula comes out tidily.

Answer: the ratio of circumference to diameter

That ratio is independent of the circle because all circles are similar, so it is a single number and may be given a name. The area result $\pi r^2$ is then derived — it is true that a unit circle has area $\pi$, but that is a theorem here, not the definition. And $22/7$ is merely a convenient approximation: $\pi$ is irrational.

Find the area of the triangle with sides $9$, $12$, $15$.

Answer: $54$

Test for a right angle first: $9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15^2$. So by the converse of Pythagoras the triangle is right-angled, with the legs $9$ and $12$ perpendicular to each other.

Then one leg is the base and the other the height: $\tfrac12(9)(12) = 54$.

Mixed review: Part II

Six problems pulled back across Chapters 5–7 — nothing new, just recall.

A right triangle has legs $6$ and $8$. What is the length of the hypotenuse?

Answer: $10$

$\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$ — the theorem from Chapter 5.

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One of the angles formed measures $65°$. What does its corresponding angle (at the other intersection) measure?

Answer: $65°$

Corresponding angles at a transversal cutting parallel lines are equal — the rule from Chapter 5.

Which of these is not an isometry of the plane?

  • Rotation about a point
  • Reflection in a line
  • Scaling every distance by a factor of $2$
  • Translation

Answer: scaling by a factor of 2

An isometry preserves distance exactly; scaling by $2$ doubles every distance instead — it fails the definition from Chapter 6.

How many symmetries does a non-square rectangle have?

  • $2$
  • $4$
  • $6$
  • $8$

Answer: $4$

The identity, the $180°$ rotation, and reflections in the two lines through opposite side midpoints — four in total, as noted alongside the square's $8$ in Chapter 6.

A triangle has base $10$ and height $6$. What is its area?

Answer: $30$

$\tfrac12(10)(6) = 30$ — the triangle-is-half-a-rectangle rule from this chapter.

If a shape's linear dimensions are all scaled by a factor of $3$, by what factor does its area scale?

Answer: $9$

Area scales by $r^2$, and $3^2 = 9$ — the scaling law from this chapter.

Recap

  • Three assumptions carry the chapter: rectangle $=$ $bh$, congruent figures have equal area, and areas of pieces add.
  • A triangle is half a rectangle, so its area is $\tfrac12 bh$ — and only the perpendicular height matters.
  • Scaling by $r$ multiplies every length by $r$ and every area by $r^2$. Most everyday mistakes about size are this law being ignored.
  • Inscribed polygons give area $= \tfrac12 r C$ for a disc, before $\pi$ is even mentioned.
  • $\pi$ is defined as $C/2r$; then $C = 2\pi r$ and area $= \pi r^2$ follow, which is why the same $\pi$ appears in both.
