Part II — Intuitive Geometry · Chapter 5

Distance and Angles

Part I was about numbers. This part is about the plane — and the bridge between them is that distance and angle are numbers attached to pictures. Once that is granted, geometry becomes something you can compute with.

By the end of this chapter you can state the three properties distance obeys and use the triangle inequality, work with supplementary, vertical, corresponding and alternate angles, prove that the angles of a triangle add to $180°$, and prove the Pythagorean theorem by dissection — as well as use its converse to test for a right angle.
Before you start

Very little algebra is needed here: the rules of arithmetic (Chapter 1) and square roots (Chapter 3), which the Pythagorean theorem at the end will put to work.

5.1 Distance

We begin with an ordinary plane, its points, and its lines, and we take for granted the two facts everyone's eye already agrees with: through any two distinct points there is exactly one line, and any two distinct lines meet in at most one point.

To that we add a way of measuring. Fix a unit of length. Then to every pair of points $P$, $Q$ we attach a number $d(P,Q) \ge 0$, the distance from $P$ to $Q$, also written $PQ$. This is the same move Chapter 3 made for the number line, where the distance between $x$ and $y$ was $|x - y|$; here we simply insist that the plane behaves the same way.

The properties of distance
  1. Positivity. $d(P,Q) \ge 0$, and $d(P,Q) = 0$ exactly when $P = Q$.
  2. Symmetry. $d(P,Q) = d(Q,P)$ — distance has no preferred direction.
  3. The triangle inequality. $d(P,R) \le d(P,Q) + d(Q,R)$ for any third point $Q$.

The third is the only one with any content, and it says something you already believe: a detour never shortens a trip. Going from $P$ to $R$ by way of $Q$ costs at least as much as going straight. It costs exactly as much precisely when $Q$ lies on the segment between $P$ and $R$ — which is one way to say what "between" means.

Why straight lines are called straight

Nothing so far has said that the segment $PR$ is the shortest path from $P$ to $R$; we have only compared it against three-step journeys. But repeating the triangle inequality shows that $d(P,R) \le d(P,Q_1) + d(Q_1,Q_2) + \dots + d(Q_n,R)$ for any chain of stops. So the straight route beats every broken one. The inequality is the whole reason a line is the shortest way between two points.

Example 1. Reading the triangle inequality

Two sides of a triangle measure $7$ and $10$. What can be said about the third side $c$?

  1. Applying the inequality to the vertex opposite $c$: $c \le 7 + 10 = 17$.
  2. Applying it to each of the other two vertices: $7 \le 10 + c$ and $10 \le 7 + c$. Every side is at most the sum of the other two — there are three statements here, not one.
  3. The second gives $c \ge -3$, which is no news; the third gives $c \ge 3$.
  4. So $3 \le c \le 17$. And if the three points are genuinely not on one line, both inequalities are strict: $3 < c < 17$. $\blacksquare$

The general rule worth keeping: the third side is squeezed between the difference and the sum of the other two.

5.2 Angles

Distance measures separation. Angle measures turn. Two rays $\overrightarrow{OA}$ and $\overrightarrow{OB}$ from a common point $O$ cut the plane; the angle $\angle AOB$ is the amount of rotation that carries the first ray onto the second.

To turn that into a number, split a full turn into $360$ equal parts and call each one a degree. The choice of $360$ is pure convention — it is a convenient number with many divisors, and one common explanation traces it to Babylonian astronomy. Chapter 11 replaces it with a measure that is not arbitrary at all.

Vocabulary
  • A right angle is a quarter turn, $90°$. Two lines meeting at a right angle are perpendicular.
  • An angle is acute if it is less than $90°$, obtuse if between $90°$ and $180°$, and straight if exactly $180°$.
  • Two angles are complementary if they add to $90°$, supplementary if they add to $180°$.

Two facts follow immediately, and we will use them constantly. If a ray stands on a line, the two angles it makes on one side are supplementary — together they sweep out a half turn. And from that:

Vertical angles are equal

When two lines cross they make four angles. The two opposite ones (not sharing a side) are equal.

Proof. Call the four angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ in order around the crossing, so $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are opposite. Each neighbouring pair lies on a straight line, so $\alpha + \beta = 180°$ and $\beta + \gamma = 180°$. Subtracting, $\alpha - \gamma = 0$, that is, $\alpha = \gamma$. $\blacksquare$

Notice the shape of that argument

We did not measure anything. We used one fact twice and then subtracted — exactly the elimination move from Chapter 2. Most of elementary geometry is linear algebra about angles, conducted in words.

5.3 Parallel lines and transversals

Two lines in the plane are parallel if they never meet. A third line crossing both is called a transversal, and it creates eight angles — of which, remarkably, only two distinct values ever appear.

The reason is the defining property of parallels, which we take as our one geometric assumption in this section:

The parallel property

If a transversal cuts two parallel lines, then corresponding angles are equal (angles in the same position at the two crossings). Conversely, if corresponding angles are equal, the two lines are parallel.

Everything else in the picture comes from combining this with vertical and supplementary angles. For instance alternate interior angles — the two angles on opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallels — are equal, because one of them is corresponding to an angle that is vertical to the other.

Example 2. The angles of a triangle add to $180°$

Let the triangle have vertices $A$, $B$, $C$, with angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ there.

  1. Draw the line through $C$ parallel to $AB$. This is the whole idea; the rest is bookkeeping.
  2. At $C$, three angles sit side by side on that straight line: one on the left, then $\gamma$, then one on the right. So they add to $180°$.
  3. The left one and $\alpha$ are alternate interior angles for the transversal $AC$ cutting the two parallels — so it equals $\alpha$.
  4. Likewise the right one equals $\beta$, using the transversal $BC$.
  5. Therefore $\alpha + \gamma + \beta = 180°$. $\blacksquare$

Worth noticing how much this proof depends on: without the parallel property it is simply false. On the surface of a sphere, "lines" are great circles, no two of them are parallel, and triangles have more than $180°$.

5.4 The Pythagorean theorem

Now the theorem that ties distance and angle together — and the one that all of Part III will run on.

Pythagorean theorem

In a right triangle with legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$ (the side opposite the right angle), $$a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}.$$

There are hundreds of proofs. Here is one you can check with your eyes, and it needs nothing beyond "area does not change when you move pieces around".

Example 3. The dissection proof

Take four copies of the right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$.

  1. Draw a square of side $a + b$. Its area is $(a+b)^2$, whatever we put inside it.
  2. First arrangement. Place one triangle in each corner, rotated a quarter turn from the last. The gap they leave is a square. Each of its sides is a hypotenuse, so it has side $c$ and area $c^2$. Why is the gap really a square? At each of its corners two triangle angles meet; those two angles are the non-right angles of the triangle, which sum to $90°$ by Example 2. Together with the gap's corner they fill a straight $180°$, so the corner is $90°$.
  3. Second arrangement. Push the four triangles into two pairs, each pair forming an $a \times b$ rectangle. What is left is two squares, of sides $a$ and $b$, with areas $a^2$ and $b^2$.
  4. Both arrangements sit in the same $(a+b)^2$ square and use the same four triangles. So the leftovers are equal in area: $$c^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2}. \qquad \blacksquare$$
The algebra behind the picture

If you prefer symbols: the first arrangement says $(a+b)^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac12 ab + c^2$, and expanding the left side gives $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2$. Cancel $2ab$ and you have the theorem. The picture and the expansion of $(a+b)^2$ from Chapter 1 are the same fact.

The converse also holds, and it is what makes the theorem a usable test: if $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ in a triangle, then the angle opposite $c$ is right. So $3,4,5$ is a right triangle, checked by arithmetic alone — no protractor.

Example 4. Using it in both directions

(a) A right triangle has legs $5$ and $12$. Find the hypotenuse. (b) Is a triangle with sides $6$, $8$, $11$ right-angled?

  1. (a) $c^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$, so $c = 13$ — taking the positive root, since a length is positive.
  2. (b) The longest side would have to be the hypotenuse. Test: $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$, but $11^2 = 121$.
  3. $100 \neq 121$, so by the converse it is not right-angled. Since $121 > 100$, the angle opposite the side $11$ is in fact obtuse. $\blacksquare$

One last consequence, which Chapter 8 turns into a formula. If $P$ and $Q$ are two points and you complete the right triangle whose legs are horizontal and vertical, the theorem hands you the distance $PQ$ in terms of those two legs. Distance in the plane is Pythagoras, and nothing else.

Practice

Give angles in degrees as a plain number — write $40$, not $40°$.

An angle measures $40°$. What is its supplement?

Answer: $140$

Supplementary angles add to $180°$, so the supplement is $180 - 40 = 140$. (Its complement, by contrast, would be $90 - 40 = 50$.)

Two lines cross. One of the four angles is $115°$. How large is the angle next to it?

Answer: $65$

The angle next to it shares a straight line with it, so the two are supplementary: $180 - 115 = 65$. The angle opposite the $115°$ one is again $115°$, and the fourth is $65°$ — four angles, two values, as always.

Two angles of a triangle are $43°$ and $62°$. What is the third?

Answer: $75$

$180 - 43 - 62 = 75$. This is Example 2 in action: draw the parallel to one side through the opposite vertex, and the three angles line up along it.

A right triangle has legs $10$ and $24$. How long is the hypotenuse?

Answer: $26$

$c^2 = 10^2 + 24^2 = 100 + 576 = 676$, and $26^2 = 676$, so $c = 26$.

This is the $5,12,13$ triangle with every side doubled — scaling a right triangle keeps it right, since both sides of $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ pick up the same factor $r^2$.

Is the triangle with sides $9$, $40$, $41$ right-angled?

  • No — the numbers are too large.
  • Yes, with the right angle opposite the side $41$.
  • Yes, with the right angle opposite the side $40$.
  • There is not enough information.

Answer: yes, with the right angle opposite the side $41$

$9^2 + 40^2 = 81 + 1600 = 1681$ and $41^2 = 1681$. They agree, so by the converse of the Pythagorean theorem the triangle is right-angled, and the right angle is always opposite the longest side.

Two sides of a triangle are $5$ and $12$, and the three vertices are not on one line. What is the smallest whole number the third side can be?

Answer: $8$

The triangle inequality applied at each vertex gives $c \le 5 + 12 = 17$, $12 \le 5 + c$, and $5 \le 12 + c$. The middle one is the binding constraint: $c \ge 7$.

Since the vertices are not collinear, every inequality is strict: $7 < c < 17$. The smallest whole number in that range is $8$.

A transversal cuts two parallel lines. One of the eight angles is $70°$. Apart from $70°$, what is the only other value that appears among the eight?

Answer: $110$

At each crossing, an angle and its neighbour are supplementary: $180 - 70 = 110$. The parallel property forces the second crossing to repeat the first, so the eight angles take only these two values — four of each.

A rectangle is $15$ wide and has a diagonal of length $17$. How tall is it?

Answer: $8$

The diagonal cuts the rectangle into two right triangles with legs $15$ and $h$ and hypotenuse $17$. So $$15^2 + h^2 = 17^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h^2 = 289 - 225 = 64,$$ giving $h = 8$.

Which property of distance is being used when we say the straight path from $P$ to $R$ is the shortest?

  • Symmetry, $d(P,Q) = d(Q,P)$.
  • Positivity, $d(P,Q) \ge 0$.
  • The triangle inequality, $d(P,R) \le d(P,Q) + d(Q,R)$.
  • The Pythagorean theorem.

Answer: the triangle inequality

Applying it repeatedly along any chain of intermediate stops shows the direct distance is at most the total of the legs. Symmetry and positivity say nothing about comparing routes, and Pythagoras applies only to right triangles.

A right triangle has two legs of equal length. How large is each of its other two angles?

Answer: $45$

The three angles add to $180°$, and one of them is $90°$, so the other two add to $90°$. Equal legs force equal opposite angles, so each is $45°$.

This triangle will reappear in Chapter 11: its sides are in the ratio $1 : 1 : \sqrt 2$, which is where $\cos 45° = \sin 45° = 1/\sqrt2$ comes from.

Recap

  • Distance is a number attached to a pair of points, obeying positivity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality — the last of which is why straight is shortest.
  • Angles measure turn. Supplementary angles add to $180°$, and vertical angles are equal because each pair shares a straight line with the same neighbour.
  • A transversal across parallels produces only two angle values; corresponding and alternate angles are equal.
  • Hence the angles of a triangle add to $180°$ — a fact that depends squarely on the parallel property.
  • $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ in a right triangle, provable by cutting one square two ways; and the converse turns it into a test for right angles.
