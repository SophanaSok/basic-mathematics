Part IV — Miscellaneous · Chapter 13

Mappings

Chapter 12's functions took numbers to numbers. Nothing in the definition required that. Drop the requirement and the same idea covers isometries of the plane, shuffles of a deck, and the question of when something can be undone.

By the end of this chapter you can state what a mapping between two sets is and distinguish codomain from image, compose mappings and respect the order, decide whether a mapping is injective, surjective, or bijective, know that invertible means bijective and why, and count and compose permutations of a finite set.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with what a function is (Chapter 12) — a mapping is that same definition with the word "numbers" removed. The symmetries of Chapter 6 are the running example.

13.1 Definition of a mapping

Definition

Let $S$ and $T$ be sets. A mapping $f: S \to T$ assigns to each element $x \in S$ exactly one element $f(x) \in T$. We call $S$ the domain and $T$ the codomain. The image of $f$ is the set of elements actually hit: $$f(S) = \{\, f(x) : x \in S \,\} \subseteq T.$$

Only the word "numbers" has been removed from Chapter 12's definition. What that buys is reach: $S$ can be the points of the plane and $T$ the same, giving the isometries of Chapter 6; $S$ can be a set of five cards and $T$ the same five, giving a shuffle; $S$ can be the set of people in a room and $T$ the days of the year, giving each person's birthday.

Codomain is not image

The codomain is where the values are allowed to land; the image is where they actually do. For $f:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}$ with $f(x) = x^2$, the codomain is all the real numbers but the image is only $[0,\infty)$. The distinction looks pedantic until §13.2, where "surjective" is precisely the statement that the two coincide.

13.2 Composition and inverses

Given $f: S \to T$ and $g: T \to U$, the composite $g \circ f : S \to U$ is defined by

$$(g \circ f)(x) = g\bigl(f(x)\bigr).$$

Read it right to left: $f$ first, then $g$. The codomain of $f$ must match the domain of $g$, or the second step has nothing to work on.

Composition is associative — $(h\circ g)\circ f = h\circ(g\circ f)$, since both send $x$ to $h(g(f(x)))$ — but almost never commutative, as Chapter 6 already showed with a rotation and a reflection.

Three properties
  • $f$ is injective (one-to-one) if different inputs give different outputs: $f(x) = f(y)$ forces $x = y$.
  • $f$ is surjective (onto) if every element of $T$ is hit: the image is all of $T$.
  • $f$ is bijective if it is both.

In the arrow diagram: injective means no element on the right receives two arrows; surjective means none receives zero. Bijective means every element on the right receives exactly one — a perfect pairing.

Example 1. The same formula, three different answers

Consider $f(x) = x^2$. Is it injective? Surjective?

  1. As $f: \mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}$: not injective, since $f(2) = f(-2) = 4$. Not surjective either, since nothing maps to $-1$.
  2. As $f: \mathbf{R}\to[0,\infty)$: still not injective, but now surjective — every $y\ge0$ is $(\sqrt y)^2$.
  3. As $f: [0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$: injective (two non-negative numbers with the same square are equal) and surjective. Bijective.
  4. So the question "is $f$ injective?" is unanswerable until the domain and codomain are specified. $\blacksquare$

This is the sharpest reason the pedantry of §13.1 was worth it. The formula did not change once; the answers changed three times.

Now the payoff. A mapping can be undone exactly when it is bijective.

Inverses

$f: S\to T$ has an inverse $f^{-1}: T \to S$ — a mapping with $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$ for all $x\in S$ and $f(f^{-1}(y)) = y$ for all $y\in T$ — if and only if $f$ is bijective.

Why. To define $f^{-1}(y)$ we must name the element sent to $y$. Surjectivity guarantees there is at least one; injectivity guarantees there is at most one. Together they give exactly one, which is what a mapping needs. Conversely, if $f$ has an inverse then $f(x)=f(y)$ gives $x = f^{-1}(f(x)) = f^{-1}(f(y)) = y$, so $f$ is injective; and any $y$ is hit by $f^{-1}(y)$, so $f$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

Where you have already met this

Chapter 12's logarithm exists because $x\mapsto a^x$, viewed as a mapping from $\mathbf{R}$ to the positive reals, is a bijection. Restricting the codomain from all reals to the positive ones was not cosmetic — it is what made the inverse exist. And the square root is the inverse of squaring only after the domain has been cut down to $x\ge0$, as in Example 1.

13.3 Permutations

Definition

A permutation of a set $S$ is a bijection $\sigma: S \to S$ — a rearrangement of $S$ onto itself.

For a finite set there is a simplification worth knowing: a mapping from a finite set to itself is injective if and only if it is surjective. If no two elements collide, all $n$ outputs are distinct and must fill up all $n$ slots; and if all slots are filled by $n$ outputs, none of them can have collided. So for finite $S$, either property alone makes a permutation. (For infinite sets this fails badly: $n \mapsto n+1$ on the positive integers is injective but misses $1$.)

Example 2. Counting permutations

How many permutations does a set of $n$ elements have?

  1. Choose the image of the first element: $n$ choices.
  2. The second must go somewhere different, since $\sigma$ is injective: $n-1$ choices.
  3. The third: $n-2$ choices; and so on down to $1$ for the last.
  4. Multiplying, the total is $$n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 2 \cdot 1.$$ For $n=5$ that is $120$; for a deck of $52$ cards it is a number with $68$ digits. $\blacksquare$

Permutations are composed like any other mappings, and the composite of two permutations is a permutation. Every permutation has an inverse — undo the rearrangement — and the identity permutation leaves everything alone. Those are the three conditions of Chapter 6 again: the permutations of a set form a group.

Example 3. Composing two permutations

On $\{1,2,3\}$, let $\sigma$ send $1\to2,\, 2\to3,\, 3\to1$, and let $\tau$ swap $1$ and $2$, fixing $3$. Compute $\sigma\circ\tau$ and $\tau\circ\sigma$.

  1. $\sigma\circ\tau$ means $\tau$ first. $1 \xrightarrow{\tau} 2 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 3$; $2 \to 1 \to 2$; $3 \to 3 \to 1$.
  2. So $\sigma\circ\tau$ sends $1\to3,\ 2\to2,\ 3\to1$ — the swap of $1$ and $3$.
  3. The other order: $1 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 2 \xrightarrow{\tau} 1$; $2\to3\to3$; $3\to1\to2$.
  4. So $\tau\circ\sigma$ sends $1\to1,\ 2\to3,\ 3\to2$ — the swap of $2$ and $3$. A different permutation. $\blacksquare$

Both results are swaps, but of different pairs. Order matters, and the only reliable way to get it right is to track one element at a time, out loud.

Practice

Give numerical answers as plain numbers.

How many permutations does a set of $5$ elements have?

Answer: $120$

$5! = 5\times4\times3\times2\times1 = 120$. Each element in turn has one fewer destination available, because a permutation cannot send two elements to the same place.

Which of these mappings $\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}$ is injective?

  • $f(x) = x^2$
  • $f(x) = |x|$
  • $f(x) = 2x + 1$
  • $f(x) = \sin x$

Answer: $f(x) = 2x+1$

If $2x+1 = 2y+1$ then $x = y$, so it is injective (and being a non-constant linear function, surjective too, hence bijective). The other three all fail: $f(2) = f(-2)$ for the first two, and $\sin 0 = \sin 2\pi$ for the last.

A mapping $f: S \to T$ has an inverse exactly when it is —

  • injective.
  • bijective.
  • surjective.
  • defined on a finite set.

Answer: bijective

Surjectivity guarantees every $y\in T$ has at least one pre-image, so $f^{-1}(y)$ has something to be; injectivity guarantees at most one, so the choice is unambiguous. Either alone is not enough.

Let $f(x) = x + 2$ and $g(x) = x^2 - 6$. What is $(g\circ f)(1)$?

Answer: $3$

$g\circ f$ means $f$ first: $f(1) = 3$, then $g(3) = 9 - 6 = 3$.

The other order gives something else entirely: $g(1) = -5$, then $f(-5) = -3$. The notation puts the last map first, which is the single commonest slip in this chapter.

$S = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and $f$ sends $1\to a$, $2\to a$, $3\to b$, $4\to b$. How many elements does the image of $f$ have?

Answer: $2$

The image is $\{a, b\}$ — only the elements actually hit. Four arrows, but they land on just two targets, so $f$ is not injective. Whether it is surjective depends on the codomain, which was not given.

Which statement about a mapping $f: S \to S$ is true for finite $S$ but can fail for infinite $S$?

  • Injective implies surjective.
  • Bijective implies invertible.
  • Composition is associative.
  • The image is a subset of the codomain.

Answer: injective implies surjective

On a finite set, $n$ distinct outputs must fill all $n$ slots. On the positive integers, $n\mapsto n+1$ is injective yet misses $1$ entirely — so it is not surjective.

The other three hold for any sets whatsoever.

On $\{1,2,3\}$, $\sigma$ sends $1\to2,\,2\to3,\,3\to1$ and $\tau$ swaps $1$ and $2$. What does $\sigma\circ\tau$ (apply $\tau$ first) do to $1$?

  • Sends it to $2$.
  • Sends it to $3$.
  • Leaves it at $1$.
  • It is undefined.

Answer: sends it to $3$

$\tau$ sends $1$ to $2$, and $\sigma$ sends $2$ to $3$. So $(\sigma\circ\tau)(1) = 3$.

In the other order, $\sigma$ sends $1$ to $2$ and $\tau$ sends $2$ back to $1$, so $(\tau\circ\sigma)(1) = 1$ — a different answer from the same two permutations.

How many injective mappings are there from a set of $5$ elements to a set of $4$?

Answer: $0$

Injectivity would need five distinct outputs among four available elements, which is impossible — two of the five must collide.

This is the pigeonhole principle, and it is the reason that in any group of $400$ people, two share a birthday.

$S$ is the set of people in a room and $T$ the days of the year. Which rule is not a mapping $S \to T$?

  • Each person to their birthday.
  • Each person to the first of January.
  • Each day to the people born on it.
  • Each person to the day one week after their birthday.

Answer: each day to the people born on it

It runs the wrong way — from $T$ to $S$ — and worse, it assigns a set of people to each day, possibly empty and possibly large. A mapping must give exactly one element of the codomain.

The second option is a perfectly good mapping, merely a constant one: dull, but legal.

How many bijections are there from $\{1,2,3,4\}$ to itself?

Answer: $24$

Bijections from a finite set to itself are exactly its permutations, and there are $4! = 24$ of them.

Together with composition they form a group — the same structure as the eight symmetries of the square in Chapter 6, which in fact sit inside this one as a subgroup permuting the four corners.

Recap

  • A mapping $f: S\to T$ gives each element of $S$ exactly one element of $T$; the image is what it actually hits, which may be smaller than the codomain.
  • $g\circ f$ means $f$ first. Composition is associative but not commutative.
  • Injective: no collisions. Surjective: nothing missed. Bijective: both.
  • $f$ is invertible exactly when it is bijective — surjectivity supplies a pre-image, injectivity makes it unique.
  • A permutation is a bijection of a set to itself; a finite set of $n$ elements has $n!$ of them, and they form a group.
