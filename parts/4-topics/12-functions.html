Part IV — Miscellaneous · Chapter 12

Functions

A function is a rule with a domain: give it an input from its domain and it returns exactly one output. That is the whole definition, and getting it exactly right is worth more than any amount of practice at "finding $f(3)$".

By the end of this chapter you can state what a function is and why the domain is part of it, apply the vertical line test, describe a polynomial by degree and roots, predict how shifts and stretches move a graph, work with $a^x$ for any real $x$, and use logarithms to turn products into sums and solve equations with the unknown in the exponent.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with powers and roots (Chapter 3), with parabolas (Chapter 4), and with lines in coordinates (Chapter 10). Those are the graphs this chapter generalises.

12.1 Definition of a function

Definition

A function $f$ consists of a set $D$, its domain, together with a rule assigning to each $x \in D$ exactly one number $f(x)$, its value at $x$. The set of values that actually occur is the image (or range).

Two clauses carry all the weight. Each $x$ in the domain must get a value — no gaps. And exactly one value — no ambiguity. A rule like "$y$ such that $y^2 = x$" is not a function, because $x = 4$ gives both $2$ and $-2$; restricting to the positive root repairs it, and that is what the symbol $\sqrt{\;}$ means.

The domain is part of the function

$f(x) = x^2$ on all real numbers and $g(x) = x^2$ on $x \ge 0$ are different functions, even though the formula is identical. The first takes every value twice; the second takes each once, and only the second can be undone. When a domain is not stated, the convention is to take the largest set on which the formula makes sense — which for $1/(x-2)$ excludes $2$, and for $\sqrt{x-3}$ means $x \ge 3$.

Example 1. Finding a natural domain

What is the natural domain of $f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x+4}}{x-1}$?

  1. The square root needs $x + 4 \ge 0$, that is, $x \ge -4$.
  2. The denominator must not vanish: $x \neq 1$.
  3. Both conditions at once: $x \ge -4$ and $x \neq 1$.
  4. So the domain is $[-4, 1) \cup (1, \infty)$. $\blacksquare$

Two rules cover almost every case at this level: no negative under an even root, no zero in a denominator.

12.2 Polynomial functions

A polynomial is a function built from $x$ by adding and multiplying only:

$$f(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0,$$

with $a_n \neq 0$. The number $n$ is the degree. Degree $1$ is the linear functions of Chapter 10, degree $2$ the quadratics of Chapter 4. The domain is always all of $\mathbf{R}$: there is nothing to divide by and nothing to take a root of.

A number $r$ with $f(r) = 0$ is a root. Roots and factors are two views of one thing:

The factor theorem

$r$ is a root of $f$ if and only if $(x - r)$ divides $f(x)$ — that is, $f(x) = (x-r)\,q(x)$ for some polynomial $q$ of degree one less.

One direction is immediate: if $f(x) = (x-r)q(x)$ then $f(r) = 0 \cdot q(r) = 0$. The other direction is division with remainder: dividing $f(x)$ by $(x-r)$ leaves a constant remainder $c$, so $f(x) = (x-r)q(x) + c$; putting $x = r$ gives $c = f(r) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

A consequence worth stating: a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, since each root pulls out a factor and the degree cannot drop below zero. A cubic has at most three — one, two, or three, depending on whether a root repeats — but never four.

12.3 Graphs of functions

The graph of $f$ is the set of points $(x, f(x))$ for $x$ in the domain. Since each $x$ gets exactly one value, we get a test that can be applied by eye:

The vertical line test

A set of points in the plane is the graph of a function exactly when no vertical line meets it more than once.

A circle fails this test — most vertical lines cut it twice — which is why $x^2+y^2=1$ defines a curve but not a function of $x$. Splitting it into upper and lower halves gives two functions, $y = \pm\sqrt{1-x^2}$.

Once you know one graph, you know a whole family of them. Replacing $f(x)$ by $af(x-h)+k$ moves and stretches the curve without changing its shape.

Transformations
  • $f(x) + k$: shift up by $k$.
  • $f(x - h)$: shift right by $h$. Note the sign — subtracting inside moves right.
  • $a\,f(x)$: stretch vertically by $|a|$, flipping over the $x$-axis if $a < 0$.
  • $f(-x)$: reflect in the $y$-axis.
Why subtracting shifts right

The new graph at $x$ shows what the old graph had at $x - h$. To see the old value at $0$ you now have to look at $x = h$. So everything the old curve did has been postponed by $h$ — moved to the right. Argue it this way once and you will never have to guess the direction again.

12.4 The exponential function

Chapter 3 defined $a^x$ for integer and then fractional $x$: $a^n$ is repeated multiplication, $a^{-n} = 1/a^n$, and $a^{p/q} = \sqrt[q]{a^p}$. That covers every rational exponent. The remaining step is to define $a^x$ for irrational $x$ — for instance $2^{\sqrt2}$.

The idea is to squeeze. Rational numbers come arbitrarily close to $\sqrt2$ from both sides ($1.4$, $1.41$, $1.414$, …), the corresponding powers $2^{1.4}, 2^{1.41}, \dots$ close in on a single number, and that number is defined to be $2^{\sqrt2}$. The same argument as for the area of a circle: approximate with things you understand, and take the limit.

The exponential function

For a fixed $a > 0$, the function $f(x) = a^x$ is defined for every real $x$ and satisfies $$a^{x+y} = a^x a^y, \qquad (a^x)^y = a^{xy}, \qquad a^0 = 1.$$ It is positive everywhere, increasing if $a > 1$ and decreasing if $a < 1$, and never takes the value $0$.

Exponential is not "very fast"

Exponential growth means the ratio over a fixed step is constant: each unit of $x$ multiplies the value by $a$. That is different in kind from $x^2$ or $x^{10}$, where the ratio depends on where you are. Given enough room, $2^x$ overtakes $x^{10}$ and never looks back — the contest is not close, though it takes until $x \approx 59$ for it to be visible.

12.5 Logarithms

Since $a^x$ (for $a > 1$) is increasing, it takes each positive value exactly once. So it can be undone, and the undoing has a name.

Definition

For $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, and $y > 0$, the logarithm $\log_a y$ is the unique number $x$ with $a^x = y$. In words: the exponent you need.

Its domain is the positive numbers — negative numbers and zero have no logarithm, because $a^x$ is never zero or negative.

Each law of exponents becomes a law of logarithms, by reading it backwards.

Laws of logarithms

$$\log_a(uv) = \log_a u + \log_a v, \qquad \log_a\!\left(\frac uv\right) = \log_a u - \log_a v, \qquad \log_a(u^k) = k\log_a u.$$

Proof of the first. Let $x = \log_a u$ and $y = \log_a v$, so $a^x = u$ and $a^y = v$. Then $uv = a^x a^y = a^{x+y}$, and by the definition of the logarithm, $\log_a(uv) = x + y$. $\blacksquare$

This is the property that made logarithms historically indispensable: they convert multiplication into addition, which is far easier to do by hand. The slide rule was a physical realisation of exactly this identity.

Example 2. Solving for an exponent

Solve $3 \cdot 2^{x} = 96$.

  1. Divide by $3$: $2^x = 32$.
  2. Recognise $32 = 2^5$, so $x = 5$. When the numbers cooperate, no logarithm is needed.
  3. If they had not cooperated — say $2^x = 30$ — take logs of both sides: $x\log 2 = \log 30$, so $x = \log 30/\log 2 \approx 4.907$.
  4. Check: $2^5 = 32$ and $3 \times 32 = 96$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Example 3. Changing base

Express $\log_2 7$ using logarithms to base $10$.

  1. Let $x = \log_2 7$, so $2^x = 7$.
  2. Take $\log_{10}$ of both sides: $\log_{10}(2^x) = \log_{10} 7$.
  3. By the third law, $x\log_{10}2 = \log_{10}7$.
  4. So $x = \dfrac{\log_{10}7}{\log_{10}2} \approx \dfrac{0.845}{0.301} \approx 2.807$.
  5. Plausible: $2^2 = 4$ and $2^3 = 8$, so the answer must lie between $2$ and $3$, nearer $3$. $\blacksquare$

In general $\log_a y = \dfrac{\log_b y}{\log_b a}$ — which is why a calculator with only two logarithm buttons can compute every base.

Practice

Give exact answers where possible; otherwise three decimal places.

If $f(x) = x^2 - 2x - 2$, what is $f(-3)$?

Answer: $13$

$f(-3) = (-3)^2 - 2(-3) - 2 = 9 + 6 - 2 = 13$. Both minus signs matter: $(-3)^2$ is $+9$, and $-2(-3)$ is $+6$.

What is the natural domain of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 5}$?

  • All real numbers.
  • $x > 5$.
  • All real numbers except $5$.
  • $x \ge 5$.

Answer: all real numbers except $5$

Only $x = 5$ causes trouble, making the denominator zero. Every other real number is fine, including numbers far below $5$ — the function is negative there, which is not a problem.

Which of these is not the graph of a function of $x$?

  • The line $y = 3x - 1$.
  • The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$.
  • The parabola $y = x^2$.
  • The curve $y = |x|$.

Answer: the circle

A vertical line such as $x = 0$ meets the circle at $(0,2)$ and $(0,-2)$ — two outputs for one input, so it is not a function. The other three pass the test everywhere.

The circle can be split into two functions, $y = \sqrt{4-x^2}$ and $y = -\sqrt{4-x^2}$, each perfectly respectable.

What is $\log_2 8$?

Answer: $3$

The logarithm asks for the exponent: $2^3 = 8$, so $\log_2 8 = 3$. Reading a logarithm as a question — "what power?" — makes most of them answerable on sight.

What is $\log_3\!\left(\tfrac19\right)$?

Answer: $-2$

$3^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{3^2} = \dfrac19$, so the exponent required is $-2$.

Logarithms of numbers between $0$ and $1$ are negative (for base $> 1$), and the logarithm of $0$ or of a negative number does not exist at all.

Solve $5 \cdot 2^{x} = 80$.

Answer: $x = 4$

Divide by $5$: $2^x = 16 = 2^4$, so $x = 4$. Since $2^x$ takes each positive value exactly once, that is the only solution.

How does the graph of $y = (x-3)^2$ compare with that of $y = x^2$?

  • Shifted $3$ to the left.
  • Shifted $3$ to the right.
  • Shifted $3$ down.
  • Stretched vertically by $3$.

Answer: shifted $3$ to the right

The new graph at $x$ shows what the old one had at $x - 3$, so the vertex, formerly at $x=0$, is now at $x=3$. Check directly: $y = 0$ when $x = 3$.

What is $7^{0}$?

Answer: $1$

The law $a^{x+y} = a^xa^y$ with $y = 0$ gives $a^x = a^x \cdot a^0$, and dividing by the non-zero $a^x$ leaves $a^0 = 1$. So it is forced, not chosen: any other value would break the law of exponents.

How many real roots does $f(x) = x^3 + x + 1$ have? (It has at least one, since it is negative at $x=-1$ and positive at $x=0$.)

Answer: $1$

Degree $3$ allows at most three. But each of $x^3$ and $x$ increases as $x$ does, so $f$ is increasing everywhere — it can cross zero only once.

The bracketed remark supplies the existence: $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(0) = 1$, so the graph must cross the axis between them. Exactly one root.

Compute $\log_2 7$ to three decimal places. ($\log_{10}7 \approx 0.8451$, $\log_{10}2 \approx 0.3010$.)

Answer: $\approx 2.807$

By the change of base formula, $$\log_2 7 = \frac{\log_{10}7}{\log_{10}2} \approx \frac{0.8451}{0.3010} \approx 2.807.$$

The check that matters: $2^2 = 4 < 7 < 8 = 2^3$, so the answer had to be between $2$ and $3$.

Recap

  • A function is a domain plus a rule giving exactly one value to each element of it — and the domain is part of the function, not an afterthought.
  • The vertical line test is that definition, applied by eye.
  • For polynomials, $r$ is a root exactly when $(x-r)$ is a factor; degree $n$ allows at most $n$ roots.
  • $af(x-h)+k$ stretches by $a$, shifts right by $h$, and up by $k$ — with $x - h$ moving the graph the opposite way to the sign.
  • $a^x$ extends to all real $x$ by squeezing with rationals; it is positive everywhere and never zero.
  • $\log_a$ inverts it, turning products into sums, and $\log_a y = \log_b y / \log_b a$ changes base.
