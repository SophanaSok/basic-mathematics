Part IV — Miscellaneous · Chapter 15

Induction and Summations

How do you prove something about every positive integer, when there are infinitely many of them and you have finite time? You prove the first case, and you prove that each case drags the next one along behind it.

By the end of this chapter you can write and check a proof by induction, identify where the inductive hypothesis is used, read and manipulate sigma notation, derive and apply the standard sums, and sum a geometric series both for finite $n$ and — when $|r| < 1$ — in the limit.

15.1 Induction

Suppose that for each positive integer $n$ there is a statement $S(n)$ — for instance, "$1 + 2 + \dots + n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$". We want to prove all of them.

The principle of induction

Suppose that

  1. Base case. $S(1)$ is true; and
  2. Inductive step. for every $n$, if $S(n)$ is true then $S(n+1)$ is true.

Then $S(n)$ is true for every positive integer $n$.

The image everyone uses is a row of dominoes: knock over the first, and make sure each one is close enough to topple its neighbour. Both parts are needed, and neither is enough alone. The statement "$n = n+1$" satisfies the inductive step perfectly — if $n = n+1$ then adding $1$ gives $n+1 = n+2$ — and is false throughout, because the base case fails. The dominoes are well placed; nobody pushed the first one.

Example 1. The sum of the first $n$ integers

Prove that $1 + 2 + \dots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ for every $n \ge 1$.

  1. Base case. For $n=1$ the left side is $1$ and the right side is $\frac{1\cdot2}{2} = 1$. ✓
  2. Inductive step. Assume the formula holds for some $n$ — this assumption is the inductive hypothesis. We must deduce it for $n+1$.
  3. The sum to $n+1$ is the sum to $n$ plus one more term, and here the hypothesis enters: $$\underbrace{1 + \dots + n}_{= \frac{n(n+1)}{2}} + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + (n+1).$$
  4. Factor out $(n+1)$: $$(n+1)\left(\frac n2 + 1\right) = (n+1)\cdot\frac{n+2}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}.$$
  5. That is exactly the formula with $n+1$ in place of $n$. Base case plus inductive step, so the formula holds for every $n$. $\blacksquare$
Induction proves, it does not discover

Notice that the proof verified a formula someone already had. Induction is a checking machine: it will confirm a correct guess and refuse a wrong one, but it will not hand you the guess. Gauss's trick does that here — pair the sum $1+2+\dots+n$ with itself written backwards, so each of the $n$ columns totals $n+1$, giving $2S = n(n+1)$.

Where the hypothesis must appear

In any honest inductive step there is one moment where you use the assumption for $n$ — in Example 1, replacing $1+\dots+n$ by $\frac{n(n+1)}2$. If you write out a step and find you never used it, you have either proved something stronger directly or made a mistake. Point at the moment of use; it is the thing an examiner and a reader both look for.

15.2 Summations

Writing $1 + 2 + \dots + n$ is fine until the terms get complicated. The compact notation is

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n.$$

Here $k$ is the index, running over the integers from the bottom limit to the top one. The index is a dummy: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2$ and $\sum_{j=1}^{n} j^2$ are the same number, just as the $x$ in $f(x) = x^2$ could have been anything.

Two rules, and three sums

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k + b_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k + \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} c\,a_k = c\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k.$$ And three standard results: $$\sum_{k=1}^{n} 1 = n, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^{2} = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}.$$

The two rules are just the commutative and distributive laws of Chapter 1, applied to a long but finite sum. What you may not do is pull a sum through a product: $\sum a_kb_k$ is not $\left(\sum a_k\right)\left(\sum b_k\right)$, as $1\cdot1 + 2\cdot2 = 5$ against $(1+2)(1+2) = 9$ makes clear.

Example 2. Using the standard sums

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} (3k^2 - 2k + 4)$.

  1. Split the sum and pull out the constants: $$3\sum_{k=1}^{20}k^2 \;-\; 2\sum_{k=1}^{20}k \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{20}4.$$
  2. $\sum k^2 = \frac{20\cdot21\cdot41}{6} = 2870$.
  3. $\sum k = \frac{20\cdot21}{2} = 210$.
  4. $\sum 4 = 4\times20 = 80$. A constant summed $n$ times is $cn$ — not $c$.
  5. Total: $3(2870) - 2(210) + 80 = 8610 - 420 + 80 = 8270$. $\blacksquare$

15.3 Geometric series

A geometric sum is one where each term is a fixed multiple of the one before:

$$S_n = 1 + r + r^2 + \dots + r^{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} r^{k}.$$

There is a closed form, and the derivation is a trick worth keeping.

Example 3. Summing it, by subtraction

Find a closed form for $S_n = 1 + r + \dots + r^n$, assuming $r \neq 1$.

  1. Multiply through by $r$: $$rS_n = r + r^2 + \dots + r^{n} + r^{n+1}.$$
  2. Subtract. Almost everything cancels — the two sums share every term but the first of one and the last of the other: $$S_n - rS_n = 1 - r^{n+1}.$$
  3. Factor the left side: $S_n(1-r) = 1 - r^{n+1}$.
  4. Divide by $1-r$, legal since $r\neq1$: $$\boxed{\;S_n = \frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}\;}$$ $\blacksquare$

At $r = 1$ the formula is $0/0$ and the sum is simply $n+1$, every term being $1$. The excluded case is excluded for a reason.

Now let $n$ grow. If $|r| < 1$ then $r^{n+1}$ shrinks towards $0$ — each multiplication by $r$ makes it smaller — so the partial sums close in on a single number:

The infinite geometric series

For $|r| < 1$, $$1 + r + r^2 + r^3 + \dots = \frac{1}{1-r}.$$ For $|r| \ge 1$ the terms do not shrink and the sum has no limit.

$0.999\ldots = 1$, settled

The decimal $0.999\dots$ means $\frac9{10} + \frac9{100} + \frac9{1000} + \dots$, which is $\frac9{10}$ times a geometric series with $r = \frac1{10}$: $$\frac{9}{10}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \frac1{10}} = \frac{9}{10}\cdot\frac{10}{9} = 1.$$ Not "nearly $1$" and not "$1$ by convention" — the infinite decimal is defined as the limit of its partial sums, and that limit is exactly $1$.

Example 4. An inequality by induction

Prove that $2^n > n$ for every positive integer $n$.

  1. Base case. $n=1$: $2^1 = 2 > 1$ ✓.
  2. Inductive step. Assume $2^n > n$.
  3. Then $2^{n+1} = 2\cdot 2^n > 2n$, using the hypothesis.
  4. And $2n \ge n+1$ whenever $n \ge 1$, since $2n - (n+1) = n - 1 \ge 0$.
  5. Chaining: $2^{n+1} > 2n \ge n+1$, which is the statement for $n+1$. $\blacksquare$

Induction is not only for equalities. The pattern is identical: establish the first case, then show each case forces the next.

Practice

Give numerical answers as exact numbers or fractions.

Compute $1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + 100$.

Answer: $5050$

$\dfrac{100 \times 101}{2} = 5050$. This is the sum the young Gauss is said to have done in his head, by pairing $1$ with $100$, $2$ with $99$, and so on — fifty pairs each totalling $101$.

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{10} k^{2}$.

Answer: $385$

$\dfrac{10 \times 11 \times 21}{6} = \dfrac{2310}{6} = 385$.

Rough check: the average of $1, 4, 9, \ldots, 100$ is a bit under $40$, and there are ten terms — so a few hundred is the right size.

What is $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \dots$?

Answer: $2$

$\dfrac{1}{1-\frac12} = 2$. Each term covers half of the remaining distance to $2$, so the partial sums $1, 1.5, 1.75, 1.875, \dots$ approach $2$ without ever reaching it.

Compute $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16$.

Answer: $31$

By the formula with $r = 2$ and $n = 4$: $\dfrac{1-2^5}{1-2} = \dfrac{-31}{-1} = 31$. Directly: $1+2+4+8+16 = 31$ ✓.

The general pattern $2^{n+1} - 1$ is why $n$ binary digits reach exactly $2^n - 1$.

A student "proves" $n = n+1$ by noting that if $n = n+1$ then adding $1$ gives $n+1 = n+2$. What is wrong?

  • The inductive step is invalid.
  • There is no base case — no value of $n$ where the statement is known true.
  • Induction only works for statements about sums.
  • The statement should have been checked for negative $n$ as well.

Answer: there is no base case

The inductive step really is valid: the implication "if $n = n+1$ then $n+1=n+2$" is true. But an implication tells you nothing until you know its hypothesis holds somewhere — the point the interlude in Part I made about implication and its converse.

The dominoes are perfectly spaced, and nobody ever pushed the first.

What is $1 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{27} + \dots$?

Answer: $\tfrac32$

$\dfrac{1}{1-\frac13} = \dfrac{1}{\frac23} = \dfrac32$.

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5} (2k - 1)$.

Answer: $25$

$2\sum_{k=1}^{5}k - \sum_{k=1}^{5}1 = 2(15) - 5 = 25$. Directly: $1+3+5+7+9 = 25$.

The first $n$ odd numbers always sum to $n^2$ — which is worth a moment: a square of side $n$ can be built from $n$ L-shaped strips of sizes $1, 3, 5, \dots$

In an inductive step, what role does the inductive hypothesis play?

  • It is what you are trying to prove, so it may not be assumed.
  • It replaces the base case.
  • It is assumed true for $n$ and used to derive the statement for $n+1$.
  • It must be verified separately for each $n$.

Answer: assumed for $n$, used to derive $n+1$

You are not assuming what you set out to prove. What is being proved is the implication $S(n) \Rightarrow S(n+1)$, and the standard way to prove an implication is to assume the hypothesis and derive the conclusion.

If you finish an inductive step without ever having used $S(n)$, something is wrong — or you have proved the result directly and do not need induction.

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=5}^{10} k$.

Answer: $45$

$\sum_{k=1}^{10}k - \sum_{k=1}^{4}k = 55 - 10 = 45$. Directly: $5+6+7+8+9+10 = 45$ ✓.

Shifting the lower limit is the most common source of off-by-one errors here. Subtract the sum up to one below the new starting point — up to $4$, not up to $5$.

For which $r$ does the infinite series $1 + r + r^2 + \dots$ have a finite sum?

  • Every $r$.
  • Exactly when $|r| < 1$.
  • Exactly when $r > 0$.
  • Exactly when $r$ is rational.

Answer: exactly when $|r| < 1$

The partial sum is $\frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}$, and this settles down only if $r^{n+1}\to0$, which happens precisely when $|r| < 1$. At $r = 1$ the sum is $n+1$ and grows without bound; at $r = -1$ the partial sums alternate $1, 0, 1, 0, \dots$ and never settle.

Recap

  • Induction proves $S(n)$ for all $n$ from two ingredients: a base case, and $S(n)\Rightarrow S(n+1)$. Drop either and it collapses.
  • The inductive hypothesis must actually be used somewhere in the step — find the moment and point at it.
  • Sigma notation packages a long sum; sums split over addition and let constants out, but never distribute over products.
  • $\sum k = \frac{n(n+1)}2$ and $\sum k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$ are worth knowing.
  • $1+r+\dots+r^n = \frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}$, and for $|r|<1$ the infinite sum is $\frac1{1-r}$ — which is what makes $0.999\dots$ equal to $1$.
