Part IV — Miscellaneous · Chapter 14

Complex Numbers

Chapter 4 left a gap: when $b^2-4ac < 0$ a quadratic has no root, because no real number squares to a negative. We close the gap by enlarging the number system — and get, unexpectedly, a second language for the rotations of Chapter 11.

By the end of this chapter you can add, multiply, and divide complex numbers; use the conjugate to rationalise a denominator and to compute $|z|$; plot complex numbers and read $|z-w|$ as a distance; convert to polar form; explain why multiplying multiplies lengths and adds angles; and find the $n$th roots of a complex number.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with quadratics that have no real root (Chapter 4), with points of the plane (Chapter 9), and with the addition formulas (Chapter 11), which is where multiplication-as-rotation comes from.

14.1 Arithmetic of complex numbers

Introduce a new symbol $i$ with the single defining property

$$i^{2} = -1,$$

and consider all expressions $a + bi$ with $a$ and $b$ real. These are the complex numbers; $a$ is the real part and $b$ the imaginary part. Two are equal exactly when their real parts agree and their imaginary parts agree.

Now insist that the usual rules of algebra keep working. That decides every computation. For addition, collect like terms:

$$(a+bi) + (c+di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i.$$

For multiplication, expand as usual and then use $i^2 = -1$ on the last term:

$$(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^{2} = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.$$
Nothing was assumed but consistency

We did not choose a multiplication rule; we chose $i^2=-1$ and then let the distributive law of Chapter 1 do the rest. That is the right way to extend a number system: add the minimum, keep the laws, and see what is forced.

Division needs one device. The conjugate of $z = a+bi$ is $\bar z = a - bi$, and the product of a number with its conjugate is real:

$$z\bar z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^{2} - (bi)^{2} = a^{2}+b^{2}.$$

So to divide, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom, and the denominator becomes an ordinary real number.

Example 1. Dividing complex numbers

Compute $\dfrac{3+2i}{1-4i}$.

  1. Multiply numerator and denominator by $\overline{1-4i} = 1+4i$: $$\frac{(3+2i)(1+4i)}{(1-4i)(1+4i)}.$$
  2. Denominator: $1^2 + 4^2 = 17$ — real, as promised.
  3. Numerator: $3 + 12i + 2i + 8i^2 = 3 + 14i - 8 = -5 + 14i$.
  4. So the quotient is $\dfrac{-5+14i}{17} = -\dfrac{5}{17} + \dfrac{14}{17}i$.
  5. Check by multiplying back: $\left(-\tfrac5{17}+\tfrac{14}{17}i\right)(1-4i) = -\tfrac5{17} + \tfrac{20}{17}i + \tfrac{14}{17}i + \tfrac{56}{17} = \tfrac{51}{17} + \tfrac{34}{17}i = 3 + 2i$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The same trick as rationalising $\frac{1}{1-\sqrt2}$ in Chapter 3 — multiply by the conjugate and use a difference of squares. Only the meaning of "conjugate" has changed.

With division available, the complex numbers satisfy every rule listed in Chapter 1 for the rationals and reals: addition and multiplication are commutative and associative, they distribute, $0$ and $1$ behave, and every non-zero $z$ has an inverse $\bar z/|z|^2$. What is lost is order — there is no sensible way to say one complex number is less than another. In exchange, every quadratic now has roots.

Example 2. The quadratic with no real root, solved

Solve $x^2 - 4x + 13 = 0$.

  1. $\Delta = 16 - 52 = -36$. Chapter 4 stopped here.
  2. Now $\sqrt{-36} = 6i$, since $(6i)^2 = 36i^2 = -36$.
  3. The formula gives $x = \dfrac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$.
  4. Check $x = 2+3i$: $(2+3i)^2 = 4 + 12i - 9 = -5+12i$, and $-5 + 12i - 4(2+3i) + 13 = -5+12i-8-12i+13 = 0$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

The two roots are conjugates of each other. That always happens when the coefficients are real, and it is why complex roots arrive in pairs.

14.2 The complex plane

A complex number is determined by two real numbers, so it is naturally a point: plot $a+bi$ at $(a,b)$. The horizontal axis carries the real numbers, the vertical one the multiples of $i$.

In this picture, addition of complex numbers is the addition of points from Chapter 9 — the parallelogram law, unchanged. And the quantity $z\bar z = a^2+b^2$ is the square of the distance to the origin.

Absolute value

$$|z| = \sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}} = \sqrt{z\bar z},$$ the distance from $z$ to the origin. Consequently $|z - w|$ is the distance between $z$ and $w$, and $|z| = r$ describes a circle of radius $r$ about the origin.

This extends $|x|$ from Chapter 3 exactly: for a real $x$, $|x| = \sqrt{x^2}$ is the old absolute value. And the multiplicative property $|zw| = |z||w|$ holds, which is much less obvious — §14.3 makes it clear.

14.3 Polar form

A point in the plane can be described by its distance from the origin and its angle. For $z \neq 0$ write $r = |z|$ and let $\theta$ be the angle from the positive real axis. Then the point is $(r\cos\theta,\; r\sin\theta)$, so

$$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta).$$

This is the polar form; $\theta$ is the argument of $z$, determined up to adding multiples of $2\pi$. Now multiply two numbers in this form and watch what happens:

$$\begin{aligned} & r(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)\cdot s(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) \\ &\quad = rs\bigl[(\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta)\bigr] \\ &\quad = rs\bigl[\cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta)\bigr]. \end{aligned}$$

The addition formulas of Chapter 11 appeared without being invited — they are exactly the terms the expansion produces.

Multiplication is rotate-and-scale

$$|zw| = |z|\,|w|, \qquad \arg(zw) = \arg z + \arg w.$$ Multiplying by $w$ stretches the plane by $|w|$ and rotates it by $\arg w$.

What $i$ really is

$i$ has $|i| = 1$ and argument $90°$, so multiplying by $i$ is precisely a quarter turn counterclockwise. Then $i^2$ is two quarter turns — a half turn — which sends $1$ to $-1$. The defining equation $i^2 = -1$, which looks like an arbitrary act of will, is the statement that turning twice through a right angle reverses direction.

Example 3. Powers, and De Moivre's formula

Compute $(1+i)^{8}$.

  1. Polar form of $1+i$: $r = \sqrt{1+1} = \sqrt2$, and the point $(1,1)$ sits at $45° = \pi/4$.
  2. Multiplying a number by itself multiplies the lengths and adds the angles, so raising to the $n$th power gives $$z^{n} = r^{n}\bigl(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta\bigr),$$ which is De Moivre's formula.
  3. Here $n = 8$: $r^8 = (\sqrt2)^8 = 2^4 = 16$, and $8\theta = 8\cdot45° = 360°$.
  4. $\cos360° = 1$, $\sin360° = 0$, so $(1+i)^8 = 16$.
  5. Spot check: $(1+i)^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i$, so $(1+i)^8 = (2i)^4 = 16i^4 = 16$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Expanding $(1+i)^8$ by brute force takes a page. In polar form it takes a line, because multiplication has been converted into addition of angles.

Example 4. The cube roots of $1$

Find all complex $z$ with $z^3 = 1$.

  1. Write $z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. Then $z^3 = r^3(\cos3\theta + i\sin3\theta)$, and we need this to be $1$.
  2. Matching lengths: $r^3 = 1$, so $r = 1$ — all three roots are on the unit circle.
  3. Matching angles: $3\theta$ must be a multiple of $360°$, so $\theta = 0°,\,120°,\,240°$. Larger multiples repeat: $\theta = 360°$ is the same point as $0°$.
  4. So the three roots are $1$, and $-\tfrac12 \pm \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ — the vertices of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the unit circle. $\blacksquare$

The pattern is general: the $n$th roots of any non-zero complex number are $n$ points evenly spaced around a circle. Over the reals, $x^3=1$ had one solution; over the complex numbers it has exactly three, which is what the degree promised all along.

Practice

Write complex numbers in the form 3+2i or 3-2i, with no spaces.

Compute $(4+5i) + (3-4i)$.

Answer: $7 + i$

Real parts: $4+3 = 7$. Imaginary parts: $5 + (-4) = 1$. So the sum is $7+i$ — coordinatewise addition, exactly as for points in the plane.

Compute $(3+4i)(2-2i)$.

Answer: $14 + 2i$

Expanding: $6 - 6i + 8i - 8i^2$. The last term is $-8(-1) = +8$, so the total is $(6+8) + (-6+8)i = 14 + 2i$.

Check with absolute values, which must multiply: $|3+4i| = 5$ and $|2-2i| = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$, so the product should have absolute value $10\sqrt2 \approx 14.14$. And indeed $|14+2i| = \sqrt{196+4} = \sqrt{200} = 10\sqrt2$ ✓.

What is $|5 - 12i|$?

Answer: $13$

$|5-12i| = \sqrt{25+144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$ — the distance from the origin to the point $(5,-12)$, which is the $5,12,13$ triangle again.

What is the conjugate of $2 + 7i$?

Answer: $2 - 7i$

Conjugation reflects in the real axis: $\overline{a+bi} = a-bi$. Note $(2+7i)(2-7i) = 4 + 49 = 53$, real and equal to $|2+7i|^2$.

What is $i^{6}$?

Answer: $-1$

$i^6 = (i^2)^3 = (-1)^3 = -1$. The powers cycle with period $4$: $i, -1, -i, 1, i, -1, \dots$ — because multiplying by $i$ is a quarter turn, and four of those bring you home.

Multiplying a complex number by $i$ does what to its plot in the plane?

  • Reflects it in the real axis.
  • Doubles its distance from the origin.
  • Rotates it $90°$ counterclockwise about the origin.
  • Leaves it where it is.

Answer: rotates it $90°$ counterclockwise

$|i| = 1$ so no stretching occurs, and $\arg i = 90°$, so the argument increases by $90°$. Reflection in the real axis is conjugation, a different operation entirely.

The solutions of $z^2 = -9$ are $z = ki$ for two values of $k$. What are they?

Answer: $k = 3$ and $k = -3$

$(ki)^2 = k^2 i^2 = -k^2$, so we need $-k^2 = -9$, giving $k = \pm3$. The solutions are $3i$ and $-3i$ — conjugates, as always for a real-coefficient quadratic.

What is $|(4+7i) - (1+3i)|$?

Answer: $5$

The difference is $3+4i$, whose absolute value is $\sqrt{9+16} = 5$. Geometrically: the distance between the points $(4,7)$ and $(1,3)$.

If $|z| = 2$ and $|w| = 4$, what is $|zw|$?

Answer: $8$

Absolute values multiply: $|zw| = |z||w| = 2\times4 = 8$. The arguments meanwhile add — but since none was given, the product's position is unknown while its distance from the origin is not.

How many complex solutions does $z^4 = 1$ have?

Answer: $4$

Matching absolute values gives $|z| = 1$; matching arguments gives $4\theta = 0°, 360°, 720°, 1080°$, so $\theta = 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°$. The roots are $1, i, -1, -i$ — the corners of a square on the unit circle.

Over the reals only $1$ and $-1$ solve it. The two that were missing are precisely the ones that needed $i$.

Recap

  • Adjoin $i$ with $i^2=-1$ and keep the usual laws; addition and multiplication are then forced.
  • The conjugate $\bar z = a - bi$ satisfies $z\bar z = a^2+b^2$, which is how division is done.
  • Plotting $a+bi$ at $(a,b)$ makes addition the parallelogram law and $|z-w|$ the distance between two numbers.
  • In polar form $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, multiplication multiplies the $r$'s and adds the $\theta$'s — the addition formulas, wearing a new hat.
  • Hence De Moivre's formula, and the fact that $z^n = c$ always has exactly $n$ solutions, evenly spaced around a circle.
