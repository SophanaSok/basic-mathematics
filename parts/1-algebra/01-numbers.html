Part I — Algebra · Chapter 1

Numbers

You have been using the rules of arithmetic for years. This chapter asks where they come from — and finds that almost all of them follow from a very short list.

By the end of this chapter you can state the laws of addition and multiplication, explain from those laws why $(-1)(-1) = 1$, prove simple statements about all even and odd integers, add and multiply fractions and say why the rules are what they are, and explain precisely why division by zero is impossible.

1.1 The integers

Counting comes first: $1, 2, 3, \dots$ These are the positive integers. Add zero and the negatives and you have the integers:

$$\dots,\; -3,\; -2,\; -1,\; 0,\; 1,\; 2,\; 3,\; \dots$$

The best picture of them is a line. Choose a point and call it $0$, choose a unit of length, and step off that length repeatedly to the right for the positive integers and to the left for the negatives.

Why negatives at all?

Because subtraction should always be possible. With only positive integers, $3 - 5$ has no answer, and a rule with exceptions is a poor rule. Introducing negative numbers is the smallest repair that makes subtraction work every time — and it costs us nothing, because (as we will see) all the old rules of arithmetic survive.

On the line, the integer $-a$ sits at the same distance from $0$ as $a$, on the other side. Reflecting the line in the point $0$ exchanges $a$ and $-a$. That picture is worth keeping, because it explains the sign rules later on.

1.2 Rules for addition

Addition of integers obeys four rules. Read each one as a permission slip: it tells you something you are allowed to do to an expression without changing its value.

Rules for addition

For all integers $a$, $b$, $c$:

  1. Associativity. $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ — brackets in a sum never matter.
  2. Commutativity. $a + b = b + a$ — order in a sum never matters.
  3. Zero. $a + 0 = a$ — there is a number that changes nothing.
  4. Opposites. $a + (-a) = 0$ — every number has something that cancels it.

Rules 1 and 2 together are what let you write $a + b + c + d$ with no brackets and add the terms in any order you like. That freedom is so familiar it is invisible; it is worth noticing that it is a stated property, not a law of nature.

Rule 4 is what subtraction really is. We define

$$a - b = a + (-b),$$

so subtraction is not a fifth operation — it is addition of the opposite. Every fact about subtraction is therefore a fact about addition in disguise.

Example 1. The opposite of an opposite

Show that $-(-a) = a$.

  1. By Rule 4 applied to $-a$, the number $-(-a)$ is the one that adds to $-a$ to give $0$. So $(-a) + \big(-(-a)\big) = 0$.
  2. But also $(-a) + a = 0$, by Rule 4 applied to $a$ together with commutativity.
  3. So $-a$ has two cancelling partners: $-(-a)$ and $a$. If we know a cancelling partner is unique, these must be equal.
  4. Uniqueness: suppose $x$ and $y$ both satisfy $(-a) + x = 0$ and $(-a) + y = 0$. Then $$x = x + 0 = x + \big((-a) + y\big) = \big(x + (-a)\big) + y = 0 + y = y.$$ Rules used, in order: zero, the assumption about $y$, associativity, the assumption about $x$ with commutativity, zero again.
  5. Hence $-(-a) = a$. $\blacksquare$

Look at what that proof did not use: no pictures, no examples, no appeal to "minus a minus is a plus". Only the four rules. This is the pattern for the whole chapter.

A useful consequence: cancellation

If $a + c = b + c$, then $a = b$. (Add $-c$ to both sides and use associativity, opposites, and zero.) So you may always cancel a common term from both sides of an equation — and now you know why you are allowed to.

1.3 Rules for multiplication

Rules for multiplication

For all integers $a$, $b$, $c$:

  1. Associativity. $(ab)c = a(bc)$.
  2. Commutativity. $ab = ba$.
  3. One. $1 \cdot a = a$.
  4. Distributivity. $a(b + c) = ab + ac$.

The first three mirror the rules for addition. The fourth is the only rule that mentions both operations, and it is the engine of all of algebra. Every expansion you have ever done — $ (x+2)(x+3)$, $(a+b)^2$, long multiplication itself — is distributivity applied repeatedly.

Distributivity also decides the rules of signs. They are not conventions somebody chose; they are forced.

Example 2. Anything times zero is zero

Show that $a \cdot 0 = 0$ for every integer $a$.

  1. $0 + 0 = 0$, by the zero rule.
  2. Multiply by $a$ and use distributivity: $a\cdot 0 = a(0 + 0) = a\cdot 0 + a \cdot 0.$
  3. So $a \cdot 0 = a\cdot 0 + a\cdot 0$. Cancel one copy of $a \cdot 0$ from both sides (cancellation, above) to get $0 = a \cdot 0$. $\blacksquare$

Example 3. Why a negative times a positive is negative

Show that $(-a)b = -(ab)$.

  1. Saying "$(-a)b$ equals $-(ab)$" means exactly: $(-a)b$ is the number that adds to $ab$ to give $0$. That is what the symbol $-(ab)$ denotes. So this is what we must check.
  2. Compute the sum, pulling out the common factor $b$ with distributivity: $$ab + (-a)b = \big(a + (-a)\big)b = 0 \cdot b.$$
  3. By Example 2, $0 \cdot b = 0$.
  4. So $ab + (-a)b = 0$, which says $(-a)b$ is the opposite of $ab$; that is, $(-a)b = -(ab)$. $\blacksquare$

Example 4. Why a negative times a negative is positive

Show that $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

  1. Apply Example 3 with $b$ replaced by $-b$: $(-a)(-b) = -\big(a(-b)\big)$.
  2. Inside, $a(-b) = (-b)a = -(ba) = -(ab)$, using commutativity and Example 3 again.
  3. So $(-a)(-b) = -\big(-(ab)\big)$.
  4. By Example 1, the opposite of an opposite is the number itself: $-\big(-(ab)\big) = ab$. Hence $(-a)(-b) = ab$. $\blacksquare$

In particular $(-1)(-1) = 1$. There was never a choice about this. Once you insist that distributivity holds for negative numbers too, the sign rule is a theorem.

Watch out

$-a$ does not mean "a negative number". It means "the opposite of $a$". If $a = -4$ then $-a = 4$, which is positive. The minus sign is an instruction to reflect through $0$, not a label saying which side of $0$ you are on.

1.4 Even and odd integers

Here is the first place where we prove something about infinitely many numbers at once. The trick is always the same: give the arbitrary object a name and work with the name.

Definitions

An integer $n$ is even if $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, and odd if $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$.

Notice that these definitions are statements about existence: "even" means there is an integer $k$ doing the job. To use the definition, you name that $k$; to prove something is even, you must produce such a $k$.

Example 5. Odd plus odd is even

Let $m$ and $n$ be odd. Show $m + n$ is even.

  1. Because $m$ is odd, there is an integer $j$ with $m = 2j + 1$. Because $n$ is odd, there is an integer $k$ with $n = 2k + 1$. Two different letters — nothing says the two integers are the same.
  2. Add: $m + n = (2j + 1) + (2k + 1) = 2j + 2k + 2$.
  3. Factor out the $2$ using distributivity: $m + n = 2(j + k + 1)$.
  4. Now $j + k + 1$ is an integer, so we have exhibited the required integer, and $m + n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

Example 6. If $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even

A direct attack is awkward. Prove the equivalent statement if $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd instead — a move we will look at properly in the interlude on logic.

  1. Let $n$ be odd, say $n = 2k + 1$ with $k$ an integer.
  2. Square it: $n^2 = (2k+1)(2k+1) = 4k^2 + 4k + 1$. Expanded by distributivity, twice.
  3. Rewrite: $n^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, and $2k^2 + 2k$ is an integer.
  4. So $n^2$ is odd. Therefore an even square cannot come from an odd number, which is to say: if $n^2$ is even, $n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

Keep this one. It is exactly the lemma that shows $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction in Chapter 3.

1.5 Rational numbers

Integers let you subtract freely, but not divide: $3 \div 2$ is not an integer. The same repair works. A rational number is a quotient

$$\frac{m}{n}, \qquad m, n \text{ integers}, \quad n \neq 0.$$

A fraction is a single number, not a pair of numbers or an unfinished sum. On the number line, $\frac{m}{n}$ is found by cutting each unit into $n$ equal pieces and taking $m$ of them.

Equality, addition, multiplication

For integers with $n, q \neq 0$:

$$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{p}{q} \iff mq = np, \qquad \frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{p}{q} = \frac{mp}{nq}, \qquad \frac{m}{n} + \frac{p}{q} = \frac{mq + np}{nq}.$$

Two of these deserve comment. Equality is a rule because a rational number has many names: $\frac{2}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ are the same number, and the test $mq = np$ is how you check. Cancelling a common factor from top and bottom changes the name, never the number.

Addition looks like an arbitrary formula, and it is not. To add, both pieces must be cut the same size, so rewrite each fraction over the common denominator $nq$:

$$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{mq}{nq}, \qquad \frac{p}{q} = \frac{np}{nq}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{m}{n} + \frac{p}{q} = \frac{mq}{nq} + \frac{np}{nq} = \frac{mq + np}{nq}.$$

The formula is not a rule to memorise; it is the record of a two-line calculation.

Example 7. A sum of fractions, carefully

Compute $\dfrac{5}{6} - \dfrac{3}{4}$.

  1. Subtraction is addition of the opposite: $\dfrac{5}{6} + \left(-\dfrac{3}{4}\right)$.
  2. A common denominator: $6 \cdot 4 = 24$ works, though $12$ is smaller and also works. Use $12$: $\dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{10}{12}$ and $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{9}{12}$.
  3. Now the pieces match: $\dfrac{10}{12} - \dfrac{9}{12} = \dfrac{1}{12}$.
  4. Check with the general formula: $\dfrac{5\cdot 4 - 6 \cdot 3}{6 \cdot 4} = \dfrac{2}{24} = \dfrac{1}{12}$. Same number, different name. $\blacksquare$

1.6 Multiplicative inverses

Among the rationals, something new is possible: every number except $0$ has a multiplicative inverse, a number that multiplies with it to give $1$.

$$\frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{n}{m} = \frac{mn}{nm} = 1 \qquad (m \neq 0).$$

Write the inverse of $a$ as $a^{-1}$ or $\frac{1}{a}$. Just as subtraction was defined through opposites, division is defined through inverses:

$$\frac{a}{b} = a \cdot b^{-1} \qquad (b \neq 0).$$

So there are really only two operations, addition and multiplication, each with a way of undoing it.

Why you cannot divide by zero

Dividing by $0$ would mean multiplying by a number $z$ with $0 \cdot z = 1$. But Example 2 showed $0 \cdot z = 0$ for every number $z$, and $0 \neq 1$. So no such $z$ exists — not because dividing by zero is forbidden by decree, but because the thing it would have to name does not exist. Keeping $0 \cdot z = 0$ and inventing $\frac{1}{0}$ are incompatible; we keep the rule that does useful work.

Watch out

The inverse rule for multiplication has no analogue for addition inside a fraction: $\dfrac{1}{a + b}$ is not $\dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b}$. Test it with $a = b = 1$: the left side is $\frac12$, the right side is $2$. One counterexample is enough to kill a proposed rule forever — see the interlude.

Wrong turn. Splitting a denominator

Compute $\dfrac{1}{2+3}$.

  1. The tempting move. Split the denominator across the sum: $\dfrac{1}{2+3} = \dfrac12 + \dfrac13 = \dfrac36 + \dfrac26 = \dfrac56$.
  2. The check. Making a denominator larger makes a fraction smaller, so $\dfrac{1}{2+3}$ has to come out below $\dfrac12$. But $\dfrac56$ is above $\dfrac12$. The answer is not merely wrong, it is on the wrong side of a bound we can see without computing anything.
  3. The repair. There is nothing to split: $2+3 = 5$, so $\dfrac{1}{2+3} = \dfrac15$ — comfortably below $\dfrac12$, as required. $\blacksquare$

In letters, the sum of the two inverses is $\dfrac1a + \dfrac1b = \dfrac{b}{ab} + \dfrac{a}{ab} = \dfrac{a+b}{ab}$. Notice where $a+b$ ends up: on top. The invented rule would have put it underneath, which is the clearest sign that the two expressions were never going to agree.

Example 8. A fraction divided by a fraction

Compute $\dfrac{2/3}{4/9}$.

  1. Division means multiplication by the inverse: $\dfrac{2}{3} \cdot \left(\dfrac{4}{9}\right)^{-1}$.
  2. The inverse of $\dfrac{4}{9}$ is $\dfrac{9}{4}$, since $\dfrac{4}{9}\cdot\dfrac{9}{4} = \dfrac{36}{36} = 1$.
  3. Multiply: $\dfrac{2}{3}\cdot\dfrac{9}{4} = \dfrac{18}{12}$.
  4. Same number in lowest terms: $\dfrac{18}{12} = \dfrac{3}{2}$. $\blacksquare$

"Invert and multiply" is not a separate rule either. It is the definition of division plus the observation that inverting a fraction flips it.

Practice

Work each one on paper first. Answers may be typed in any equivalent form — 3/2, 1.5 and 6/4 are all accepted.

Compute $-12 - (-5)$.

Answer: $-7$

By definition, $-12 - (-5) = -12 + \big(-(-5)\big)$, and $-(-5) = 5$ by Example 1. So the sum is $-12 + 5 = -7$.

Compute $(-2)(3)(-1)(-5)$.

Answer: $-30$

The absolute sizes multiply to $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 30$. There are three negative factors; each pair of negatives gives a positive by Example 4, so one negative remains: the product is $-30$. In general an odd number of negative factors gives a negative product, an even number gives a positive one.

Expand $(a + b)(c + d)$ using distributivity only.

Answer: $ac + ad + bc + bd$

Treat $(c+d)$ as one object $x$. Then $(a+b)x = ax + bx$ by distributivity. Now put $x = c + d$ back and distribute each piece: $a(c+d) = ac + ad$ and $b(c+d) = bc + bd$. Adding gives $ac + ad + bc + bd$. Every step was one application of the same rule — this is all that "multiply out the brackets" ever means.

Compute $\dfrac{7}{4} + \dfrac{5}{6} - \dfrac{7}{12}$.

Answer: $2$

Use $12$ as the common denominator, since $4$ and $6$ both divide it: $\frac{7}{4} = \frac{21}{12}$ and $\frac{5}{6} = \frac{10}{12}$. Now all three pieces are the same size, so the numerators simply add: $$\frac{21}{12} + \frac{10}{12} - \frac{7}{12} = \frac{21 + 10 - 7}{12} = \frac{24}{12} = 2.$$ A whole number is a perfectly good rational number: $2 = \frac{2}{1}$.

Compute $\dfrac{10}{9} \div \dfrac{4}{3}$.

Answer: $\dfrac{5}{6}$

Division is multiplication by the inverse, and the inverse of $\frac43$ is $\frac34$: $$\frac{10}{9}\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \frac{30}{36} = \frac{5}{6},$$ cancelling the common factor $6$ from numerator and denominator — which changes the name of the number, not the number.

Let $m$ and $n$ be even, say $m = 2j$ and $n = 2k$. Write $m + n$ as $2$ times an integer — that is, fill in the blank: $m + n = \underline{\qquad}$.

Answer: $2(j+k)$

$m + n = 2j + 2k = 2(j + k)$ by distributivity, and $j + k$ is an integer, so $m + n$ is even. This is the whole proof that the sum of two even integers is even: name the witnesses, add, factor.

Which of these statements is false?

  • The product of two odd integers is odd.
  • The sum of an even integer and an odd integer is even.
  • If $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd.
  • The product of an even integer with any integer is even.

Answer: the second statement

Even plus odd is odd: if $m = 2j$ and $n = 2k+1$ then $m + n = 2(j+k) + 1$. A single counterexample settles it too: $2 + 3 = 5$.

The others are true. Odd times odd: $(2j+1)(2k+1) = 2(2jk + j + k) + 1$. Even times anything: $2j \cdot n = 2(jn)$. And $n$ odd $\Rightarrow n^2$ odd is Example 6.

Compute $\big(7 + (-7)\big)\big(19^{4} - 8\big)$ without expanding the second bracket.

Answer: $0$

The first bracket is $7 + (-7) = 0$ by the rule of opposites, and $0 \cdot x = 0$ for every $x$ by Example 2. So the product is $0$ whatever the second bracket happens to be. Recognising a zero factor beats computing $19^4$.

Which integers $a$ have a multiplicative inverse that is also an integer? List them, separated by commas.

Answer: $1$ and $-1$

$1 \cdot 1 = 1$ and $(-1)(-1) = 1$, so both work. For any other integer $a$, the inverse would be $\frac1a$, a number strictly between $-1$ and $1$ and not equal to $0$ — and there is no such integer. This is exactly the shortcoming that forces us to build the rational numbers: inside the integers, division almost never works.

Let $n$ be an odd integer, $n = 2k + 1$. Write the multiplicative inverse of $n$ in terms of $k$.

Answer: $\dfrac{1}{2k+1}$

The inverse of $n$ is $\frac1n$, and substituting $n = 2k+1$ gives $\frac{1}{2k+1}$. Note that no odd $n$ is ever $0$, so the inverse always exists — the only integer excluded from having an inverse is $0$, which is even.

Recap

  • Four rules for addition, four for multiplication. Subtraction is addition of the opposite; division is multiplication by the inverse.
  • Distributivity is the only rule linking the two operations, and it forces the rules of signs: $(-a)b = -(ab)$ and $(-a)(-b) = ab$.
  • $a \cdot 0 = 0$ for every $a$ — which is precisely why nothing can be divided by $0$.
  • To prove a statement about all even or all odd integers, name the witness ($n = 2k$ or $n = 2k+1$), compute, and factor.
  • A fraction is one number with many names; equality is the test $mq = np$, and the addition formula is just the common-denominator calculation written down.
