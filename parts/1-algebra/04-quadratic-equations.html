Part I — Algebra · Chapter 4

Quadratic Equations

There is only one technique in this chapter — completing the square. The quadratic formula is what you get by doing it once, in general, and then never having to do it again.

By the end of this chapter you can solve $x^2 = c$ and factored equations, complete the square on any quadratic, derive the quadratic formula rather than recall it, use the discriminant to predict the number of real roots before solving, and locate the vertex and axis of symmetry of a parabola.

4.1 Equations you can already solve

Two special shapes are easy, and the whole chapter consists of reducing the general case to the first of them.

Two easy shapes
  1. $x^2 = c$. If $c > 0$ there are two solutions, $x = \sqrt c$ and $x = -\sqrt c$ (written $x = \pm\sqrt c$). If $c = 0$ the only solution is $x = 0$. If $c < 0$ there is no real solution, since squares are never negative (Chapter 3).
  2. A product equal to zero. If $uv = 0$ then $u = 0$ or $v = 0$.

The second deserves a proof, because it is the reason factoring solves equations at all. Suppose $uv = 0$ and $u \neq 0$. Then $u^{-1}$ exists, and

$$v = 1 \cdot v = (u^{-1}u)v = u^{-1}(uv) = u^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0.$$

So if the first factor is not zero, the second must be. Note where this came from: the existence of inverses, and $a \cdot 0 = 0$ from Chapter 1.

Watch out

The zero-product property is special to zero. From $uv = 6$ you may not conclude that $u = 6$ or $v = 6$ — try $u = 2$, $v = 3$. So always move everything to one side and set it equal to $0$ before factoring.

Example 1. Solving by factoring

Solve $x^2 + 2x = 15$.

  1. Move everything to one side: $x^2 + 2x - 15 = 0$.
  2. Factor: look for two numbers with product $-15$ and sum $2$ — namely $5$ and $-3$. So $(x + 5)(x - 3) = 0$.
  3. By the zero-product property, $x + 5 = 0$ or $x - 3 = 0$.
  4. So $x = -5$ or $x = 3$. Check both in the original: $25 - 10 = 15$ ✓ and $9 + 6 = 15$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Factoring is fast when it works. The next section is what to do when it does not — which is most of the time.

4.2 Completing the square

The obstacle to solving $x^2 + bx + c = 0$ directly is the middle term: if it were absent we could take square roots at once. Completing the square removes it, by recognising a perfect square hiding in the expression:

$$\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^{2} = x^{2} + bx + \frac{b^{2}}{4}.$$

The left side has the same $x^2$ and $bx$ as our equation; only the constant differs. So subtract the surplus:

$$x^{2} + bx = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^{2} - \frac{b^{2}}{4}.$$

That identity is the entire chapter. It converts any quadratic into "a perfect square, plus a constant" — and perfect squares we can undo.

Example 2. Completing the square, step by step

Solve $x^2 - 6x + 2 = 0$.

  1. Half the coefficient of $x$ is $-3$, and $(x-3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9$. Always: halve the middle coefficient, then square it.
  2. So $x^2 - 6x = (x-3)^2 - 9$, and the equation becomes $(x - 3)^2 - 9 + 2 = 0$, i.e. $(x-3)^2 = 7$.
  3. Take square roots — both of them: $x - 3 = \pm\sqrt7$.
  4. So $x = 3 + \sqrt7$ or $x = 3 - \sqrt7$. Numerically, about $5.646$ and $0.354$.
  5. Check the sum and product against the equation: the two roots add to $6$ (matching $-b$) and multiply to $9 - 7 = 2$ (matching $c$) ✓. $\blacksquare$

When the leading coefficient is not $1$, divide through by it first — legal, since $a \neq 0$ for a quadratic.

4.3 The quadratic formula

Now do Example 2 once with letters instead of numbers, and you never have to complete the square again.

Example 3. Deriving the formula

Solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, with $a \neq 0$.

  1. Divide by $a$: $\;x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x + \dfrac{c}{a} = 0$.
  2. Complete the square on the first two terms: $$x^{2} + \frac{b}{a}x = \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^{2} - \frac{b^{2}}{4a^{2}}.$$
  3. Substitute and move the constants to the right: $$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^{2} = \frac{b^{2}}{4a^{2}} - \frac{c}{a} = \frac{b^{2} - 4ac}{4a^{2}}.$$
  4. Take square roots: $$x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^{2} - 4ac}}{2a}.$$ Valid when $b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$; note $\sqrt{4a^2} = |2a|$, and the $\pm$ absorbs the sign.
  5. Subtract $\dfrac{b}{2a}$: $$\boxed{\;x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^{2} - 4ac}}{2a}\;}$$ $\blacksquare$
Read the formula, don't just use it

The formula says the roots sit symmetrically on either side of $-\dfrac{b}{2a}$, at a distance $\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ from it. That centre point and that spread are the two facts worth remembering; everything else follows.

4.4 The discriminant

The quantity under the root controls everything:

$$\Delta = b^{2} - 4ac.$$
What $\Delta$ tells you
  • $\Delta > 0$: two distinct real roots — the graph crosses the $x$-axis twice.
  • $\Delta = 0$: one (double) root, $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$ — the graph touches the axis.
  • $\Delta < 0$: no real root — the graph misses the axis entirely.

You can therefore answer "how many solutions?" without solving anything. And the third case is not a dead end: in Chapter 14 we build numbers in which $\Delta < 0$ has square roots too, and every quadratic gets its two roots back.

Example 4. Counting roots without solving

How many real solutions does $2x^2 - 4x + 5 = 0$ have?

  1. Here $a = 2$, $b = -4$, $c = 5$.
  2. $\Delta = (-4)^2 - 4(2)(5) = 16 - 40 = -24$.
  3. $\Delta < 0$, so there is no real solution. $\blacksquare$

Sanity check by completing the square: $2x^2 - 4x + 5 = 2(x-1)^2 + 3$, which is at least $3$ for every real $x$ — so it is never $0$.

4.5 Vertex, symmetry, and the graph

Completing the square on $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ rewrites it as

$$y = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^{2} + \left(c - \frac{b^{2}}{4a}\right).$$

In this form the graph is transparent. The squared term is $0$ at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$ and positive everywhere else, so:

  • If $a > 0$ the curve opens upward and has its lowest point at $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$; if $a < 0$ it opens downward and has its highest point there. This point is the vertex.
  • Since $\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ depends only on the distance from $x$ to $-\frac{b}{2a}$, points equally far to the left and right give the same $y$: the curve is symmetric about the vertical line $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$.
  • The two roots, when they exist, are that same distance either side of the vertex — which is what the $\pm$ in the formula was saying all along.

Example 5. Vertex and range

Find the vertex of $y = 3x^2 + 12x + 7$ and the smallest value $y$ can take.

  1. Vertex at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = -\dfrac{12}{6} = -2$.
  2. Its height: $y = 3(4) + 12(-2) + 7 = 12 - 24 + 7 = -5$.
  3. Since $a = 3 > 0$ the parabola opens upward, so this is the minimum: $y \ge -5$ for every $x$, with equality only at $x = -2$.
  4. Confirm by completing the square: $3x^2 + 12x + 7 = 3(x+2)^2 - 5$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

Practice

Where an exercise asks for several roots, separate them with a comma in any order.

Solve $x^2 + 5x = 14$.

Answer: $x = 2$ and $x = -7$

Rearrange to $x^2 + 5x - 14 = 0$. Two numbers with product $-14$ and sum $5$ are $7$ and $-2$, so $(x + 7)(x - 2) = 0$, giving $x = -7$ or $x = 2$.

Check: $4 + 10 = 14$ ✓ and $49 - 35 = 14$ ✓.

Complete the square: $x^2 - 8x + 3 = (x - 4)^2 + k$. What is $k$?

Answer: $k = -13$

$(x-4)^2 = x^2 - 8x + 16$, so $x^2 - 8x = (x-4)^2 - 16$, and therefore $$x^2 - 8x + 3 = (x-4)^2 - 16 + 3 = (x-4)^2 - 13.$$ So $k = -13$.

Compute the discriminant of $3x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0$.

Answer: $\Delta = -8$

$\Delta = 2^2 - 4(3)(1) = 4 - 12 = -8$. Being negative, it tells us this equation has no real solution — the parabola $y = 3x^2 + 2x + 1$ stays entirely above the $x$-axis.

How many real roots does $x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0$ have?

  • None.
  • Two distinct roots.
  • Exactly one (a double root).
  • Infinitely many.

Answer: exactly one

$\Delta = 36 - 36 = 0$, so there is a single double root at $x = -\frac{b}{2a} = 3$. Indeed $x^2 - 6x + 9 = (x-3)^2$, which is zero only at $x = 3$: the parabola touches the $x$-axis at its vertex instead of crossing.

Solve $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$ and give both roots to two decimal places.

Answer: $x \approx 2.62$ and $x \approx 0.38$

$\Delta = 9 - 4 = 5$, so $$x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt5}{2}.$$ With $\sqrt5 \approx 2.236$: $x \approx \frac{5.236}{2} \approx 2.618$ and $x \approx \frac{0.764}{2} \approx 0.382$.

Check the centre: the two roots average to $1.5 = -\frac{b}{2a}$ ✓, as the formula guarantees.

At which $x$ does $y = -2x^2 + 8x - 1$ take its largest value?

Answer: $x = 2$

The vertex is at $x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{8}{-4} = 2$. Since $a = -2 < 0$ the parabola opens downward, so the vertex is the highest point. Its height is $-2(4) + 16 - 1 = 7$, so $y \le 7$ for every $x$.

For which values of $k$ does $x^2 + kx + 4 = 0$ have exactly one (double) root? Give one such value.

Answer: $k = 4$ or $k = -4$

A double root means $\Delta = 0$: here $\Delta = k^2 - 16$, so $k^2 = 16$ and $k = \pm 4$.

Sense check: with $k = 4$ the equation is $x^2 + 4x + 4 = (x+2)^2 = 0$, with the double root $x = -2$; with $k = -4$ it is $(x-2)^2 = 0$.

Solve $2x^2 + 10x = 0$.

Answer: $x = 0$ and $x = -5$

Factor: $2x(x + 5) = 0$. By the zero-product property, $2x = 0$ or $x + 5 = 0$, so $x = 0$ or $x = -5$.

Dividing both sides by $x$ instead would have lost the root $x = 0$ — and dividing by something that might be zero is exactly the move Chapter 2 warned about.

A rectangle has perimeter $26$ and area $40$. What is the length of its longer side?

Answer: $8$

Let the sides be $x$ and $y$. Then $2(x + y) = 26$, so $y = 13 - x$, and $xy = 40$ gives $$x(13 - x) = 40 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 - 13x + 40 = 0.$$ Factoring: $(x - 5)(x - 8) = 0$, so the sides are $5$ and $8$. The longer side is $8$.

Check: perimeter $2(5+8) = 26$ ✓, area $40$ ✓.

A parabola $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ has $a > 0$ and no real root. What follows?

  • $y > 0$ for every real $x$.
  • $y < 0$ for every real $x$.
  • $y = 0$ at the vertex.
  • Nothing follows without knowing $b$.

Answer: $y > 0$ for every real $x$

Opening upward ($a > 0$) the curve has a minimum at the vertex. Having no real root means it never meets the $x$-axis, so that minimum is strictly above it — and since the vertex is the lowest point, the whole curve is above the axis.

With $a < 0$ and no real root the same reasoning gives $y < 0$ everywhere.

Recap

  • $x^2 = c$ has two roots, one, or none according to the sign of $c$; and $uv = 0$ forces $u = 0$ or $v = 0$ — which is why factoring works.
  • $x^2 + bx = \left(x + \frac b2\right)^2 - \frac{b^2}{4}$ turns any quadratic into a perfect square plus a constant.
  • Doing that in general gives $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ — a derivation, not a spell.
  • $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ counts the real roots: two, one, or none.
  • The vertex is at $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$, the parabola is symmetric about that line, and the roots sit equally far either side of it.
