Part I — Algebra · Chapter 3

Real Numbers

The fractions leave gaps in the number line. Filling them gives the real numbers — and with them, order, inequalities, distance, and roots.

By the end of this chapter you can prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, state the positivity rules and derive the rules for inequalities from them (including the one that flips), read $|x - a|$ as a distance and solve inequalities involving it, and compute confidently with integer, negative, and fractional exponents.
Before you start

You should be comfortable with the rules of arithmetic and with the even/odd proofs of Chapter 1 — the proof that $\sqrt2$ is not a fraction, a few lines below, is written in exactly that style.

3.1 Why the fractions are not enough

Between any two fractions there is another — take their average. So the rationals are packed everywhere on the line, with no visible gaps. It is startling that gaps exist at all.

But they do. Draw a square with side $1$; by the Pythagorean theorem (which we prove in Chapter 5) its diagonal has length $d$ with $d^2 = 2$. That length is a perfectly definite point on the line, and it is not a fraction.

Example 1. $\sqrt{2}$ is not a rational number

Suppose it were. We show that assumption leads to a contradiction, so it must be false.

  1. Suppose $\sqrt2 = \dfrac{m}{n}$ with $m, n$ integers, $n \neq 0$, and the fraction in lowest terms — any fraction can be put in lowest terms by cancelling common factors, so this costs nothing.
  2. Square both sides: $2 = \dfrac{m^2}{n^2}$, hence $m^2 = 2n^2$.
  3. So $m^2$ is even. By Example 6 of Chapter 1, $m$ is then even too. Write $m = 2k$.
  4. Substitute: $(2k)^2 = 2n^2$, so $4k^2 = 2n^2$, so $n^2 = 2k^2$.
  5. By the same lemma, $n$ is even.
  6. But now $m$ and $n$ are both even — they share the factor $2$, contradicting "lowest terms". The supposition in step 1 is impossible, so $\sqrt2$ is not a fraction. $\blacksquare$
What just happened

This is proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want, derive an impossibility, conclude the assumption was false. Note how little it needed — the definition of "even", and the lemma from Chapter 1. Note also what it does not do: it gives no decimal expansion for $\sqrt2$, and does not need one.

The real numbers are what you get by filling every such gap: the complete number line, with one number for every point. Rationals with terminating or repeating decimal expansions sit inside it; numbers like $\sqrt2$ and $\pi$, with non-repeating expansions, are the new arrivals, called irrational.

3.2 Addition, multiplication, and positivity

All eight rules from Chapter 1 hold for real numbers, plus the inverse rule: every $a \neq 0$ has $a^{-1}$ with $a a^{-1} = 1$. Everything proved in Chapter 1 from those rules therefore holds here unchanged — $(-a)(-b) = ab$, $a\cdot 0 = 0$, and the rest. That is the payoff of proving things from a short list.

What is genuinely new is order, and it rests on a single idea: some numbers are positive.

Positivity rules

There is a set of positive numbers such that:

  1. Trichotomy. For every real $a$, exactly one holds: $a$ is positive, $a = 0$, or $-a$ is positive.
  2. Closure under addition. If $a, b$ are positive, so is $a + b$.
  3. Closure under multiplication. If $a, b$ are positive, so is $ab$.

Then define $a > b$ to mean "$a - b$ is positive", and $a < b$ to mean $b > a$.

Everything about inequalities follows from those three. Here is the pattern.

Example 2. A square is never negative

Show that $a^2 > 0$ for every $a \neq 0$.

  1. By trichotomy, either $a$ is positive or $-a$ is positive (the case $a = 0$ is excluded).
  2. If $a$ is positive, then $a\cdot a = a^2$ is positive by closure under multiplication.
  3. If $-a$ is positive, then $(-a)(-a)$ is positive by the same rule — and $(-a)(-a) = a^2$ by Chapter 1, Example 4.
  4. Either way $a^2$ is positive. $\blacksquare$

In particular $1 = 1^2$ is positive, so $-1$ is not. And no real number squares to $-1$ — which is exactly the gap that Chapter 14 fills.

3.3 Order and inequalities

Rules for inequalities

Let $a < b$. Then:

  1. $a + c < b + c$ for every $c$ — adding anything preserves an inequality.
  2. $ac < bc$ if $c > 0$ — multiplying by a positive number preserves it.
  3. $ac > bc$ if $c < 0$ — multiplying by a negative number reverses it.
  4. If also $b < d$ then $a < d$ (transitivity).

Rule 3 is the one that catches people, so here is its proof. Suppose $a < b$ and $c < 0$. Then $b - a$ is positive and $-c$ is positive, so their product $(b-a)(-c)$ is positive by closure. Expanding, $(b-a)(-c) = ac - bc$. So $ac - bc$ is positive, which says precisely $ac > bc$. The direction flipped, and it had to.

Example 3. Solving an inequality

Solve $4 - 3x \le 13$.

  1. Add $-4$ to both sides: $-3x \le 9$. Direction unchanged — Rule 1.
  2. Multiply both sides by $-\frac13$. This number is negative, so the inequality reverses: $x \ge -3$.
  3. The solution set is every real number $x$ with $x \ge -3$, written $[-3, \infty)$.
  4. Spot-check the boundary and one point inside: $x = -3$ gives $4 + 9 = 13 \le 13$ ✓; $x = 0$ gives $4 \le 13$ ✓; and $x = -4$ gives $16 \le 13$ ✗, correctly excluded.
Watch out

Never multiply an inequality by an expression whose sign you do not know. From $\frac{1}{x} < 1$ you cannot simply multiply by $x$: if $x > 0$ you get $1 < x$, but if $x < 0$ the inequality reverses and every negative $x$ already works. Split into cases, or rearrange to compare with $0$ instead.

3.4 Absolute value and distance

The absolute value of $a$ is

$$|a| = \begin{cases} a & \text{if } a \ge 0,\\ -a & \text{if } a < 0.\end{cases}$$

It strips the sign: $|5| = 5$ and $|-5| = 5$. Two consequences are worth having permanently in mind:

  • $|a|$ is the distance from $a$ to $0$ on the number line.
  • $|a - b|$ is the distance between $a$ and $b$ — and it does not matter which way round you subtract, since $|a - b| = |b - a|$.

Once you read absolute value as distance, inequalities involving it stop needing algebra at all. The statement $|x - 3| < 2$ says "$x$ is within $2$ of $3$", so $1 < x < 5$ — read straight off the line.

Properties of absolute value
  1. $|a| \ge 0$, with $|a| = 0$ only when $a = 0$.
  2. $|ab| = |a||b|$.
  3. $|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$ — the triangle inequality.

The third is an inequality, not an equation, because $a$ and $b$ may point in opposite directions: with $a = 3$ and $b = -3$ the left side is $0$ and the right side is $6$. Equality holds exactly when $a$ and $b$ have the same sign (or one is zero) — when nothing cancels.

3.5 Powers and roots

For a positive integer $n$, $a^n$ means $a$ multiplied by itself $n$ times. From that definition alone, three laws are immediate — count the factors:

$$a^m a^n = a^{m+n}, \qquad (a^m)^n = a^{mn}, \qquad (ab)^n = a^n b^n.$$

The third comes from regrouping: $(ab)^n$ is $n$ copies of $ab$ multiplied together, and commutativity and associativity let us gather all the $a$'s on one side and all the $b$'s on the other, leaving $a^n b^n$.

We now extend the notation to zero, negative, and fractional exponents. There is only one sensible way to do it: choose the meaning that keeps those three laws true.

Extending the exponent
  • $a^0 = 1$ (for $a \neq 0$), because $a^n a^0 = a^{n+0} = a^n$ forces $a^0$ to be the number that changes nothing under multiplication.
  • $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$, because $a^n a^{-n} = a^0 = 1$ forces $a^{-n}$ to be the inverse of $a^n$.
  • $a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$ (for $a > 0$), because $\big(a^{1/n}\big)^n = a^{n/n} = a^1 = a$ forces $a^{1/n}$ to be the $n$-th root.
  • Hence $a^{m/n} = \big(\sqrt[n]{a}\big)^m$.
  • $\sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a}\,\sqrt[n]{b}$ (for $a, b > 0$), because the third law above must keep holding at the fractional exponent $1/n$ too: $(ab)^{1/n} = a^{1/n} b^{1/n}$. Roots distribute over products for the same reason every other rule in this box holds — it's forced, not chosen.

Nothing here is a convention chosen for tidiness; each is the unique choice compatible with the laws we already had.

Example 4. Simplifying with the laws

Simplify $\dfrac{(8)^{2/3} \cdot 2^{-1}}{2^{2}}$.

  1. $8 = 2^3$, so $8^{2/3} = (2^3)^{2/3} = 2^{3 \cdot 2/3} = 2^2 = 4$. Second law: $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.
  2. The numerator is $2^2 \cdot 2^{-1} = 2^{2 - 1} = 2$.
  3. Divide: $\dfrac{2}{2^2} = 2^{1-2} = 2^{-1} = \dfrac12$. $\blacksquare$
Watch out — two everyday traps

$\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, not $x$. The square root symbol always means the non-negative root, so $\sqrt{(-5)^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 = |-5|$.

$\sqrt{a + b} \neq \sqrt a + \sqrt b$. Roots distribute over products (that's the rule above) but never over sums — there is no such law, and no reason to expect one. Test with $a = b = 9$: the left side is $\sqrt{18} \approx 4.24$, the right side is $6$.

Practice

Compute $|{-3} - 5|$.

Answer: $8$

Inside the bars, $-3 - 5 = -8$, and $|-8| = 8$. Read as distance: the gap between $-3$ and $5$ on the line is $8$ units, and distances are never negative.

Solve $-2x + 1 > 7$.

  • $x > -3$
  • $x > 3$
  • $x < -3$
  • $x < 3$

Answer: $x < -3$

Add $-1$: $-2x > 6$. Now multiply by $-\frac12$, a negative number, so the inequality reverses: $x < -3$.

Check: $x = -4$ gives $8 + 1 = 9 > 7$ ✓; $x = 0$ gives $1 > 7$ ✗.

Solve $|x - 3| = 4$. List all solutions, separated by a comma.

Answer: $x = 7$ and $x = -1$

The equation says $x$ is at distance exactly $4$ from $3$. There are two such points: $3 + 4 = 7$ and $3 - 4 = -1$.

Algebraically: $x - 3 = 4$ or $x - 3 = -4$. An equation with absolute value almost always splits into two cases like this.

Which interval is the solution set of $|x - 2| \le 5$?

  • $[-5, 5]$
  • $[-3, 7]$
  • $[2, 7]$
  • Everything outside $[-3, 7]$

Answer: $[-3, 7]$

"The distance from $x$ to $2$ is at most $5$" means $x$ lies between $2 - 5 = -3$ and $2 + 5 = 7$, endpoints included because the relation is $\le$.

The last option would be the answer to $|x - 2| \ge 5$ — a "far from" statement, which always gives two pieces rather than one interval.

Compute $4^{-3/2}$.

Answer: $\dfrac18$

Take the pieces one at a time: $$4^{-3/2} = \frac{1}{4^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{\big(\sqrt4\big)^{3}} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac18.$$ Any order works — root first or power first — because $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.

Compute $27^{2/3}$.

Answer: $9$

$27 = 3^3$, so $27^{2/3} = (3^3)^{2/3} = 3^{2} = 9$. Equivalently: the cube root of $27$ is $3$, and $3^2 = 9$.

Simplify $\sqrt{x^{2}}$ so that the result is correct for every real $x$.

Answer: $|x|$

The symbol $\sqrt{\;}$ denotes the non-negative root, so $\sqrt{x^2}$ is never negative. For $x = -5$: $\sqrt{(-5)^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, which is $|-5|$, not $-5$. The answer $x$ is right only when $x \ge 0$; $|x|$ is right always.

Which of these statements is false for all real $a, b$?

  • $|ab| = |a|\,|b|$
  • $|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$
  • $|a - b| = |b - a|$
  • $|a + b| = |a| + |b|$

Answer: $|a + b| = |a| + |b|$

It fails whenever $a$ and $b$ have opposite signs: with $a = 3$, $b = -3$ the left side is $0$ and the right side is $6$. The correct general statement is the triangle inequality $|a+b| \le |a| + |b|$, with equality exactly when $a$ and $b$ have the same sign or one of them is $0$.

The other three hold for all real $a$ and $b$.

What is the smallest possible value of $(x + 1)^2 - 4$, and at which $x$ does it occur? Give the value of $x$.

Answer: $x = -1$ (where the expression equals $-4$)

By Example 2, a square is never negative: $(x+1)^2 \ge 0$ for every $x$, so $(x+1)^2 - 4 \ge -4$. The value $-4$ is actually attained when $(x+1)^2 = 0$, that is when $x = -1$.

This "a square is at least zero" argument is the whole idea behind completing the square in Chapter 4.

A student "proves" that $\frac1x < 1$ implies $x > 1$, by multiplying both sides by $x$. Where is the error?

  • $\frac1x < 1$ is never true, so nothing follows.
  • Multiplying by $x$ assumes $x > 0$; if $x < 0$ the inequality reverses, and indeed every negative $x$ satisfies $\frac1x < 1$.
  • The inequality should have been squared instead.
  • $\frac1x$ is undefined, so the statement is meaningless.

Answer: the second option

Multiplying an inequality by $x$ is only safe once you know the sign of $x$. Take $x = -2$: then $\frac1x = -\frac12 < 1$ is true, while $x > 1$ is false — so the claimed implication is wrong as stated.

The honest solution splits into cases: for $x > 0$, multiplying gives $1 < x$; for $x < 0$, the left side is negative and the inequality holds automatically. The full solution set is $x < 0$ or $x > 1$.

Recap

  • $\sqrt2$ is not a fraction — proved by contradiction from the even/odd lemma. The reals fill the gaps the rationals leave.
  • Order comes from three positivity rules. Everything about inequalities is derived from them, including the reversal when you multiply by a negative number.
  • $a^2 > 0$ for $a \neq 0$: no real number squares to a negative.
  • $|a - b|$ is distance. Read absolute-value inequalities as "near" ($<$, one interval) or "far" ($>$, two pieces).
  • Zero, negative, and fractional exponents are defined by the requirement that $a^ma^n = a^{m+n}$ and $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ keep working.
