Before you start

How to use this course

Read slowly, write constantly, and do not move on from a sentence you cannot justify. That is the whole method.

Read with a pencil

Mathematics is not readable at the speed of prose. Every time a line of algebra appears, work it out yourself on paper before reading the next line. If the site says $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, multiply it out. The habit feels slow for a week and then becomes the reason you understand anything.

When a chapter states a rule, try to see why it must hold before reading the justification. Even a failed attempt makes the explanation land, because you already know where the difficulty is.

The exercises are the course

Each chapter ends with practice problems that check themselves. A few notes on them:

  • Answers are matched forgivingly. For a number, 0.5, 1/2 and 2/4 are all accepted; spaces never matter; -3 and −3 (unicode minus) are the same.
  • When an exercise wants several numbers, separate them with commas in any order: 2,-3 is the same as -3, 2.
  • Every problem has a full worked solution, not just an answer. The solution opens by itself once your answer is right, so compare your route with the one shown; opening it before you answer is still your choice.
  • Stuck? Every problem has a Show a clue button from the start, which says how many clues there are. The first only says where to look; the next say more; the worked solution comes last and is always there. A clue opens only when you ask for it, and the clues you opened before solving the problem stay open when you come back.
  • Getting one wrong is information, not failure. When a wrong answer looks like a common slip (a sign flipped, a fraction upside down, the decimal point moved, a value left out) the card asks you about it, without giving the answer away.

What a chapter asks you to do

A chapter is not meant to be read straight through and then tested. It keeps stopping to ask for something:

  • One step at a time. A chapter opens a piece at a time, with a Continue button under each piece. Where a piece ends in a question, answer it to go on — or skip it, and the course will remember to bring you back to that section. The switch under the chapter title turns this off and shows the whole page at once.
  • A puzzle at the top. Commit to a guess; the recap comes back to it. Guessing wrongly first is useful — it shows you what the chapter is for.
  • A warm-up of three questions from earlier chapters. They are not scored. If one doesn't come to you, its solution links to the section to reread before going on.
  • Your turn, after most worked examples: one problem of the same kind, to do before the method fades. Also not scored.
  • Examples with the later steps closed. Do each step on paper, then open it to compare. Likewise the boxes that ask you to predict a result before it is derived.
  • Section checks that are not typed: lines of an argument to put in order, blanks to fill inside an equation, several options to check, or a figure to set so that it shows the answer. The lines can be dragged or moved with their arrow buttons.
  • Missions under each interactive figure: things to make the figure do. A star marks each one you manage, and they are counted on the contents page.

Only the practice set and the end-of-Part reviews count toward a chapter's progress ring. There, as everywhere, a wrong answer opens nothing by itself: it says a clue is there if you want one, and once every clue is open it suggests the solution.

Order matters

Chapters are written to depend only on earlier ones. Skipping ahead mostly works inside a part, but Part III genuinely needs Part I, and the trigonometry chapter needs the geometry before it. If a chapter feels impossible, the problem is usually two chapters back.

Relation to Serge Lang's book

The course follows the topic sequence of Serge Lang's Basic Mathematics: the same four parts, the same chapters, in the same order. That sequence is the pedagogical heart of the book — it starts from the rules of arithmetic and refuses to use anything it has not established. The scope-and-sequence page lists every section by the course it is taught in, with the Common Core standard and the gate-exam topics each one covers.

The writing here is independent: all explanations, figures, worked examples, and exercises were written for this site. If you own the book, the best use of this site is as a second voice — read a chapter there, then work the same ideas here, or the reverse. Nothing here reproduces the book's text or its problem sets.

XP, streaks, and what to review

Correct answers and completed missions earn XP — most for an answer that is right first time, less after a miss, least once the solution has been opened. A clue never costs anything, and no help pays more than working it out: the first clue changes nothing, an answer right the first time after the second or third clue earns what an answer right after a miss does, and an answer given with the solution open earns less than that. The header shows your level, today's XP against a daily goal, and a streak of the days in a row you have done something. They are there to make a little work every day feel worth doing, which is what actually builds the skill. Every road to a right answer pays something; the combo below adds a little for accuracy, never for speed. In the Arena, the XP from one section falls the more you come back to it in a day — once earlier runs have paid two answers from it, its answers count half, and after four, a quarter — and the bonus for finishing a run is paid in full twice a day, then 1. Your first run of a day pays in full however its questions fall. The next day it is all back: practice spread over days teaches more than the same practice piled into one, and the result screen says when this has happened and why.

The contents page and the top of each chapter show your next steps, up to three, each with the reason it is there: first the sections due for a check that the Arena can ask about, then the section your answers found hardest, then where you left off. It earns nothing by itself, stays in Study mode, and Hide for today puts it away until tomorrow.

More useful is your progress page. The course notes how each question went — how many tries it took, whether the solution was opened first — and from that lists the sections worth rereading, weakest first. A short version of the same note appears above each chapter's recap.

The game, and why it is built this way

The course plays like a game, but every rule is there to help the mathematics stick. Where the two pull apart, the mathematics wins.

  • Combo. Each answer that is right the first time fills one of five pips, and a full meter adds up to double XP. A first miss on a practice problem costs two pips, and so does answering one with its solution open, so reading the solution first never leaves you better off than trying and missing. Asking for a clue never costs a pip, opening the solution costs nothing until you answer with it open, and an answer right the first time after the second or third clue leaves the meter as it was. Once you are right the solution opens by itself; keeping it in view for a few seconds while you compare your route with the one shown earns a shield that absorbs your next miss — that comparison is where most of the learning happens.
  • Encounters. Each chapter's practice set is an encounter with a boss: the tempting wrong idea behind the chapter's opening puzzle. Every problem you solve wears it down, in any order. You start with three hearts and lose one for each problem you get wrong; asking for a clue or opening the solution never costs a heart. The medal — gold, silver or bronze — counts the problems you got wrong and the ones you solved with the solution open, so reading the solution first is never worth more than a miss, and the medal decides nothing else. Running out of hearts never locks anything; the course just points you at the section worth rereading.
  • Playable figures. Some questions are answered by building something in a 3D scene: three planes that share a line, a slanted solid of volume 12, a box six pyramids fill exactly. While the question is open the scene shows quantities but never says whether you are right — only Check does — so the answer has to come from thinking, not from wiggling until a light turns green.
  • The Arena is the only place with a clock, and it draws only from sections you have already solved, because speed practice helps with what you know and hurts with what you are still learning. The problems are freshly generated each time. A wrong answer costs a heart and stops the clock while you read the hint; "I don't know" costs nothing, so guessing never pays. Its Due review asks only about the sections due for a check today, most overdue first, at most two questions from each and ten in all, and none of them costs a heart; a section answered right goes further into its schedule, and one missed comes back sooner. A section is first due a day after you last solved something in it, not straight after you learned it. Sections the Arena has no problems for yet are listed there as due on their page, with a link. After a run you see how many answers were right the first time and what share, as information: there is no score you are meant to reach. The points a run shows are its score for the Daily board and your Best; XP is counted separately.
  • Levels and achievements come from the XP you have earned. The achievements reward study habits — getting things right the first time, going back to a weak section and fixing it, reading solutions you got right, finishing a review set — and none rewards speed alone. Your form, the figure on the course map and your progress page, grows through five stages as your level rises; a stage reached is yours to keep.

Settings

The menu button at the right of the header opens the settings, with links to every part of the course. Every setting is remembered on this device only:

  • Study mode keeps hints, reviews and progress, and removes hearts, the combo, the bosses, motion and sound; it also makes the Arena untimed by default. It is for when you want the course without the game. Your XP, level, streak, clues, the questions about a wrong answer, solutions, the due review and your next steps are unchanged.
  • Sound is off until you turn it on, and Volume sets how loud it plays.
  • Reduce motion stops every animation, the fade from one page to the next and smooth scroll. It is always on in Study mode and when your device asks for less motion, and the switch then shows it on.
  • Reduce transparency makes the see-through header and the dimmed backdrop solid. Your device's own setting for less transparency does the same, and the switch then shows it on.
  • Theme is light, dark, or whatever your system is set to, which is how it starts. Reading panel chooses the paper the text sits on (see below).
  • Graphics quality is how much the course world on the contents page draws: Low (the fewest details and no motion), Medium or High. Auto picks Low or Medium for your device, and steps down by itself, remembering it on this device, if the world cannot be drawn smoothly.
  • 3D course map opens the contents page on a world of four regions, one per Part. The list of chapters is always there below it, and it is the same course in words. Switch the map off to have the list alone; it also steps aside on its own on devices that cannot draw it at all, or not smoothly even at Low. Moving through the list with the keyboard moves the map to the chapter you are on, ready for when you scroll back up.

Progress and privacy

Everything is saved in this browser's local storage: solved exercises (bm.progress.v1), missions and puzzle guesses (bm.play.v1), how each question went (bm.attempts.v1), XP per day (bm.activity.v1), your place in each chapter (bm.lesson.v1, bm.last), achievements, medals, Arena records and your form (bm.game.v1), the combo, any unfinished Arena run or placement check (with how long each answer took, kept in this browser only and deleted when the check ends), the day's Arena practice per section and whether you hid your next steps today (bm.run.v1), your play settings (bm.prefs.v1), whether you have seen the short guide to the course (bm.guide.v1), your learning log (bm.events.v1: for each exercise check, which one, when to the minute, whether it was right, the try number and the clue level; for each Arena answer, the section, the day and whether it was right the first time; never what you typed), the ids of entries not yet copied to your account (bm.events.out.v1) and what identifies this browser to the log (bm.device.v1: an id for this browser and, once you sign in, the learner's id, which is your account's id for your own progress; the id is drawn afresh, and the learner's id dropped, when you reset or sign out). Without an account nothing is sent anywhere, so progress does not follow you to another browser, and clearing site data clears it. If storage is blocked, everything still works — it just is not remembered.

Your placement check results (bm.diag.v1): the class you said you finished last, the day you finished, which questions were asked, whether each was answered right, whether many answers came very fast, and the suggested course. Never what you typed, and never how long an answer took. "Reset all progress" keeps it. If you're not signed in, it is only in this browser, and clearing this browser's data removes it. If you're signed in, it is also copied to your account, where the course's author can see it like the other stored records; it stays there until you delete your account on the account page. Neither "Reset all progress" nor clearing this browser's data removes the account's copy, and the next sign-in brings it back. Review boxes in bm.game.v1 may be started from it. "Reset all progress" clears those boxes but keeps the results they were started from, so the course may start review schedules from them again.

Where the course is served from: Cloudflare Pages, at learn.groundupmath.org. Cloudflare sees the requests your browser makes for the pages, as every web host does, and never sees your progress. The course's old address, sophanasok.github.io/basic-mathematics, only sends you on to the same page here: it runs none of the course, and progress a browser saved there stays in that browser.

An account is optional. Signed in, the same records are copied to the course's database so that they follow you between devices. They include a short log of your answers (bm.events.v1). For each check on a page: which exercise and section, right or wrong, which try, how many clues were open and whether the solution was, and the minute it happened, so the time between your steps can be seen to the minute. For each Arena answer: which kind of question and section, right or wrong, and the day, with no time of day. Never what you typed. Only what the course uses is kept: for each exercise its first few checks and the one that solved it, and for each section's Arena practice its recent days plus a summary of the rest. Each entry carries a random code for this browser, which changes when you sign out or reset, so the database can tell how many browsers an account used but cannot link two accounts. The database also notes the day each entry reached it. The course's author can see the stored records, which is how questions that are too hard get found and fixed; the course's statistics use only totals across readers. Resetting your progress deletes the log; you can download or delete everything from the account page.

A parent's account can also hold learners: profiles with a name the parent chose and no sign-in of their own. For each one the database keeps the same records as an account's (the solved exercises, the attempts, XP per day, the place in each chapter, the game record and the short answer log, with the same limits and none of what was typed), plus the name, whether the learner is under 13 and, if so, which version of the consent statement was agreed to and when. A parent can add up to eight. On the account page the parent switches to a learner to see their progress, and can download or delete it there; deleting a learner deletes their records, and deleting the account deletes the learners with it. The course's author sees a learner's records as any reader's, under a random id, and never the name. A learner is never on the Daily board. When a parent adds an under-13 learner, the account page shows this statement, headed “What is kept about this learner”, which is the full account of what is kept: “The name you give here, that they are under 13, the exercises they solve, how many tries each took, whether a hint or the solution was used, missions, XP per day, where they are in each chapter, their game record, and a short log of their answers (which exercise and section, right or wrong, which try, how many clues were open, and the minute; never what they typed). It is used only to run the course and to show you their progress. It is never sold or shared beyond the hosts named on the about page. It is kept until you delete the learner or your account. You can download or delete it from this page at any time.”

The account service and its database are run by Supabase, a hosting company, and the course's author can see the stored records. You can sign in with an email address and a password or, where the account page offers it, with an account you already have at another service such as Google or GitHub. In that case you sign in on that service's own page; it passes on details such as your email address, name, username, picture address and your account id there, and it learns that you signed in to this site. The course never sees your password there. While you are signed in that way, this browser also keeps a key from that service, which signing out removes; to cut the link completely, remove this site from the connected apps in that service's settings.

The Daily board is optional: it stays off until you turn on its switch on the account page. Once you are on it, signed-in readers see a name made up for you that changes every day, your score in the Arena's Daily and your time; never your email, your display name or anything you typed. A day's board shows once three people have played. The Daily draws from each player's own deck, so the board compares pace and accuracy on the same day, not the same questions. Your browser reports the score and time, and the server only checks that they are in range, so the board is for fun and nothing in the course depends on it. Turning the switch off removes your rows from it.

Keyboard and accessibility

  • Answer fields accept Enter to check.
  • Single-key shortcuts work whenever you are not typing in a box: j and k (or the arrow keys once the cursor is on an item) move between exercises, n and N between unsolved ones and t to the exercise the chapter's boss is waiting for (or the first unsolved one), Enter or i opens the answer box and Esc leaves it.
  • On a selected exercise: c checks, h shows a clue, s shows or hides the solution, and 1 to 9 pick an option.
  • { and } move between sections, [ and ] between chapters, g then g or G to the first or last exercise, and g then h, a, p, c or s go home, to the Arena, your progress, the contents or the settings.
  • / opens a go-to box: type a few letters of a section, exercise, chapter or page, pick one with the arrow keys and press Enter (on this site it takes the place of Firefox's quick find).
  • ? lists the shortcuts for the page you are on. The Keyboard shortcuts switch in the settings turns them off; Tab, Enter and the figure keys always work. In the Arena, a run keeps its own keys: Enter checks and Esc pauses.
  • Screen readers in browse mode use their own single-letter navigation and never pass those keys to the page, so nothing changes there; every move of the shortcut cursor is also announced.
  • Interactive figures are operable from the keyboard: Tab to a slider or button, then arrow keys. Where a figure has points you can drag, Tab to the figure itself, move the selected point with the arrow keys, and press Space to switch to the next point. Where a figure lets you redirect a connection instead (as in Chapter 13's mapping diagram), Tab to the figure, choose an element with the arrow keys, and press Space or Enter to redirect it. Every figure also states its conclusion in text below it, so nothing is available only by dragging or clicking.
  • The 3D scenes work the same way: Tab to the scene, move the selected handle with the arrow keys (Page Up and Page Down for the third direction), press Space to select the next handle or the view itself, and Home to reset the view. Every scene can also be driven entirely from its sliders and buttons, and where 3D is not available the same scene is drawn flat with the same controls.
  • With a screen reader, formulas in the text are read from their MathML. Where a formula is the label of a button, a choice, a table header, a heading or a link, it is read as a line of text, such as “(7, 5)” or “x ≤ −3”, so every control and heading has a name. Each worked example is a heading one level below its section, so it shows up in a list of headings.
  • On a narrow screen a long formula or a wide table scrolls sideways. While it does, Tab stops on it (as “Formula, scrolls sideways” or “Table, scrolls sideways”) and the arrow keys scroll it.
  • In the Arena, Enter checks and Esc pauses; the Untimed tempo removes the clock altogether.
  • The text, figures and exercises sit on a light paper panel in both themes, because dark text on light paper is the easiest to read for long stretches; the theme shades the dark frame around it. A dark reading panel is a choice in the settings. The theme follows your system setting until you choose Light or Dark there.
  • The settings open beside the header on a wide screen and from the bottom of a phone's screen. Esc closes them and puts you back on the menu button; on a phone, Tab stays inside them until they close.
